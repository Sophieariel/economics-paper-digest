# AI Economics Papers — 2026-07-22

## 1. AI Premium [Firms & Competition]
**Authors:** Nicola Borri, Aleh Tsyvinski, Yukun Liu
**Date:** July 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35451)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35451
**Abstract:** We use 380 trillion tokens of realized AI consumption across more than four hundred large language models to construct the "AI Factor" from growth in tokens, dollars, and users, and estimate firm-level "AI Betas" from stock return comovement with this factor to characterize the "AI Premium." Firms whose returns covary more positively with the AI factor earn substantially higher subsequent returns: a value-weighted long-short strategy sorted on AI Beta generates 64.1 basis points per week. The premium is concentrated in frontier AI uses — closed-source models and paying users — rather than casual, free applications, and extends beyond the technology sector into consumer-facing and capital-intensive industries, but is largely absent in emerging markets, including China. At the occupation level, jobs emphasizing interactive tasks show higher AI exposure, while analytical and operations-control occupations show negative exposure. The results indicate that realized AI usage, rather than mere AI-related announcements or narratives, is priced by financial markets and is heterogeneous across firms, sectors, and countries.

## 2. Delegating to AI: Beliefs and the Organization of Work at New Ventures [Adoption & Diffusion]
**Authors:** Innessa Colaiacovo, Rembrand Koning
**Date:** June 2026 (Harvard Business School Working Paper No. 26-093)
**Source:** https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=69112
**Abstract:** We study how founders of new ventures decide whether and how to delegate work to generative AI. Recruiting founders of North American technology startups, we elicit their beliefs about AI's ability to perform tasks relative to humans and their willingness to delegate tasks to AI, alone or in combination with human labor. Founders report substantial heterogeneity in how AI changes their organization's labor needs: while the sample average is that firms would need 55% more employees absent AI, the median founder estimates only 17%, and nearly a third estimate no additional employees would be needed. Founders who have formally integrated AI into workflows estimate required headcount increases nearly four times higher than informal users, and are more likely to report that AI has changed hiring and management practices. Using a survey experiment that varies information about AI's demonstrated performance on a customer-service task, we show that founders differ in their willingness to delegate to AI even holding constant beliefs about its relative performance, and that this managerial heterogeneity in delegation preferences persists after belief updating. The findings suggest that AI's organizational impact depends not only on what the technology can do, but on managers' choices about how to use it.

## 3. What Investment Data Implies about the AI Transition [Productivity & Growth]
**Authors:** Jessica A. Wachter, Jonathan Wachter
**Date:** June 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35290)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35290
**Abstract:** Major technology firms are undertaking massive capital expenditures related to artificial intelligence — roughly $380 billion in 2025, with plans to nearly double this figure in 2026. We use an open-economy model featuring rare, persistent productivity booms to infer what this investment data implies about the likelihood, timing, and magnitude of a broad AI-driven productivity transition. Calibrating the model so that observed investment behavior is consistent with the model's implied probability of an AI transition, we find that markets are pricing in a scenario in which AI-sector productivity rises by a factor of roughly 2.7, generating cumulative GDP growth of between 5 and 58 percentage points by 2030 and a share of AI in the economy ranging from 8% to 39%. Long-run annual growth under our calibration averages around 7%, with substantial uncertainty bands. The model further implies an increase in the risk-free rate of about 0.5 percentage points and a rise in the equity premium of about 3 percentage points, reflecting the risk associated with the uncertain arrival and magnitude of transformative AI productivity gains.

## 4. Institutional Adaptation and Technological Shock: The Role of AI Regulation in Influencing Startup Financing [Regulation & Policy]
**Authors:** Yunshu Ling, Ke Rong, D. Daniel Sokol, Di Zhou
**Date:** April 12, 2026 (SSRN Working Paper)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6566078
**Abstract:** Technological breakthroughs often trigger periods of market volatility that complicate capital allocation decisions for investors. We examine this stabilization dynamic through two concurrent shocks to China's entrepreneurial finance ecosystem: the rise of generative AI and the subsequent implementation of AI-specific regulation. Using data on venture capital investment before and after these overlapping shocks, we find a counter-intuitive result: rather than constraining the market, the introduction of AI regulation moderated the negative impact of the generative AI shock on venture capital investment. We interpret this as evidence that regulatory clarity can function as a stabilizing institutional mechanism during periods of high technological uncertainty, enabling rather than deterring market participation and capital deployment. The findings speak to a broader debate about whether AI regulation is necessarily a drag on innovation financing, showing instead that well-timed regulatory intervention can reduce uncertainty-driven disruption in venture markets.

## 5. Trade in AI-Related Products [Trade & Welfare]
**Authors:** Michael E. Waugh
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35053)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35053
**Abstract:** This paper documents international trade patterns in products related to artificial intelligence infrastructure. I build a large-language-model-based classification system to categorize detailed (HS10) product codes in U.S. trade data according to their role in AI infrastructure construction and operation — servers, networking equipment, semiconductors, and related capital goods. AI-related products represented 23% of U.S. imports in 2025, having grown 73% since 2023, compared to only 3% growth for non-AI products over the same period. Mexico and Taiwan together account for roughly half of all U.S. trade in these product categories, reflecting concentrated supply chains in AI-related hardware. Trade policy has to date shielded most AI-related imports from tariffs through product-specific exemptions. I estimate that, absent the AI-related expansion in trade, the U.S. goods trade deficit would have been approximately $200 billion lower in 2025, indicating that the AI investment boom is already a first-order driver of observed U.S. external imbalances.
