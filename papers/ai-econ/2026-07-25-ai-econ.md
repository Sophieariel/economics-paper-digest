# AI Economics Papers — 2026-07-25

## 1. The Shift to Agentic AI: Evidence from Codex [Adoption & Diffusion]
**Authors:** Drew Johnston, David Holtz, Alex Martin Richmond, Christopher Ong, Prasanna Tambe, Aaron Chatterji
**Date:** June 25, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26959
**Abstract:** The researchers analyzed usage patterns from OpenAI's Codex tool to examine how agentic AI—technology capable of performing actions on users' behalf—transforms work practices. They examined three user populations: personal accounts, organizational accounts, and OpenAI staff. Key findings included: the number of active users has grown more than fivefold in the first half of 2026, with expansion beyond software developers. Within OpenAI, Codex became nearly universal, largely replacing ChatGPT for business purposes. More than 10% of users managed multiple concurrent agents weekly, and approximately 26.6% utilized skills for sharing complex workflow instructions. Request complexity surged, with users increasingly requesting tasks estimated to require eight or more hours. Output metrics showed dramatic increases—median legal role employees generated 13 times more tokens than six months prior, while researchers generated 50 times more. The study concludes by examining implications regarding productivity, job restructuring, and workforce changes.

## 2. Who Adopts Generative AI? Financial Digital Engagement, the Age Divide and Trust as a Barrier: Evidence from Spanish Household Microdata [Adoption & Diffusion]
**Authors:** José-Miguel Giner-Pérez
**Date:** July 13, 2026
**Source:** https://www.mdpi.com/2674-1032/5/3/61 (FinTech, Vol. 5, Issue 3, Article 61)
**Abstract:** Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is spreading rapidly among consumers, yet limited understanding exists regarding how adoption connects to households' existing involvement with digital financial services or what barriers prevent non-adoption. This research analyzes individual-level data from Spain's 2025 Survey on ICT Equipment and Use in Households (14,642 internet users aged 16+). Survey-weighted logistic regressions examine GenAI adoption based on financial digital engagement, digital capabilities, and demographics, incorporating regional fixed effects and clustered standard errors, supplemented by marginal effects analysis and examination of stated non-use reasons. Results show GenAI adoption reaches 37.3% among internet users. Online banking participants demonstrate 73% elevated adjusted odds for GenAI adoption (OR = 1.73; 95% CI 1.53–1.97), with adoption climbing from 18% to 79% across financial engagement levels, though online banking accounts for most variation. A pronounced age-related divide emerges, but financial digital status maintains consistent advantage across age groups. Among non-adopters, perceived absence of utility ranks foremost (67%); privacy and security anxieties follow (43%), concentrated disproportionately among online banking participants, women, and older populations.

## 3. The AI Investment Race [Productivity & Growth]
**Authors:** Phurichai Rungcharoenkitkul
**Date:** July 14, 2026
**Source:** https://www.bis.org/publ/work1367.htm (BIS Working Paper No. 1367)
**Abstract:** The AI build-out ranks among the largest technology-driven investment booms in US history. Its scale, reliance on debt and circular equity ties raise questions about the boom's sustainability and financial stability. We study a dynamic contest in which firms competing for a few dominant positions over-commit resources. The over-investment leaves the sector exposed to revenue disappointment that could turn boom into bust. The larger the boom, the deeper the eventual bust. The race to commit early through debt and circular financing also makes a bust more likely. Calibrated to balance sheet and deal data, the model points to over-investment of around 1.5 times the efficient level, rising to around three times where demand is less elastic. A network analysis shows that stress in one firm could cascade to others through chains of financial exposures.

## 4. Strategic Information Disclosure in Algorithmic Pricing [Firms & Competition]
**Authors:** Chengcheng Wang, Zexin Ye
**Date:** July 5, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04345
**Abstract:** As firms increasingly adopt AI-powered pricing algorithms, a key and urgent policy concern is how to regulate the potential algorithmic collusion. This paper approaches the regulatory question through the lens of information design and examines how different disclosure rules, committed to by a third-party intermediary, shape learning outcomes when firms delegate pricing to Q-learning algorithms under stochastic demand. We analyze three disclosure rules: no disclosure, full disclosure, and upper censorship. Upper censorship, which truthfully reveals low-demand states while pooling high-demand ones, delivers higher profits than full disclosure, consistent with theoretical predictions. However, we uncover a profit reversal: when the discount factor is high, no disclosure yields higher profits than full disclosure, whereas when the discount factor is low, full disclosure performs better. This pattern is exactly the opposite of what classical collusion theory predicts. Overall, these findings show that Q-learning agents respond systematically to the information structure and further suggest that restricting information sharing may backfire when algorithms are sufficiently patient, highlighting the need to reassess regulatory approaches in AI-mediated markets.

## 5. Taxing Artificial Intelligence [Regulation & Policy]
**Authors:** Juliette Faivre, Sarah H. Cen
**Date:** July 2, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02144
**Abstract:** The paper examines whether tax instruments can address harms from AI development and deployment. AI taxation should not be understood only as Pigouvian correction, but can serve multiple purposes including correcting harmful activity, redistributing uneven costs and gains, and funding regulatory capacity. The authors survey externalities linked to AI—including environmental pressures on local communities, labor and creative displacement, and systemic risks from rapid frontier development—and evaluate various tax approaches: corporate income taxes, rent-based taxes, consumption taxes on AI services, and activity-specific excise instruments. They assess each mechanism's feasibility, measurement challenges, incidence effects, leakage risks, and innovation impacts, concluding that tax policy must be carefully designed and matched to the specific harms and policy objectives given AI's varied externalities.
