# AI Economics Papers — 2026-07-29

## 1. Generative AI floods and dilutes the market for books [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Tuhin Chakrabarty, Xinyue Liu, Jane C. Ginsburg, Paramveer Dhillon
**Date:** Revised July 26, 2026 (originally posted July 22, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20349
**Summary:** The authors run AI-text detection across 14,419 self-published Amazon genre-fiction books from 2023-2026 and match detected AI content to daily sales through June 2026. They find AI-heavy books expand rapidly and win a growing share of top-rank sales even as revenue per selling book falls, since the number of selling books grew 19.2-fold while quarterly revenue grew only 8.9-fold, with human-written books losing the most ground in genres where AI content is most prevalent. The results show generative AI reshapes a creative market through sheer volume rather than quality, with direct implications for the market-effect test used in copyright fair-use disputes.

## 2. Market Design for AI: Beyond the Copyright Binary [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Yan Dai, Maryam Farboodi, Negin Golrezaei, Sepehr Shahshahani
**Date:** Revised July 24, 2026 (originally posted June 10, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12260
**Summary:** The authors model markets for human-generated content used to train AI, comparing a "free-for-all" fair-use regime against strong intellectual-property protection in both static and dynamic settings. Both extremes fail: free-for-all undercompensates creators, while strong IP rights disproportionately shortchange the most original creators (an "originality penalty") and, over time, push creators toward AI-assisted work that homogenizes training data and degrades model quality (a "curse of precision"). They propose a collective-bargaining data intermediary that subsidizes original contributions as a market design that restores efficiency.

## 3. AI Strategy: How to Choose What AI Product to Implement [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Foster Provost, Panos Ipeirotis
**Date:** July 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.23733
**Summary:** Drawing on real AI project decisions at the real-estate brokerage Compass, the authors introduce "expected ROI" (eROI), a framework that rates a candidate AI product's potential value, likelihood of success, and implementation cost separately rather than demanding one upfront ROI number. Applied retrospectively, the framework would have correctly favored a sales-lead-scoring tool that went on to generate nine-figure annual revenue over a pricing tool that was rightly shelved, cases a simple ROI estimate could not distinguish. It offers firms a practical way to prioritize AI investments under the deep uncertainty surrounding AI project outcomes, bearing on the broader economics of firm-level AI adoption choices.

## 4. Learning Dynamics of Strategic Publishers in Generative AI Ecosystems [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Sagie Dekel, Omer Madmon, Moshe Tennenholtz, Oren Kurland
**Date:** July 28, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25514
**Summary:** The authors model generative-AI search systems as an ecosystem where publishers compete for exposure through citations and attributions rather than traditional rankings, and study how publishers' content strategies evolve under repeated learning dynamics. Using potential-game theory and simulations, they find several content-selection mechanisms resembling real deployed systems are unstable, while identifying an alternative that converges to a stable equilibrium, though stability does not necessarily maximize publisher or user welfare. The findings highlight a trade-off platform designers face between ecosystem stability and the welfare of content creators versus users when choosing how generative-AI systems attribute and reward sources.

## 5. Falling Behind Drives Unsafe Development in an Idealised AI Race Experiment [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Elias Fernández Domingos, The Anh Han
**Date:** July 28, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26034
**Summary:** In a behavioral experiment, paired participants repeatedly chose between "safe" and faster "unsafe" development paths carrying an escalating chance of costly failure, calibrated to mimic competitive AI development races. Contrary to the pre-registered hypothesis, neither the risk level itself nor individual risk preferences predicted unsafe choices; instead, participants became more likely to defect to unsafe development after their rival did so, and especially after falling behind, a pattern the authors reproduce in an evolutionary game-theoretic model of conditional strategies. The results suggest competitive pressure and fear of falling behind, rather than risk appetite, drive unsafe development in AI races, implying policy aimed at curbing racing dynamics may outperform rules targeting individual risk-taking.
