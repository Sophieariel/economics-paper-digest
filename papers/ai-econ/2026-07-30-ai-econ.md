# AI Economics Papers — 2026-07-30

## 1. The Economic Benefits and Costs of AI and Policies to Mitigate AI's Impact on Inequality [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Matthew O. Jackson, Zafer Kanik
**Date:** First posted July 1, 2026; revised July 11, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01101
**Summary:** The authors build a model of how increasingly productive AI reallocates labor and reshapes wages, showing that workers who build AI see wages rise faster than GDP, workers substituted by AI see wages fall in both absolute and relative terms, and workers producing goods AI does not touch see wages track overall GDP growth. They show monopolistic AI production slows AI's deployment and impact relative to competitive production, and they derive optimal tax and regulatory policies that achieve Pareto improvements, with the right policy mix depending on whether AI production is competitive or monopolistic. The results offer a framework for designing redistribution policies that spread AI's productivity gains without undoing the incentives that drive AI investment.

## 2. AI Premium [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Nicola Borri, Yukun Liu, Aleh Tsyvinski
**Date:** First posted June 29, 2026; revised July 3, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30583
**Summary:** Using 380 trillion tokens of licensed OpenRouter usage data covering roughly 2% of global AI token consumption across more than 400 large language models, the authors construct a high-frequency "AI factor" and estimate firm-level AI betas from stock-return comovement with it. They find a large, heterogeneous "AI premium": a long-short portfolio of high- versus low-AI-beta firms earns 64.1 basis points per week, concentrated in exposure to frontier, closed-source, paying-user AI consumption rather than casual or open-weight use, and the premium extends beyond technology firms into consumer-facing and capital-heavy sectors but is absent in emerging markets including China. They also show AI exposure is priced differently across occupations, rising for nonroutine interactive work and falling for analytical and operations-control work, offering early market-based evidence on which jobs and firms investors expect AI to help or hurt.

## 3. Technology Fundamentals and False Bubble Detection: Evidence from Dot-Com and AI Episodes [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Haiqiang Chen, Li Chen, Difang Huang, Yuexin Li, Zhengjun Zhang
**Date:** First posted April 28, 2026; revised July 7, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25826
**Summary:** The authors show that standard bubble-detection tests such as PSY badly overreject when a genuine general-purpose technology shock is embedded in the underlying present-value model, because adoption itself makes fundamental prices locally explosive. They propose a technology-adjusted diagnostic that strips an estimated technology component out of productivity, IT-investment, and patent measures before testing the residual for explosiveness. Applying it, they confirm genuine bubble behavior concentrated in December 1999-March 2000 during the dot-com episode, but find no residual explosiveness in the 2020-2025 AI rally, implying the recent AI-driven run-up is better explained by technology fundamentals than by a bubble.

## 4. The Last Costly Signal: How Generative AI Collapses Competence Signaling and Why Liability Sustains Markets for Expert Services [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Andreas Bauer
**Date:** July 28, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26327
**Summary:** The author models markets for expert services, such as reports and analyses, where generative AI has collapsed the cost of producing convincing-looking expert output, showing that once the "headroom" between machine output and what buyers can detect falls below a critical threshold, no equilibrium separates high- from low-competence providers and the market unravels into an Akerlof-style lemons problem. He shows that outcome-contingent liability, a warranty backed by damages large enough relative to verifiability, can restore full separation, while type-independent provenance certification such as C2PA watermarking cannot, since its cost does not scale with competence. The model further derives how minimum contract sizes and liability-insurance design shape whether credible liability regimes can actually be sustained, offering an account of why some expert-service markets survive AI-driven commoditization and others do not.

## 5. The Computable but Not Learnable Information-Value-Free Equilibria and Regulation of Algorithmic Collusion [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Jason D. Hartline, Chang Wang, Chenhao Zhang
**Date:** July 29, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27128
**Summary:** The authors study "information-value-free" correlated equilibria, where no player benefits from deviating even with full knowledge of others' strategies, and prove that although such equilibria can be computed efficiently offline, standard online learning algorithms using only their own payoff feedback cannot actually converge to them. They interpret this gap through an information-economics lens: any learning algorithm that does converge to a correlated equilibrium must be implicitly generating and exchanging valuable information among players, and this cannot be avoided. The result bears directly on antitrust debates about algorithmic collusion, implying that regulatory approaches aimed at banning implicit information exchange between learning pricing algorithms are incompatible with how rationalizable learning algorithms actually behave.
