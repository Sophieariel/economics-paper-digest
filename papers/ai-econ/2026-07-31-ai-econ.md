# AI Economics Papers — 2026-07-31

## 1. Memory Scarcity, Open Models, and the Restructuring of the AI Industry, 2026-2030 [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Satoshi Matsuoka
**Date:** July 8, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07207
**Summary:** The author builds a quantitative scenario analysis of how a DRAM/HBM price surge, frontier-capable open-weight models, rapid inference-efficiency gains, and new entrants reselling pre-repricing compute fleets will reshape the AI industry through 2030, framing inference economics in dollars per petabyte of bandwidth delivered. The analysis finds the cost gap between incumbents and entrants never closes because a "depreciation conveyor" keeps delivering newly amortized fleets to incumbents faster than hardware prices normalize, while training bifurcates into a luxury frontier tier and a much cheaper mass tier reachable via distillation. The paper assigns probabilities across five industry outcomes, from an oligopoly of compute "landlords" to a commoditization crash, and argues solvency of the current buildout hinges on sustained token-demand growth and premium pricing that current public demand trackers likely overstate.

## 2. The Double-edged Effect of Banning Generative AI on Online Question-and-Answer Communities: Evidence from Stack Exchange [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Yuanhong Ma, Qinglai He, Xitong Li, Lynn Wu
**Date:** July 6, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04601
**Summary:** Using a difference-in-differences design across the full network of Stack Exchange communities, the authors study what happened after several communities banned AI-generated content following the launch of ChatGPT. They find the bans raised the volume of questions posted (more knowledge-seeking) but lowered the share of questions receiving satisfactory answers within the expected time (lower contribution efficiency), with both effects concentrated in non-STEM communities. The results show that platform-level AI content policies carry real, measurable economic costs and benefits for the communities they govern, with the tradeoff shaped by how reliable AI answers would otherwise have been and how much the community values social interaction.

## 3. Introducing AI to an Online Petition Platform Changed Outputs but not Outcomes [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Isabel Corpus, Eric Gilbert, Allison Koenecke, Mor Naaman
**Date:** First posted November 17, 2025; revised July 27, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.13949
**Summary:** Using a natural experiment and difference-in-differences analysis of 1.5 million petitions on Change.org, the authors causally estimate the effect of an in-platform "write with AI" tool on petition content and success. They find the tool significantly changed the lexical features of petitions and increased homogeneity across them, but did not improve the petitions' actual outcomes, a result confirmed among repeat writers observed both before and after the tool's introduction. The findings offer a rare causally identified look at what firm-level AI-adoption actually buys a platform's users, showing that AI tool integration can reshape output without delivering the promised gains and can introduce unintended homogenization costs.

## 4. Adaptive Contracts for Cost-Effective AI Delegation [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Eden Saig, Tamar Garbuz, Ariel D. Procaccia, Inbal Talgam-Cohen, Jamie Tucker-Foltz
**Date:** First posted March 17, 2026; revised July 2, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17212
**Summary:** The authors study pay-for-performance contracts between organizations that delegate text-generation work to AI providers, noting that more elaborate evaluation reduces payment noise but raises evaluation costs, eroding the benefit. They introduce "adaptive contracts" that trigger detailed evaluation only after an initial coarse signal, give efficient algorithms for computing optimal adaptive contracts under natural assumptions, prove hardness results in the general case, and extend the model to randomized variants. Question-answering and code-generation experiments show adaptive contracts outperform non-adaptive baselines, offering a practical mechanism-design tool for the emerging market in AI-delegated labor.

## 5. A General Equilibrium Theory of Orchestrated AI Agent Systems [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Jean-Philippe Garnier
**Date:** First posted February 23, 2026; revised July 13, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.21255
**Summary:** The author models a system of orchestrated large language model agents as an Arrow-Debreu production economy, treating each agent as a firm whose feasible outputs are governed by its frozen weights and the orchestrator as a consumer allocating a routing policy under a budget constraint priced in a functional (infinite-dimensional) commodity space. Using a fixed-point argument, the paper proves existence of a general equilibrium, establishes both welfare theorems (Pareto optimality of equilibrium and decentralizability of Pareto optima), and shows the orchestration dynamics converge globally to a unique equilibrium under a contraction condition, unlike classical tâtonnement processes. The framework offers a formal, DSGE-flavored language for reasoning about efficiency and welfare in multi-agent AI deployments, treating service-level objectives as analogues of policy rates.
