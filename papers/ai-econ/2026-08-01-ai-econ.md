# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-01

## 1. Quota Marketplace: Dynamic Pricing for Efficient Allocation of ML Training Resources [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Balasubramanian Sivan, Renato Paes Leme, Mihai Tiuca, Ian McFarlane, Vasilis Gkatzelis, Nehal Mehta, Soheil Hassas Yeganeh, Vahab Mirrokni, Amin Vahdat
**Date:** 2026-07-09
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09802
**Summary:** The authors design and deploy "Quota Marketplace," a market-based mechanism for allocating scarce ML training chips such as GPUs within Google, extending prior fair-allocation systems to handle workloads with heterogeneous organizational value. They prove the mechanism achieves Pareto efficiency and max-min fairness by letting users express workload value and letting prices adjust dynamically to supply and demand, and report deployment metrics showing real gains from the switch. The paper offers a rare empirically validated look at how compute markets can be engineered inside a major AI firm, directly informing debates about compute scarcity and its allocation.

## 2. When Shippers Become Algorithms: Candidate Exposure, Information Design, and the Concentration of LLM-Mediated Freight Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Takahiro Ezaki, Naoto Imura, Katsuhiro Nishinari
**Date:** 2026-07-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.19967
**Summary:** Using agent-based simulations of a digital freight market in which fifty shipper agents built on commercial LLMs (GPT, Claude, Gemini) select carriers over thirty days, the authors examine what happens to market concentration once carrier selection is delegated to AI agents. They find models converge immediately on the same top carrier, concentration rises steeply once shippers see more than about ten candidates, and revealing true carrier quality does nothing to stop it, but disclosing each carrier's remaining daily capacity cuts concentration by a third and doubles shipper surplus. The result identifies platform information design, rather than model choice or regulation, as the practical lever for containing AI-driven market concentration.

## 3. Supervised Fine-Tuning vs. In-Context Learning: An Equilibrium Analysis of LLM Personalization under Congestion [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Fengzhuo Zhang, Zhuoran Yang, Dirk Bergemann
**Date:** 2026-07-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14371
**Summary:** The authors build an economic model of LLM personalization in which users choose between costly fine-tuning and lightweight in-context learning while competing for congested compute, and platforms choose which personalization methods to offer. They show the optimal choice depends on pretraining coverage and data quality, that congestion can reverse which method dominates, and that offering both methods never hurts platform profit even though it can raise computational load, with GPT-2 experiments confirming the theoretical predictions. A review of 21 major AI platforms' documentation shows the share offering both methods rose from 9.5% in 2021 to 71.4% in 2025, consistent with the model's platform-design implications.

## 4. Who Does Withholding Delay? A Game-Theoretic Model of Open-Weight AI Release Under Asymmetric Proliferation [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Daniel Commey
**Date:** 2026-07-24
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22957
**Summary:** The author models a lab's choice among controlled access, a defender-first window, safeguarded open weights, and minimally restricted release, formalizing when restricting a dual-use AI model actually protects defenders versus handing an advantage to adversaries who can obtain substitutes anyway. The model identifies an "access inversion" condition and an adversary-substitution threshold above which broad release outperforms control, and shows a defender-first window only has value when defenders can deploy protections before adversaries catch up. The framework gives AI release-review processes a concrete list of quantities to estimate, such as actor-specific substitution times and nonrecallable losses, turning open-weight policy debates into a tractable welfare comparison.

## 5. Scaling, Lock-In, and Proxy Compliance: A Political Economy of Responsible AI [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Florian A. D. Burnat, Brittany I. Davidson
**Date:** 2026-07-30
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28023
**Summary:** The authors develop a sequential political-economy model in which an AI vendor chooses auditability and mitigation effort, a deployer monitors after adoption while facing switching costs, and enforcement depends on verifiable evidence. They characterize a "proxy-compliance" equilibrium in which vendors satisfy an observable procurement floor while under-mitigating relative to the social optimum, and show that independent audit rights, portability, incident reporting, and outcome-linked liability each raise enforcement pressure through a different channel. The model explains why documentation and standardized evaluations can coexist with persistent post-deployment harms, offering testable implications for AI governance design.
