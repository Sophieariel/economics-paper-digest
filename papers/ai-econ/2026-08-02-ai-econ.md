# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-02

## 1. When Do AI Gains Become Broadly Shareable? A Policy Threshold for AI-Driven Automation [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Aran Nayebi
**Date:** first posted 2025-05-24; revised 2026-07-29
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18687
**Summary:** The author extends a standard task-automation growth model with an AI-capability parameter to build a policy stress test for when automation gains become broadly shareable rather than narrowly captured, calibrating the model to US quantities. The analysis finds that capability growth alone does not decide the outcome: public capture rates, deployment costs, automation scope, and market structure jointly determine the threshold, with moving from low to moderate public capture (33%) doing as much good as large capability gains while full nationalization adds comparatively little. Comparing tax-heavy systems to sovereign-wealth-style public-asset models (Singapore, Abu Dhabi), the paper gives governments a concrete menu of levers—competition policy, taxation, and public ownership—for keeping future AI rents measurable and broadly distributable.

## 2. Cheaper AI, More Informality? A Dual Labor Market Model for Developing Economies [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Gabriel Montes-Rojas, Fernando Toledo, Juan Manuel Rodríguez Repeti
**Date:** 2026-07-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15381
**Summary:** The authors build a small open-economy DSGE model with a dual (formal/informal) labor market, imported AI capital, and country risk, calibrated to an economy where informal employment is widespread, to ask whether falling AI prices push workers into formal or informal jobs. The key parameter is the elasticity of substitution between imported AI capital and formal labor: when AI substitutes for formal workers, cheaper AI weakens formal labor demand and swells the informal sector as a buffer, but when AI complements formal labor, the same price decline expands formal employment, wages, investment and output. The model implies that whether AI diffusion becomes a source of displacement or of formal-sector growth in developing economies hinges on this substitution-versus-complementarity distinction rather than on the price decline itself.

## 3. The Efficiency Costs of Information Assurance in AI-Enabled Labor Markets: Evidence from LinkedIn's Policy Changes [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Lei Chen, Chaoyue Gao, Alvin Leung, Gavin Wang
**Date:** first posted 2025-11-02; revised 2026-07-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.01923
**Summary:** Exploiting LinkedIn's 2024 rollout of AI-training data collection, its October 2024 restriction of Hong Kong user data from that training, and the restoration of access in November 2025, the authors use a difference-in-differences design comparing Hong Kong to Singapore to test whether limiting AI access to user data degrades labor-market matching. They find the restriction raised labor-market frictions—higher turnover, shorter tenure, longer-open vacancies, lower posting match rates, and lower wages—with outcomes improving after the initial AI rollout, worsening after the restriction, and recovering after restoration, a pattern corroborated by a separate cross-country comparison. The results give a rare causal estimate of the efficiency cost that data-access restrictions impose on AI-enabled markets, bearing directly on the tradeoff between privacy regulation and platform matching efficiency.

## 4. When Pricing Agents Meet Buying Agents: Personalized Pricing and Verifiable Trust [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Chupeng Xie
**Date:** 2026-07-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16343
**Summary:** The paper models personalized pricing once both seller and buyer delegate to AI agents that observe different value signals and execute machine-enforced pricing mandates, solving nested subgames of pricing, inspection, mandate-selection and repeated dealing. It shows a seemingly fair, equal-nominal-surplus rule can nonetheless block trade because each agent applies the rule to its own posterior, and that verifying a signal versus verifying execution of a rule have different effects—evidence of a high signal can justify a high price, while evidence of a restrained rule can block unauthorized extraction. In repeated interactions, offers above a buyer's reference price erode relationship capital in a state-dependent way, and verification is under-provided when a seller ignores its benefit to buyer trust but can be over-provided when it instead enables extraction, giving a formal account of when AI-mediated pricing helps or harms market efficiency.

## 5. How Agentic Is Agentic Commerce? A Population-Scale Measurement of x402 Adoption and Authenticity [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Shengchen Ling, Yajin Zhou, Lei Wu, Cong Wang
**Date:** 2026-07-14
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12575
**Summary:** The authors give the first population-scale measurement of x402, the leading on-chain payment protocol for AI agents transacting for data, APIs and compute, tracing 280 days of settlements on Base (plus a coarser Solana census) back to their true payers through the meta-transaction layer. They find settlement counts are a poor adoption proxy because a facilitator subsidizes gas and nothing on-chain identifies who controls a payment: of $44.1 million in settlements, 21.2% are fictitious and 63.8% are internal transfers within a linked cluster, leaving as little as $187,861 demonstrably reaching a genuine independent service against an upper bound near $20.3 million. The paper shows the manufacturable, star-shaped, machine-timed activity dominating current data reflects operator-driven volume rather than a maturing agent economy, a caution against reading crypto-rail transaction counts as a market-size or adoption signal.
