# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-03

## 1. Artificial Intelligence: Supply-Chain Chokepoints and the Reach of Industrial Policy [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Piyush Akimitsu
**Date:** 2026-07-31
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29572
**Summary:** Using Herfindahl-Hirschman Index concentration measures built from cited, reproducible data, the author maps market concentration across every layer of the AI input stack, from downstream model and cloud usage through chips, advanced packaging, lithography, and critical minerals. The analysis finds a sharp concentration gradient: downstream layers sit below the threshold US regulators treat as highly concentrated, while upstream layers such as advanced packaging and leading-edge lithography are at or near the maximum, often controlled by a single country. Because these upstream chokepoints sit outside the reach of antitrust enforcement, the paper argues the real policy tools are export controls and domestic industrial policy, and shows the concentration is a built rather than merely geological feature that such policy can still move.

## 2. The Benchmark Ceiling: Human Judgment, Evaluation Scarcity, and the Political Economy of AI Capability Measurement [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Mark Esposito, Liu Zhang
**Date:** first posted 2026-06-04; revised 2026-07-13
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01254
**Summary:** The paper models frontier AI benchmarks as public signals of model quality whose precision depends on a thin, hard-to-replace pool of expert-judgment evaluation labor, and shows formally that as models saturate easy benchmark items, valid signal concentrates in an increasingly costly-to-produce hard tail. It demonstrates that private benchmark producers systematically underinvest in validity relative to the social optimum, then documents empirically, using platform data on over a thousand credentialed evaluators, a scarcity premium for this high-judgment labor. The result is a governance concern: as public benchmarks depreciate as measurement instruments, the institutions that certify AI capability face a structural underinvestment problem with direct implications for AI regulation and oversight.

## 3. Women Worry, Men Adopt? Gendered Risk Perceptions and Generative AI Adoption [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Fabian Stephany, Jedrzej Duszynski
**Date:** first posted 2026-01-07; revised 2026-07-30
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03880
**Summary:** Using two waves of a nationally representative UK survey (N=9,172) combined with gender-stratified random forest models and a score-matched analysis of repeated cross-sections, the authors show men report substantially higher frequent generative-AI use than women, with the gap widest among younger, digitally fluent respondents who hold high societal-risk concerns. Perceived societal risk, particularly around mental health and environmental effects, predicts women's adoption far more strongly than men's, and score-matched comparisons show that higher optimism about AI's societal impact is associated with larger adoption gains for women, narrowing the gap. The findings identify risk perception, not just access or digital skills, as a distinct behavioral channel driving unequal diffusion of generative AI and the productivity and career gains that follow from it.

## 4. Optimizing Monetization Strategies for Generative AI Firms: Implications for Search Engagement [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Veronica Rosendo-Rios, Paurav Shukla
**Date:** 2026-07-30
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28780
**Summary:** Running four experiments (N=1,063) on generative-AI platform users, the authors test how introducing advertising-supported pricing tiers alongside subscriptions affects users' upgrade and downgrade decisions. A single ad-supported option strengthens a compromise effect that pulls free users toward upgrading but pushes some paid subscribers to downgrade, while offering two ad-supported tiers instead retains subscribers while still converting free users, an effect mediated by affective and cognitive evaluations and moderated by ad intrusiveness. The results give generative-AI firms facing rising compute costs a concrete option-design lever for revenue strategy that avoids the subscriber attrition risk of a single ad-supported tier.
