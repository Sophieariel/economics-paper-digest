# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-04

## 1. Google's AI & Economy ATLAS v1.0: Mapping Gemini Usage in the Economy [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Zanna Iscenko, Scott Strand, Yiyuan Chen, Guillaume Aimard, Mihai Codreanu, Vivek Sampathkumar, Alex Imas, Julian Jacobs, Evalyne Muiruri, Juan Mateos-Garcia, Jia Jen Ng, Samirah Javed, Josh Martin, Omar Ajmeri, Denis Calin, Andrew Kim, Fabien Curto Millet, James Manyika
**Date:** 2026-07-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00038
**Summary:** Drawing on 15 million de-identified Gemini App, AI Mode, and API interactions, the authors map AI usage onto 800-plus occupations, 4,000 tasks, household activities, and 150 countries. They find workplace adoption already spans occupations covering 88% of US employment but remains shallow and collaborative rather than automating whole tasks end-to-end, while outside work AI touches nearly all of Americans' waking hours, concentrated in high-friction activities like dealing with government and professional services that standard GDP accounts likely undercount. Globally, adoption rises with national income and is more linguistically diverse than expected, giving policymakers a first large-scale empirical baseline for tracking AI diffusion through the economy.

## 2. AI Financial Advice: Supply, Demand, and Life Cycle Implications [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Taha Choukhmane, Tim de Silva, Weidong Lin, Matthew Akuzawa
**Date:** 2026-08-03
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.01607
**Summary:** The authors collect real financial-advice prompts from a representative sample, feed them to GPT-5.2, and simulate the lifetime savings and portfolio consequences of following that advice under realistic labor-income and asset-return assumptions. Following the AI's advice would push respondents toward textbook life-cycle behavior: broader equity-fund participation, equity shares that decline with age, and larger precautionary savings buffers. Recommended equity shares still differ by gender, and the authors show two-thirds of that gap comes from men and women asking different questions (demand) while one-third comes from the model itself responding differently to a gender label on an otherwise identical prompt (supply), pointing to a concrete welfare channel through which AI-mediated advice could narrow or widen wealth gaps.

## 3. One-Shot Pricing for Hands-Off-the-Wheel Advertising Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Emerson Melo, Matt Shum, Rakesh Vohra
**Date:** 2026-08-03
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.01591
**Summary:** The authors argue that once advertisers just declare a budget and ROI target and let the exchange's machine-learning models predict click value, per-impression auctions become unnecessary: the exchange already holds all the information needed to price like a monopolist facing a downward-sloping demand curve. They show this "hands-off-the-wheel" market is a Fisher market whose competitive equilibrium, computable via a single convex program, is simultaneously revenue-optimal among uniform-price mechanisms and outcome-equivalent to today's sequential pacing-adjusted auctions. The result reframes how ML-driven prediction is reshaping market structure in online advertising, replacing millions of live auctions with one centralized pricing computation.

## 4. Pay for The Second-Best Service: A Game-Theoretic Approach Against Dishonest LLM Providers [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Yuhan Cao, Yu Wang, Sitong Liu, Miao Li, Yixin Tao, Tianxing He
**Date:** first posted 2025-11-02; revised 2026-07-31
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.00847
**Summary:** Motivated by real incidents of API providers secretly swapping a promised high-end model for a cheaper one or padding token counts, the authors build the first formal mechanism-design model of a market where users repeatedly delegate queries across LLM providers who can behave dishonestly. They prove an approximately incentive-compatible mechanism exists that guarantees users a quasi-linear "second-best" utility even without knowing which providers cheat, and show no mechanism can do asymptotically better. Simulations calibrated to real API pricing confirm the mechanism disciplines provider behavior, offering a market-design fix for an information asymmetry that is becoming central to competition among LLM providers.

## 5. The Economics of AI Training Data: A Research Agenda [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Hamidah Oderinwale, Anna Kazlauskas
**Date:** first posted 2025-10-28; revised 2026-08-02
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.24990
**Summary:** The authors characterize training data's distinctive economic properties, non-rivalry, context dependence, and rivalry that emerges through contamination, and compile a systematic record of AI training-data deals from 2020-2025 showing persistent market fragmentation, five distinct pricing mechanisms, and the frequent exclusion of original content creators from compensation. They propose a formal hierarchy of exchangeable data units and argue data should enter production functions explicitly rather than being treated as a free input. The paper's main contribution is agenda-setting rather than a single empirical test, but its documentation of how data markets are actually pricing and structuring deals gives the nascent economics-of-data literature a shared empirical and conceptual foundation.
