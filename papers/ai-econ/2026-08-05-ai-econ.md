# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-05

## 1. Optimal Liability Design for Medical AI [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Rui Mao, Tingliang Huang, Houcai Shen
**Date:** 2026-08-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03114
**Summary:** The authors build a principal-agent model in which a social planner sets medical liability rules for physicians whose diagnostic skill is private information and who can choose standard care, personalized judgment, or an imperfect AI recommendation. They show the optimal policy is surprisingly simple, a single liability level applied uniformly across physician types, and that its relationship to AI accuracy is non-monotonic, so better AI does not always justify looser liability. The results imply information asymmetry is a double-edged sword once AI enters the picture, and that greater transparency does not benefit patients, physicians, and AI developers equally.

## 2. The Human-AI Substitution Principle: When Will You Be Replaced by AI in Your Organization? [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Bonny Banerjee, Shreya Singh
**Date:** 2026-07-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20781
**Summary:** The authors develop a formal Human-AI Task Allocation model for hierarchical organizations that encodes the economic asymmetry between how quickly humans acquire skills and how fast AI capabilities scale, deriving a precise "substitution principle" for when AI replaces a given worker. The model predicts abrupt workforce transitions, hybrid human-AI teams that persist because of risk heterogeneity, and flatter management hierarchies with wider spans of control, with middle managers structurally more exposed to automation than either senior specialists or frontline workers. It ties automation economics, organizational design, and AI governance together into one account of how firms restructure as AI capability grows.

## 3. Do Humans Bargain Differently with AI? Evidence from Alternating-Offer Games [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Yuhao Fu, Nobuyuki Hanaki, Haitao Wang
**Date:** 2026-08-02
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.01212
**Summary:** In a lab experiment, participants played a three-stage alternating-offer bargaining game against either another human or a GPT-based AI agent, including a treatment where the AI's payoff affected a third human participant's earnings. Proposers offered AI agents less than they offered human partners, and responders were generally more willing to accept unfair terms from an AI, except when the AI's earnings were known to benefit another real person, which partially restored human-like fairness norms. The findings suggest reciprocity toward AI counterparties in market-like interactions is weaker and more conditional than toward humans, with implications for how AI negotiation agents should be designed and regulated.

## 4. LLM-OSDA: An Optimal-Stopping Dynamic Auction for Native Advertising in Multi-Turn LLM Conversations [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Yan Fang, Jialin Chen, Chun Gan, Hang Yu, Mingjun Nie, Yeyu Zhang, Fengxiang He, Ching Law
**Date:** first posted 2026-07-31; revised 2026-08-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00123
**Summary:** The authors design an auction mechanism for native advertising embedded in multi-turn LLM conversations, where the challenge is not just picking a winning ad but choosing when in the conversation to insert it. Their LLM-OSDA mechanism combines optimal-stopping theory with envelope pricing so that truthful bidding stays weakly dominant even though timing and allocation are coupled, using a learned "StopNet" to approximate the optimal stopping policy for practical deployment. Simulations show the mechanism lifts net ad revenue by 11% over the best fixed-timing baseline without hurting user retention, illustrating how conversational AI interfaces are creating advertising market structures that need their own mechanism design.

## 5. Joint Optimization of Human Headcount and Stochastic AI Resource Capacity [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Marco Montes de Oca
**Date:** 2026-08-01
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00886
**Summary:** The author models how a firm should split a fixed budget between human headcount and generative-AI token capacity when per-employee AI usage is volatile and right-skewed, combining a cognitive-friction-adjusted production function with a chance-constrained stochastic budget frontier. Solving the model yields closed-form optimal headcount and per-capita AI usage, showing that rising volatility in individual AI usage pushes firms toward more AI use per employee but smaller headcount, turning humans and AI tokens, which are complements in production, into substitutes at the margin once budget risk is priced in. The analysis flags usage skewness as a pure "deadweight tax" on the budget and identifies an empirically testable question about whether usage dispersion scales with the mean allocation.
