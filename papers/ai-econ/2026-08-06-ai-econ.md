# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-06

## 1. The Fallback as Signal: Preserved Human Skill, Liability, and Competence Signaling in Credence-Good Markets under Improving AI [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Andreas Bauer
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04276
**Summary:** The author models how firms in credence-good markets, where sellers know more than buyers about what remedy is actually needed, decide how much human expertise to keep engaged as AI recommendations improve, treating continued human "fallback" capacity as a costly signal of competence. An agent-based version of the market reproduces the analytical equilibrium thresholds once noisy beliefs, learning-by-record, and worker churn are added, showing when firms rationally preserve human skill rather than fully automating. The results suggest liability rules keyed only to AI accuracy can misfire, encouraging firms to let human competence atrophy exactly when a human check would still add value.

## 2. Information Aggregation with AI Agents [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Spyros Galanis
**Date:** first posted 2026-04-21; revised 2026-07-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.20050
**Summary:** The author runs a controlled experiment in which LLM-based trading agents receive private signals and trade in a prediction market, testing whether they aggregate dispersed information into accurate prices the way rational-expectations theory expects of human markets. Markets aggregate information well under easy signal structures but performance deteriorates as structures get harder, and neither cheap-talk communication, longer trading duration, nor strategic prompting improves accuracy, though more capable models trade more accurately and profitably. The findings imply AI trading agents can inherit the same limits on reasoning about others' private information that constrain human markets, bearing on how much price discovery to expect from AI-mediated financial markets.

## 3. Early Adoption of Agentic Coding Tools by GitHub Projects [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Maliha Noushin Raida, Daqing Hou
**Date:** first posted 2026-07-15; revised 2026-07-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14037
**Summary:** The authors analyze 25,264 pull requests generated by AI coding agents across 2,361 popular GitHub repositories to measure how open-source software projects are adopting and governing agentic coding tools. Adoption is heavily concentrated in a small subset of projects (the median repository sees only one or two agentic PRs over three months), smaller projects show higher participation rates than larger ones, and oversight is dominated by a single human reviewing each agent's output rather than multi-person review. The results offer early project-level evidence on the pace and organizational shape of AI-tool diffusion in software production, a domain often used as a leading indicator for adoption elsewhere in the economy.
