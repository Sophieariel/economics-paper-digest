# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-07

## 1. Does generative AI narrow education-based productivity gaps? Evidence from a randomized experiment [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Guillermo Cruces, Diego Fernandez Meijide, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro Galvez, Maria Lombardi
**Date:** 2026-08-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04198
**Summary:** Researchers randomized 1,174 adults to complete a workplace-style problem-solving task with or without a generative AI assistant, then measured performance again after AI access was removed. Access to AI closed roughly three-quarters of the initial performance gap between higher- and lower-education participants, mainly because less-educated participants leaned on the tool for substantial assistance while more-educated participants used it more effectively, and part of the narrowing persisted once AI was taken away. The results suggest generative AI can compress skill-based productivity gaps in the short run, but underlying human-capital differences continue to shape both unassisted performance and how effectively workers exploit the tool.

## 2. (Early) AI Compute Asset Pricing [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Federico M. Bandi, Yinan Su
**Date:** first posted 2026-07-13; revised 2026-08-03
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12156
**Summary:** The authors build an early asset-pricing framework for computing power itself, now a capital-intensive input whose expenditure and service flow already exceed 1% of U.S. GDP, ahead of the launch of tradable compute futures contracts. They show standard no-arbitrage links between futures and spot prices break down because compute cannot be stored, construct synthetic futures from existing rental contracts as an upper bound on true futures prices, and assemble the first compute futures return panel sorted by GPU generation and maturity. The preliminary evidence points to a positive compute risk premium, consistent with compute providers hedging exposure as this new asset class begins to be financialized.

## 3. The Fallback as Signal: Preserved Human Skill, Liability, and Competence Signaling in Credence-Good Markets under Improving AI [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Andreas Bauer
**Date:** 2026-08-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04276
**Summary:** The author models a credence-good market in which firms deploying improving but still imperfect AI must decide how much to keep human experts engaged as a fallback option. Because that fallback also functions as a public signal of competence and shifts liability exposure, the model derives analytical thresholds for when firms retain versus phase out human involvement as AI accuracy rises, and shows these thresholds survive in an agent-based simulation with noisy beliefs, learning-by-record, and worker churn. The findings imply that liability rules and signaling incentives, not just raw AI accuracy, govern how quickly human expertise disappears from AI-augmented markets.

## 4. AI-Native Insurance for Agentic AI: Pricing, Underwriting, and End-to-End Automation [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Quanyan Zhu
**Date:** 2026-07-14
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13230
**Summary:** The author develops a mathematical framework for underwriting and pricing insurance contracts for autonomous "agentic" AI deployments, representing each deployment's risk through a state capturing autonomy level, operational authority, permission exposure, governance maturity, and dependency concentration. Solving the resulting contract-design optimization yields premiums, deductibles, and coverage terms, and characterizes an "insurability region" whose feasibility deteriorates monotonically as AI autonomy and exposure rise, with governance-certification thresholds gating coverage. A healthcare case study illustrates how the framework prices contracts and automates claims, positioning insurance as both an operating cost and a de facto regulatory mechanism for agentic AI.
