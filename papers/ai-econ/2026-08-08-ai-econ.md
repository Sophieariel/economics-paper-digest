# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-08

## 1. Does generative AI narrow education-based productivity gaps? Evidence from a randomized experiment [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Guillermo Cruces, Diego Fernandez Meijide, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro Galvez, Maria Lombardi
**Date:** 2026-08-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04198
**Summary:** The authors run a randomized online experiment with 1,174 adults who complete a workplace-style problem-solving task with or without a generative AI assistant, then a follow-up module without AI. Access to AI raises performance for everyone but closes about three-quarters of the initial education-based performance gap, mainly because less-educated participants lean on the assistant more heavily while more-educated participants use it more effectively; part of the gain persists even after AI is removed, though a sizable gap re-emerges. The results suggest generative AI compresses observed productivity differences tied to education while human-capital differences continue to shape how effectively people use the tool.

## 2. AI-Native Insurance for Agentic AI: Pricing, Underwriting, and End-to-End Automation [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Quanyan Zhu
**Date:** 2026-07-14
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13230
**Summary:** The author builds a mathematical framework for underwriting and pricing insurance contracts for autonomous AI agent deployments, representing each deployment as a risk state spanning autonomy level, operational authority, permission exposure, governance maturity, and dependency concentration. The model maps this risk state to loss probabilities and severities, then solves an optimization problem for premiums, deductibles, and coverage subject to participation, profitability, and incentive-compatibility constraints, deriving structural insurability conditions including a feasibility region that shrinks monotonically as exposure rises. A healthcare case study illustrates how the framework prices contracts and sets governance thresholds, framing insurance as both a cost of AI deployment and a market mechanism for regulating it.

## 3. Information Aggregation with AI Agents [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Spyros Galanis
**Date:** 2026-04-21; revised 2026-07-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.20050
**Summary:** In a controlled experiment, LLM-based trading agents receive private signals and trade in a prediction market, with information aggregation measured by how closely the closing price tracks the truth. Markets aggregate information well under easy information structures but degrade under harder ones, mirroring known human limits at reasoning about what others know; neither cheap-talk communication, market duration, strategic prompting, nor performance feedback improves aggregation, though "smarter" models both aggregate better and earn more. The findings raise doubts about how reliably AI-agent-driven markets will produce informative prices as such agents take on more trading and forecasting roles.

## 4. The Fallback as Signal: Preserved Human Skill, Liability, and Competence Signaling in Credence-Good Markets under Improving AI [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Andreas Bauer
**Date:** 2026-08-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04276
**Summary:** The paper models a credence-good market in which firms deploying steadily improving but still imperfect AI must decide how much to keep human experts engaged as a fallback option. An agent-based simulation of the market reproduces the thresholds implied by the underlying analytical model once noisy beliefs, learning-by-doing effects on record quality, and worker churn are introduced. The framing casts retained human skill as a competence and liability signal rather than pure redundancy, implying firms' AI-adoption choices are shaped by signaling and liability incentives even as AI capability rises.

## 5. Early Adoption of Agentic Coding Tools by GitHub Projects [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Maliha Noushin Raida, Daqing Hou
**Date:** 2026-07-15; revised 2026-07-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14037
**Summary:** The authors analyze 25,264 pull requests generated by agentic coding tools across 2,361 popular GitHub repositories to characterize how project-level adoption, productivity, and human oversight are unfolding. Adoption remains concentrated in a small subset of projects, with a typical repository producing only one or two agentic PRs in a three-month window, and small projects (1-5 contributors) showing higher participation intensity than medium or large ones; oversight is dominated by a single human reviewing each agent's contribution rather than multi-person review. The results offer an early empirical baseline on the diffusion of agentic AI tools into software production, showing that uptake depends on organizational review capacity as much as on tool capability.
