# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-09

## 1. Does generative AI narrow education-based productivity gaps? Evidence from a randomized experiment [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Guillermo Cruces, Diego Fernandez Meijide, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro Galvez, Maria Lombardi
**Date:** 2026-08-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04198
**Summary:** In a randomized online experiment with 1,174 adults, the authors gave a subset of participants access to a generative AI assistant while completing a workplace-style problem-solving task, then removed AI access for a follow-up module. AI access closed about three-quarters of the initial performance gap between higher- and lower-education participants, with less-educated participants leaning on the tool more heavily while more-educated participants used it more effectively, and the gains were not purely delegation since assisted participants still outperformed controls once AI was removed. The results suggest generative AI can compress observed productivity differences tied to formal education even as underlying human-capital gaps continue to shape how effectively people use the tool.

## 2. Information Aggregation with AI Agents [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Spyros Galanis
**Date:** first posted 2026-04-21; revised 2026-07-22
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.20050
**Summary:** The author runs a controlled experiment in which LLM-based trading agents receive private signals and trade in a simulated prediction market, testing whether they aggregate dispersed information the way an informationally efficient market should. Markets populated by these AI agents aggregate information well when the underlying signal structure is easy but degrade sharply as the inference problem gets harder, and neither cheap-talk communication, longer trading windows, nor strategic prompting fixes this, mirroring known human limits at reasoning about others' information; more capable models trade more accurately and profitably, but feedback on past performance does not improve them. The findings raise doubts about how reliable AI-agent-driven markets would be as substitutes for human-populated ones in tasks that require aggregating dispersed information.

## 3. The Fallback as Signal: Preserved Human Skill, Liability, and Competence Signaling in Credence-Good Markets under Improving AI [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Andreas Bauer
**Date:** 2026-08-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04276
**Summary:** The paper models a credence-good market — one where buyers cannot verify service quality even after the fact — in which firms using an improving but imperfect AI tool must decide how much to keep human experts engaged as a fallback. Combining analytical thresholds with an agent-based simulation featuring noisy beliefs, learning-by-record, and worker churn, the author shows that preserved human involvement functions as a costly signal of competence and shapes firms' liability exposure as AI accuracy rises. The results imply that as AI improves, firms in expertise-intensive markets face a persistent tradeoff between the efficiency of full automation and the reputational and liability value of visibly retaining human oversight.

## 4. AI-Augmented Human Resource Management? Insights from German companies [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Yannick Kalff, Katharina Simbeck
**Date:** first posted 2026-07-15; revised 2026-07-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13839
**Summary:** Combining interviews, group discussions, and a survey of 410 respondents, the authors examine how German firms are integrating generative AI and predictive analytics into human resource management. They find adoption is driven mainly by efficiency and rationalization goals rather than strategic talent development, with the pace and shape of integration constrained by digital infrastructure, co-determination (works council) structures, and data-governance concerns. The study offers firm-level evidence on how organizational and institutional factors mediate AI diffusion within a specific business function, complicating simple adoption-curve accounts of enterprise AI uptake.
