# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-10

## 1. Does generative AI narrow education-based productivity gaps? Evidence from a randomized experiment [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Guillermo Cruces, Diego Fernandez Meijide, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro Galvez, Maria Lombardi
**Date:** 2026-08-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04198
**Summary:** In a randomized experiment with 1,174 adults, the authors had participants complete a workplace problem-solving task with or without a generative AI assistant, then repeat a similar task unassisted, to test whether AI narrows or widens productivity gaps by education level. AI raised performance for everyone but disproportionately helped less-educated workers, cutting the education-based performance gap by roughly three-quarters, with chat logs showing lower-education users leaning on the AI for direct answers while higher-educated users deployed it more strategically. Because the gains only partly persisted once AI was removed, the results suggest generative AI compresses observed productivity differences in assisted tasks without fully closing the underlying human-capital gap, which matters for how AI reshapes productivity dispersion across the workforce.

## 2. The Fallback as Signal: Preserved Human Skill, Liability, and Competence Signaling in Credence-Good Markets under Improving AI [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Andreas Bauer
**Date:** 2026-08-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04276
**Summary:** The paper models credence-good markets, where sellers know more than buyers about the service actually needed, as AI capability steadily improves, asking why firms would keep costly human workers on hand as a fallback rather than fully automating. It shows analytically, and confirms in an agent-based simulation with noisy beliefs, learning-by-record, and worker churn, that preserving a human fallback functions as a costly signal of competence and a liability hedge, with the threshold for keeping humans engaged shifting as AI accuracy rises. The result offers an economic account of why partial human retention persists in professional services even as AI improves, highlighting liability and quality-signaling as forces that slow full automation in trust-sensitive markets.

## 3. Invisible to the Machine: Auditing AI Restaurant, Cafe, and Bar Recommendation Against a Complete Market Census [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Vladimir Pitenin
**Date:** 2026-08-07
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07069
**Summary:** Using a complete census of 4,776 restaurants, cafes and bars in two Balinese markets, the author audits which venues four production AI systems (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity) recommend across nearly a thousand persona-conditioned queries, comparing the recommended set against the full market rather than a sample. The study finds that most venues, including many well-established ones, are never surfaced by any system, that documentation signals (review volume, having a website, listed prices) predict simply being mentioned far more than star ratings do, and that stale recommendations of closed venues are a bigger practical problem than fabrication. Because AI assistants are becoming a primary discovery channel with direct revenue consequences, the findings point to a new algorithmic gatekeeping structure in local consumer markets that firms and regulators should treat as a market-structure issue, not just a model-accuracy one.

## 4. AI-Native Insurance for Agentic AI: Pricing, Underwriting, and End-to-End Automation [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Quanyan Zhu
**Date:** 2026-07-14
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13230
**Summary:** The paper develops a mathematical framework for pricing, underwriting, and designing insurance contracts for autonomous "agentic" AI deployments, mapping a deployment's risk state (autonomy level, operational authority, permission exposure, governance maturity) into event probabilities, premiums, deductibles, and policy terms. It derives structural properties of when such deployments are insurable at all, showing that insurability deteriorates monotonically as exposure and autonomy rise and identifying governance-certification thresholds firms must clear to obtain coverage, illustrated with a healthcare case study. The framework treats insurance both as a market response to a new risk class and as a de facto regulatory mechanism, offering an early economic account of how a market for agentic-AI risk could be priced and structured.

## 5. Early Adoption of Agentic Coding Tools by GitHub Projects [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Maliha Noushin Raida, Daqing Hou
**Date:** first posted 2026-07-15; revised 2026-07-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14037
**Summary:** Analyzing 25,264 pull requests generated by AI coding agents across 2,361 popular GitHub repositories, the authors document how open-source projects adopt agentic coding tools at the project level rather than just the individual-contribution level. They find that intensive adoption remains concentrated in a small subset of projects, that smaller projects show higher participation rates than larger ones, and that human oversight of agent-generated code is still dominated by a single reviewer rather than distributed collaboration. The results provide early empirical evidence on the pace and organizational shape of AI-tool diffusion in software production, a leading indicator for how automation spreads unevenly across firms and project types more broadly.
