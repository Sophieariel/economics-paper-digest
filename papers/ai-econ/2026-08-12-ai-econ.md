# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-12

## 1. When LLM Agents Negotiate: Private Information and Dynamic Bargaining in Supply Chains [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Chen Liang, Fasheng Xu
**Date:** 2026-07-29
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07538
**Summary:** The authors benchmark nine LLMs acting as buyer and seller negotiators in a classic private-information supply-chain bargaining game against the Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium, running nearly 10,000 LLM-to-LLM negotiations. They find delegated AI agents settle almost every deal and capture most of the available surplus, but slower bargaining erodes a fifth to a third of that surplus, and which model provider sits on which side of the table predicts who captures the gains better than raw model capability does. The results show that as firms hand procurement to AI agents, vendor choice and prompt design become first-order distributional decisions with real market consequences, not just efficiency questions.

## 2. Pricing Intelligence: Task-Based Learning and Labor Displacement in the AI Economy [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Carl-Christian Groh
**Date:** 2026-08-11
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11112
**Summary:** The author builds a microeconomic model in which AI providers sell task-completion access to heterogeneous users and improve at tasks through the delegation they receive, creating a tension between short-run profit maximization and learning to handle complex tasks. Under monopoly the model produces either a convex path of labor displacement or a "learning trap" where complex-task capability stalls, while intense competition between AI providers speeds up displacement and can push providers toward specializing in different task types. The paper offers a demand-side account of why AI capability growth and the pace of labor displacement are shaped as much by market structure and provider competition as by the underlying technology.

## 3. Artificial Intelligence, Data and Competition [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Zhang Xu, Mingsheng Zhang, Wei Zhao
**Date:** first posted 2024-03-10; revised 2026-08-08
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06150
**Summary:** The authors model competing pricing algorithms that use consumer data to segment markets, showing that AIs can learn to sustain tacit collusion by dividing segments among themselves, charging supra-competitive prices where they hold a segment and competing hard elsewhere. When algorithms have asymmetric data and segmentation ability, the finer-segmenting AI can use a bait-and-restrain-exploit strategy to induce the coarser one into collusion, and higher-willingness-to-pay segments are more likely to end up collusively allocated. The finding that better data does not guarantee a competitive advantage, and can instead produce the worst outcome for consumers even without explicit coordination, is a direct input into how regulators should think about monitoring algorithmic pricing and data access.

## 4. The Fallback as Signal: Preserved Human Skill, Liability, and Competence Signaling in Credence-Good Markets under Improving AI [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Andreas Bauer
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-04; revised 2026-08-09
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04276
**Summary:** The author builds a signaling model of credence-good markets, such as expert services, where firms decide how much to keep engaging human workers as AI improves, since that engagement builds a costly fallback skill that only shows its value when AI fails. Because more-skilled providers fail less often exactly when AI is down, the cost of a liability pledge falls as fallback skill rises, restoring a single-crossing condition that lets firms use liability commitments to credibly signal preserved human competence to clients and workers alike. The model characterizes the cheapest separating pledge schedule and the market-size thresholds beyond which such certification becomes unaffordable or unnecessary, giving a market-based account of why liability regimes might persist even as AI approaches human quality.

## 5. Does generative AI narrow education-based productivity gaps? Evidence from a randomized experiment [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Guillermo Cruces, Diego Fernandez Meijide, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro Galvez, Maria Lombardi
**Date:** 2026-08-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04198
**Summary:** In a randomized experiment with 1,174 adults, the authors assign a workplace-style problem-solving task with or without a generative AI assistant, then test unassisted performance afterward. AI access raises performance for everyone but especially for less-educated participants, cutting the education-based performance gap by about three-quarters, and assisted participants do not perform worse once AI is removed, with lower-education participants retaining part of the gain even though a sizable gap re-emerges. The results indicate generative AI can compress productivity differences tied to formal education in the moment of use, even as underlying human-capital differences continue to shape unassisted performance and how effectively people use the tool.
