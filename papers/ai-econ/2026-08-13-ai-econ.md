# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-13

## 1. How Organizations Use AI: Evidence from ChatGPT [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Aaron Chatterji, David Holtz, Neel Rakholia, Prasanna Tambe, Gawesha Weeratunga
**Date:** 2026-08-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12236
**Summary:** The authors link ChatGPT Enterprise account records to worker roles, message-level task classifications, and public-company financial data through March 2026, covering over 1,500 organizations and 17 million messages at a six-month adoption horizon. They document that enterprise usage is growing through both new adoption and rising intensity among existing adopters, that U.S. public-company adoption concentrates among larger, more R&D- and SG&A-intensive firms, and that usage cuts across job functions and seniority with especially high intensity among early-career workers. The scale and firm-financial linkage make this one of the first large-sample looks at which firms adopt frontier generative AI and how quickly they integrate it into workflows.

## 2. Organizational Technology Ladders: Remote Work and Generative AI Adoption [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Gregor Schubert
**Date:** 2026-08-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11626
**Summary:** Using U.S. job-posting data and an instrumental-variables strategy based on predicted labor-market pressure to offer remote work, the author finds that a 10 percentage point rise in firms' remote hiring in 2021-2022 increases the share of job postings mentioning generative AI in 2023-2024 by 0.4 to 0.7 percentage points. The evidence points to a technology-ladder mechanism: adopting remote work shifted hiring toward technical and managerial capabilities that make firms faster to convert AI exposure into actual adoption, with firms that later imposed return-to-office mandates showing an even larger response. The paper offers a causal account of why earlier technology choices create organizational capital that shapes the speed and unevenness of generative AI diffusion across firms.

## 3. Voice AI in Firms: A Natural Field Experiment on Automated Job Interviews [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Brian Jabarian, Luca Henkel
**Date:** 2026-07-30
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28222
**Summary:** In a natural field experiment with 70,000 job applicants randomly assigned to human recruiters or AI voice agents for the interview stage (with humans still making all hiring decisions), the authors find AI-interviewed applicants are 12% more likely to receive offers, with higher job starts and worker retention and no drop in hired-worker productivity. Transcript analysis shows AI agents conduct more structured, controlled-variance interviews that still respond to individual applicants, collecting more hiring-relevant information than human-led interviews. The results show automating information collection, rather than decision-making itself, can improve firm hiring outcomes through standardization.

## 4. Pricing Access to Dynamic Information Services [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Weijie Zhong
**Date:** first posted 2025-10-10; revised 2026-08-10
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09859
**Summary:** The author characterizes the revenue-optimal way to sell a real-time, capacity-constrained information service to customers who privately differ in how urgently they need it, showing the optimal mechanism keeps the underlying information process undistorted and screens customers entirely through a one-dimensional menu of service caps, unlike a standard Mussa-Rosen quality-distortion monopolist. This rationalizes a contractual form seen across AI service tiers, expert-network consultations, and analyst retainers: a flat fee for capped access to a shared, high-quality process, with per-unit price typically falling as the cap rises. The result gives a theoretical foundation for how AI providers and similar information sellers should price tiered access when speed and reliability, not information quality, are what customers pay to unlock.

## 5. Environmental and Economic Implications of Artificial Intelligence Data Centers in the United States [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Johanna Bolaños-Zuñiga, Alberto J. Lamadrid
**Date:** 2026-08-10
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09882
**Summary:** The authors assess how AI data centers' electricity demand growth, cooling needs, and backup operations translate into environmental and economic impacts across the United States, finding that outcomes depend less on facility design than on the surrounding electricity, water, and land-use systems, with emissions driven by marginal generation mixes and transmission constraints that vary sharply by region. They show existing regulatory and market structures often fail to price the location- and time-specific externalities these facilities generate, even though efficiency, cooling, and operational improvements can mitigate impacts where system conditions allow. The paper makes the case that data-center-driven compute expansion needs integrated electricity-market and land-use policy, not facility-level standards alone, to internalize its economic and environmental costs.
