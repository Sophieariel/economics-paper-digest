# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-15

## 1. Certificates without Electrons? Theory and Evidence on Impacts from AI-Driven Power Demand [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Dana Golden, Aruna Balasubramanian, Niranjan Balasubramanian
**Date:** first posted 2026-05-30; revised 2026-08-02
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00811
**Summary:** The authors build a game-theoretic model of how data-center operators choose among renewable energy certificates, power-purchase agreements, and behind-the-meter colocation, then test its predictions with a difference-in-differences design that exploits the staggered release of large language models as a natural experiment linking AI activity to local grid outcomes. They find that even when certificates nominally cover 100% of a data center's consumption, a timing mismatch between AI power draw and credited renewable generation lets AI demand raise fossil generation, push wholesale prices up to 25% in affected zones, and add roughly half to one extra outage per year, with effects scaling with model size. On-site storage colocation reverses these harms by absorbing demand spikes and removing generators' revenue risk, while certificate-only procurement does not, showing that how AI firms structure their power contracts materially shapes the welfare costs their compute imposes on the grid.

## 2. What Capital After Labor? Forecasting the Talent ROI Transition in the Human-AI Era [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Kwan Soo Shin
**Date:** first posted 2026-06-18; revised 2026-07-30
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19846
**Summary:** The author argues that AI augmentation breaks the historical link between labor time and output, and looks for early signs of this in a panel of 365 Korean firms (2,281 firm-year observations) exposed to the country's staged 52-hour workweek mandate. Overhead spending as a share of revenue rose steadily from 2018 to 2024, and two-way fixed-effects, event-study, and Callaway-Sant'Anna difference-in-differences estimates converge on a positive, if institution-dependent, rise in overhead pressure at firms still running time-based labor accounting even as AI raises internal overhead. The paper's main contribution is a forecasting exercise projecting that firms which shift to output-based "talent ROI" accounting will out-perform time-based peers by 1.5-2.0 percentage points of TFP growth by 2032, offering an early empirical signature of AI-driven pressure on firm-level labor-cost accounting.

## 3. Beyond the Grid: Cost, Carbon, and Capital Requirements of On-Site Power Technologies for AI Data Centers [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Eliseo Curcio
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-08
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08170
**Summary:** This paper prices nine on-site power technologies against grid electricity for AI data centers, treating the grid price as endogenous to data-center demand and costing each option on a full-site basis that includes standby charges, delivered fuel costs, and current U.S. tax-credit provisions. It finds that no on-site alternative beats the grid at today's prices — the cheapest option, gas combined-cycle generation, still costs about 114 against a 92 grid price per MWh, battery storage is capped near 18% of annual energy and dirtier than the grid at the margin, and even massive global electrolyzer investment leaves hydrogen roughly twice the grid price. The author concludes that on-site power functions as a way for developers to buy access and depth in constrained interconnection queues rather than a cost-competitive substitute for grid power, a framing relevant to where the AI buildout's capital is actually being spent.

## 4. Measuring AI innovation with trademark data [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** C. Castaldi, F. Castellacci, A. Fronzetti Colladon, L. Segneri, F. Venturini
**Date:** first posted 2026-07-21
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18795
**Summary:** This research note proposes tracking AI diffusion through trademark filings rather than the more commonly used patent counts, arguing trademarks better capture firms turning AI into new goods and services and are available promptly across sectors and countries. The authors illustrate the approach with an empirical exploration of Italian firms' AI-related trademark filings, showing how the data can be broken down by sector, region, and firm characteristics to map where AI commercialization is concentrated. They position AI trademarks as a complement to AI patents for researchers, managers, and policymakers who need a timely, broad-coverage signal of AI adoption and diffusion across the economy.
