# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-17

## 1. How Organizations Use AI: Evidence from ChatGPT [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Aaron Chatterji, David Holtz, Neel Rakholia, Prasanna Tambe, Gawesha Weeratunga
**Date:** 2026-08-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12236
**Summary:** The authors link ChatGPT Enterprise account records to worker roles, task classifications, and public-company financial data through March 2026, covering more than 1,500 organizations and 17 million messages at a six-month adoption horizon. They document that enterprise usage is growing fast on both the extensive and intensive margins, that adoption among U.S. public companies is concentrated among larger, more valuable, and more R&D- and SG&A-intensive firms, and that usage is heaviest among early-career workers across a broad range of knowledge-work tasks. The results provide some of the first large-scale, firm-level evidence on how unevenly companies are integrating a general-purpose AI technology into their workflows.

## 2. Organizational Technology Ladders: Remote Work and Generative AI Adoption [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Gregor Schubert
**Date:** 2026-08-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11626
**Summary:** Using U.S. job-posting data and an instrumental-variables strategy built on predicted differences in labor-market pressure to offer remote work, the author shows that firms which adopted remote-work technology more heavily during COVID-19 later adopted generative AI faster, with a 10-point increase in 2021-22 remote hiring raising generative-AI mentions in job postings by 0.4 to 0.7 points. The evidence points to an "organizational technology ladder," in which adopting one technology builds hiring practices, skills, and organizational capital that lower the cost of adopting the next, partly by shifting hiring toward technical and managerial capabilities. Firms that imposed return-to-office mandates, read as revealing low remote-work productivity, show a much larger AI-adoption response to prior remote-work exposure, consistent with adoption frictions operating at the organizational rather than purely technological level.

## 3. Keep, Customize, or Exit: Default Design and Token Pricing in LLM Reasoning Services [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Ahmet Bugra Gundogan, Yigit Turkmen, Melih Bastopcu
**Date:** 2026-08-13
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13315
**Summary:** The authors model an LLM provider that sets a per-token price and a default reasoning-token allocation while users can accept the default, customize it, or leave, framing the interaction as a Stackelberg game and solving in closed form for the user's optimal customized allocation. They characterize the provider's optimal default through a three-regime rule, reduce the equilibrium problem to a one-dimensional price search, and show that defaults only shape the reasoning allocation actually used when customization carries a convenience cost. Experiments on open-weight reasoning models across math and science benchmarks support the underlying accuracy-token tradeoff and show how model and task characteristics move equilibrium prices and default allocations, offering a market-design lens on how AI firms price and package "thinking."

## 4. Equilibrium Pricing in Oligopolistic Data Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Bhaskar Ray Chaudhury, Jugal Garg, Eklavya Sharma, Jiaxin Song
**Date:** 2026-08-14
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14018
**Summary:** The authors study price competition among strategic data sellers facing budget-constrained buyers such as machine-learning firms, showing that the non-rivalry of data breaks the equilibrium-existence guarantees that hold for rivalrous goods, so that neither an exact nor even an approximate Nash equilibrium in uniform prices need exist. Allowing sellers to post piecewise-linear convex pricing schedules restores approximate stability: a pricing profile is guaranteed to exist in which no seller can more than double its revenue by deviating to any uniform price. Simulations show equilibria converge quickly and beat the worst-case bound in practice, giving a tractable market-design benchmark for how data markets that feed AI training might be priced and regulated.

## 5. Error-Aware Reverse Auction Mechanism for Large Language Model Routing [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Haolong Chen, Zhengyuan Xin, Liang Zhang, Lei Xue, Guangxu Zhu
**Date:** 2026-08-13
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12719
**Summary:** The authors replace centralized, task-center-based prediction of which LLM should answer a query with a market mechanism in which competing model providers bid self-predicted success probabilities and costs in a reverse auction, addressing the information-risk mismatch and scalability limits of centralized routing. Their Error-Aware Reverse Auction Mechanism is proven Bayesian incentive compatible and individually rational even when both provider bids and center evaluations are noisy, and comes with an explicit welfare-loss bound plus conditions under which noise from opposite-signed errors cancels out or is otherwise smoothed away. Tests on simulations and real benchmarks show the mechanism achieves a better cost-performance frontier than centralized routing baselines, pointing to auction-based market design as a way to allocate LLM queries efficiently as the model marketplace grows.
