# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-18

## 1. How Organizations Use AI: Evidence from ChatGPT [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Aaron Chatterji, David Holtz, Neel Rakholia, Prasanna Tambe, Gawesha Weeratunga
**Date:** 2026-08-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12236
**Summary:** The authors link ChatGPT Enterprise account records to worker roles, task classifications, and public-company financials through March 2026, covering more than 1,500 organizations and 17 million messages at a six-month adoption horizon. They find enterprise AI usage has grown through both new firm adoption and deepening use among existing adopters, that adoption among U.S. public companies concentrates in larger, more R&D- and SG&A-intensive firms, and that use spans job functions with especially high intensity among early-career workers. The results give one of the first large-scale, firm-linked pictures of how organizations actually integrate generative AI, showing wide dispersion in adoption speed and breadth that matters for how AI's productivity effects will play out across the economy.

## 2. U.S. Technological Containment and the Rise of China's Open AI Ecosystem [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Wang Jin, Nadav Kunievsky, Bowen Lou, Tianshu Sun, James Evans
**Date:** first posted 2026-06-14; revised 2026-08-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15999
**Summary:** The authors trace how U.S. export controls on advanced chips and computing infrastructure pushed China to fold open-source AI into national strategy, documenting that Chinese developers increased engagement with open-weight LLM repositories substantially more than U.S. developers after the controls took effect. They show the resulting Chinese-origin open models diffused widely through global open-source communities and research even though they remain largely invisible in U.S. patent filings, while American commercial entities quietly draw on them in open-access research. The findings suggest that technological containment policy does not just slow a rival's AI progress but reshapes the global ecosystem and diffusion channels through which AI capability spreads, with the U.S. commercial sector absorbing more of that spillover than official statistics capture.

## 3. Organizational Technology Ladders: Remote Work and Generative AI Adoption [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Gregor Schubert
**Date:** 2026-08-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11626
**Summary:** The author proposes that firms climb an "organizational technology ladder," where adopting one technology builds skills and organizational capital that lower the cost of adopting the next, and tests this using U.S. job-posting data with an instrumental-variables strategy based on local labor-market pressure to offer remote work. A 10-percentage-point rise in a firm's remote hiring in 2021-2022 raises the share of its job postings mentioning generative AI in 2023-2024 by 0.4 percentage points across firms and 0.7 points across occupations within firms, with the effect concentrated where earlier remote-work adoption shifted hiring toward technical and managerial capability. The pattern is sharply larger at firms with return-to-office mandates, read as firms with low remote productivity, pointing to organizational frictions rather than raw technology exposure as a key driver of uneven generative AI diffusion across firms.

## 4. Equilibrium Pricing in Oligopolistic Data Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Bhaskar Ray Chaudhury, Jugal Garg, Eklavya Sharma, Jiaxin Song
**Date:** 2026-08-14
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14018
**Summary:** The authors model a market where budget-constrained buyers such as ML firms purchase training data from strategic sellers who each set a price, turning equilibrium pricing into a Nash equilibrium of a pricing game. They show the standard existence guarantees for equilibrium prices in markets for rivalrous goods break down for data because it is non-rival: exact Nash equilibria need not exist, and even reasonably good approximate equilibria can fail to exist under simple uniform pricing. Allowing sellers to post piecewise-linear convex pricing functions instead restores a bounded approximate equilibrium, giving a first theoretical account of why data markets may need richer pricing instruments than physical-goods markets to reach stable, competitive outcomes.

## 5. Sequential LLM Release Facilitates Manipulation in Regulated Markets [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Eilam Shapira, Moshe Tennenholtz, Roi Reichart
**Date:** first posted 2026-01-16; revised 2026-08-17
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11496
**Summary:** Using GLEE, a benchmark of 587,000 strategic decisions by 13 LLMs across more than 1,300 bargaining, negotiation and persuasion setups, the authors study what happens to market outcomes when a regulator lets a new model release expand the set of AI agents available to negotiate on people's or firms' behalf. Across more than 50,000 release-to-release comparisons they find many releases move the two sides' payoffs in opposite directions, and identify a "Poisoned Apple effect" in which a newly released model that no rational agent actually adopts in equilibrium still redistributes payoffs between counterparties and alters the market design a regulator faces. The result shows that in markets where AI agents mediate transactions, sequential model releases are themselves a regulatory-relevant event, since technology restrictions meant to protect participants can end up amplifying this manipulation channel rather than closing it.
