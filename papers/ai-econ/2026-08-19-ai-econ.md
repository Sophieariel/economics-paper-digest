# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-19

## 1. Organizational Technology Ladders: Remote Work and Generative AI Adoption [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Gregor Schubert
**Date:** 2026-08-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11626
**Summary:** Using U.S. job-posting data and an instrumental-variables strategy based on predicted differences in labor-market pressure to offer remote work, the author tests whether firms' earlier adoption of remote-work technology during COVID-19 shaped their later uptake of generative AI. A 10 percentage point increase in 2021-2022 remote hiring raises the share of job postings mentioning generative AI in 2023-2024 by 0.4 points across firms and 0.7 points across occupations within firms, with the effect concentrated in firms whose remote adoption shifted hiring toward technical and managerial capabilities. Firms that later imposed return-to-office mandates show an even larger response, consistent with an "organizational technology ladder" in which one technology's adoption builds the capital that lowers the cost of adopting the next, a mechanism relevant to predicting which firms diffuse AI fastest.

## 2. Equilibrium Pricing in Oligopolistic Data Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Bhaskar Ray Chaudhury, Jugal Garg, Eklavya Sharma, Jiaxin Song
**Date:** 2026-08-14
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14018
**Summary:** The authors model competition among strategic data sellers facing budget-constrained buyers such as machine-learning firms purchasing training data, and ask whether a stable pricing equilibrium exists. They prove that data's non-rivalry breaks the usual competitive-equilibrium guarantee: an exact Nash equilibrium in uniform prices need not exist, and even a 1.363-approximate one can fail to exist, but allowing sellers to use piecewise-linear convex pricing restores a 2-approximate equilibrium, with simulations converging faster than the worst-case bound. The result is a market-design foundation for how data markets that feed AI training should be priced, showing that the standard pricing tools economists use for rivalrous goods do not transfer cleanly to data.

## 3. Sequential LLM Release Facilitates Manipulation in Regulated Markets [Regulation & Policy]

**Authors:** Eilam Shapira, Moshe Tennenholtz, Roi Reichart
**Date:** 2026-01-16
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11496
**Summary:** Using GLEE, an independently collected benchmark of 587,000 strategic decisions by 13 LLMs across 1,320 bargaining, negotiation and persuasion configurations, the authors treat each new model release as an expansion of the strategy set available to AI agents mediating markets, and study how releases reshape equilibrium payoffs across more than 50,000 release comparisons. They find many releases move counterparties' payoffs in opposite directions, and identify a "Poisoned Apple effect" in which a released model that no one adopts in equilibrium still shifts payoffs asymmetrically and alters the market design a regulator faces, accounting for roughly three in ten opposing payoff shifts and amplified by technology restrictions. The finding gives regulators concrete, data-backed evidence that simply expanding the menu of available AI agents can facilitate manipulation even absent adoption, a governance problem specific to how AI models are released into markets.

## 4. Keep, Customize, or Exit: Default Design and Token Pricing in LLM Reasoning Services [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Ahmet Bugra Gundogan, Yigit Turkmen, Melih Bastopcu
**Date:** 2026-08-13
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13315
**Summary:** The authors model an LLM reasoning-service provider that sets a per-token price and a default reasoning-token allocation, while users can accept the default, customize their allocation, or exit, formalizing the interaction as a Stackelberg game and deriving the user's optimal customized allocation in closed form. They characterize the provider's optimal default through a three-regime rule and show defaults only affect the allocation actually used when users place value on avoiding the hassle of customizing; experiments on two open-weight reasoning models across five math and science benchmarks support the underlying accuracy-token tradeoff. The paper gives a market-design account of a pricing lever, the reasoning-token default, that LLM providers are already using and shows how it interacts with price competition and user heterogeneity.

## 5. A Stackelberg Game Framework with Drainability Guardrails for Pricing and Scaling in Multi-Tenant GPU Cloud Platforms [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Junji Yan, Asrin Efe Yorulmaz, Hanchen Zhou, Tamer Başar
**Date:** 2026-04-18; revised 2026-08-15
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16802
**Summary:** The authors formulate GPU cloud pricing and capacity-scaling as a large-population Stackelberg game in which price endogenously shapes tenant demand, deriving a closed-form equilibrium demand map for the resulting congestion dynamics. They uncover a structural failure mode in which delay-insensitive workloads sustain a residual demand floor that makes backlog undrainable under bounded price and capacity, and derive a computable "drainability guardrail" condition under which the platform provably converges to a unique operating point. The result is a market-structure account of how compute providers must jointly set price and capacity to avoid a chronically congested GPU market, directly relevant to understanding pricing power and scarcity in AI compute markets.
