# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-20

## 1. AI in Search Reduces Publisher Referrals Without Improving User Experience: Experimental Evidence [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Stephanie T. Wang, Jeffrey Gleason, Yakov Bart, Christo Wilson, Danaé Metaxa
**Date:** 2026-08-18
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18352
**Summary:** The authors run a preregistered field experiment (N=1,100) on Google Search, randomly varying exposure to AI Overviews and AI Mode to estimate their causal effect on user behavior and on the click-through traffic that search sends to publishers. Removing the AI-generated answers restores click-throughs to publisher sites, while an AI Mode-only experience further cuts referral traffic and leaves users with less trust in the results, so the AI features are not compensating publishers with a better user experience in exchange for lost clicks. The finding gives economists causal evidence on how generative AI integration into a dominant platform redistributes traffic and revenue away from the content producers that platform depends on.

## 2. The Impact of Generative AI on Collaborative Open-Source Software Development: Evidence from GitHub Copilot [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Fangchen Song, Ashish Agarwal, Wen Wen
**Date:** first posted 2024-10-02; revised 2026-08-13
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02091
**Summary:** Using GitHub's proprietary Copilot usage data merged with public open-source project records, the authors estimate that Copilot adoption raises project-level code contributions by 5.9%, driven by both more contributors and larger individual contributions, particularly among peripheral developers. The gain comes with an offsetting cost: an 8% rise in coordination time and more code discussion, yet the net effect on the timely merging of contributions remains positive. The paper documents a concrete productivity-versus-coordination tradeoff from AI-tool diffusion inside a real, large-scale collaborative production setting.

## 3. Automation, Income Incidence, and Capital Accumulation in Incomplete Markets [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Erhan Bayraktar
**Date:** first posted 2026-05-06; revised 2026-07-31
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05127
**Summary:** The paper builds an incomplete-markets, heterogeneous-household structural model in which an automating sector trades off productivity gains against adoption costs, and solves jointly for competitive factor prices, aggregate capital, and the stationary distribution of income, consumption, and wealth. It shows that whether automation raises or lowers aggregate consumption and capital depends on the incidence scenario: concentrated ownership, adverse reskilling, and rapid capital obsolescence produce losses relative to no automation, while broader ownership and stronger complementarity produce aggregate gains. A calibration using U.S. evidence on AI adoption, investment, and labor-income pass-through places the current economy near the boundary between the favorable and adverse scenarios, offering a quantitative welfare framework for separating AI's aggregate gains from its distributional incidence.

## 4. Training AI For When Humans Will Use It [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Kevin A. Bryan, Joshua S. Gans
**Date:** 2026-08-12
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12538
**Summary:** The authors formalize the economic value of an AI system as depending on how its predictions are combined with human verification and other inputs in a "composite experiment," and derive via a geometric argument how this changes optimal model training away from simply maximizing unconditional prediction accuracy. They show optimal training can be discontinuous in economic variables and characterize how heterogeneous users, or a monopoly model trainer serving many downstream users, shape the resulting training choice. The framework gives a market-structure account of why AI providers may rationally train models to be less accurate on average than they could be, once downstream use is priced in.

## 5. Global Automation Atlas [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Prashant Garg, Tommaso Crosta, Jasmin Baier
**Date:** first posted 2026-05-16; revised 2026-07-21
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17086
**Summary:** The authors use a large language model to classify 18,797 work tasks across 124 economies by automation exposure, labor margin, technology channel, and AI materiality, conditioning feasible automation on both task content and country-level conditions rather than assuming fixed cross-country exposure scores. The exposed task share ranges from 3.3% to 61.6% and rises with income but stays heterogeneous within income groups, with lower-income economies concentrated in labor-substituting automation while richer economies see more labor-augmenting AI use, and women are disproportionately employed in the occupations most exposed to substitution. The atlas gives economists a country-conditioned measure of AI and automation exposure that correlates with observed ChatGPT use and firm-reported adoption, useful for cross-country diffusion and growth analysis.
