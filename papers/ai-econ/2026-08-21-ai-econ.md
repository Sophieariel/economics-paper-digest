# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-21

## 1. Open at the Edge, Captured at the Center: llama.cpp and the Political Economy of Local AI Inference [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Woohyeuk Lee, Hanlin Li, David Gray Widder
**Date:** 2026-08-19
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19001
**Summary:** Drawing on 7,681 merged pull requests from March 2023 to March 2026 plus repository discussions and corporate statements, the authors trace how llama.cpp, the dominant open-source tool for running AI models locally, evolved as a piece of market infrastructure. They find that local inference broadens who can run open-weight models while simultaneously concentrating control over the infrastructure that makes that execution possible, with power shifting toward hardware vendors, model distributors and core maintainers — a dynamic crystallized by Hugging Face's 2026 acquisition of the project. The paper argues that competition policy aimed at "open" AI needs to look past model releases to the inference-tooling layer where capture is actually occurring.

## 2. Power Couple? AI Growth and Renewable Energy Investment [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Luyi Gui, Tinglong Dai
**Date:** first posted 2026-03-08; revised 2026-08-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26678
**Summary:** The authors build a game-theoretic model of how AI developers' capability-scaling decisions interact with renewable-energy investment, distinguishing cases where the market value of added AI capability grows faster than the energy needed to produce it ("market-led scaling") from cases where energy costs grow faster ("resource-led scaling"). In the market-led regime they identify an "adaptation trap": rising climate damages make frontier AI more valuable for adaptation, which sustains fossil-fuel use even as renewable capacity expands; in the resource-led regime, renewable investment instead lowers the cost of further AI capability and can support fully renewable-powered AI growth. A calibrated case study shows both regimes are empirically plausible, implying that whether the AI buildout decarbonizes or entrenches fossil generation depends on how fast AI's economic value scales relative to its energy appetite.

## 3. Growth Without Us: Machine Consumers, Corporate Circularity, and the Decoupling of GDP from Humanity after AGI [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Sahil Sharma
**Date:** 2026-08-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20231
**Summary:** The paper models a hypothetical post-AGI economy in which corporations own populations of AI and robotic agents that both produce and consume compute, energy and maintenance, and shows this closed inter-corporate system is formally equivalent to a classical von Neumann expanding economy with a well-defined, maximal growth rate once human demographic constraints no longer bind output. It proves a "golden-rule decoupling theorem": at maximal growth the interest rate equals the growth rate, so any positive human consumption from wealth causes the human ownership share of the corporate network to decay exponentially, severing GDP growth from human welfare. The result reframes AI policy debates around ownership rules rather than employment, arguing that whether humans retain a stake in a fully automated economy is determined by law, not by growth itself.
