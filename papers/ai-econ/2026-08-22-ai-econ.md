# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-22

## 1. AI in Search Reduces Publisher Referrals Without Improving User Experience: Experimental Evidence [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Stephanie T. Wang, Jeffrey Gleason, Yakov Bart, Christo Wilson, Danaé Metaxa
**Date:** 2026-08-18
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18352
**Summary:** The authors run a preregistered field experiment (N=1,100) on Google Search that randomly varies exposure to AI Overviews and AI Mode to estimate their causal effect on user click-through behavior and publisher referral traffic. They find that removing AI-generated answers increases clicks to publisher sites, while an AI Mode-only search experience further cuts click-through rates and also lowers users' trust in and satisfaction with the results. The findings quantify a concrete market-structure cost of generative search integration: platforms capture more attention while the publishers who supply the underlying content lose referral traffic without any offsetting gain in user experience.

## 2. The Impact of Generative AI on Collaborative Open-Source Software Development: Evidence from GitHub Copilot [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Fangchen Song, Ashish Agarwal, Wen Wen
**Date:** first posted 2024-10-02; revised 2026-08-13
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02091
**Summary:** Using GitHub's proprietary Copilot usage data merged with public open-source project records, the authors estimate Copilot's causal effect on collaborative software output and coordination costs. They find Copilot use raises project-level code contributions by 5.9%, driven by more contributors and larger individual contributions, but also increases coordination time by 8% as more code discussion is needed, with peripheral developers gaining less and facing larger coordination costs than core developers. The net effect on timely code merges is still positive, illustrating a broader productivity-versus-coordination tradeoff relevant to how generative AI tools reshape decentralized collaborative production.

## 3. Power Couple? AI Growth and Renewable Energy Investment [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Luyi Gui, Tinglong Dai
**Date:** first posted 2026-03-08; revised 2026-08-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26678
**Summary:** The authors build a game-theoretic model of interacting AI developers and renewable-energy investors to characterize when AI scaling and clean-energy investment reinforce or work against each other. They identify two regimes: under "market-led scaling," where the value of added AI capability grows faster than the energy it requires, developers keep pushing capability even on fossil power, producing an "adaptation trap" that renewable investment cannot fully unwind; under "resource-led scaling," rising energy costs bind AI growth and renewable investment both lowers the cost of capability and cuts emissions, an "adaptation pathway." A calibrated case study shows both regimes are empirically plausible, implying that decarbonizing AI depends on renewable capacity keeping pace with compute demand growth rather than following automatically from AI-driven investment.

## 4. Growth Without Us: Machine Consumers, Corporate Circularity, and the Decoupling of GDP from Humanity after AGI [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Sahil Sharma
**Date:** 2026-08-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20231
**Summary:** The paper models a post-AGI economy as a closed, corporation-owned network of AI and robotic agents that both produce and consume compute, energy and maintenance, formalized as a von Neumann expanding economy in which zero human consumption does not stop growth but instead maximizes it. It derives a "golden-rule decoupling theorem" showing that at maximal growth the interest rate equals the growth rate, so any positive human consumption out of wealth causes the human ownership share of the machine economy to decay exponentially at that same rate, severing GDP growth from human welfare. The analysis reduces welfare in such an economy to a single state variable, the human ownership share, and argues that once labor income is obsolete, ownership policy becomes the sole lever for preserving human welfare.

## 5. Stranded credentials: how a skill-signaling market absorbed generative AI [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Song Yao
**Date:** 2026-08-17
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17111
**Summary:** Using a panel of 444,698 participations on Kaggle from 2010-2026, the author audits how well competition medals earned under two evaluation formats continued to predict future performance as generative AI became available to competitors. Medals earned close to the AI transition kept most of their predictive value in both formats, but the stock of older medals from a discontinued evaluation format lost 82% of its informativeness, with roughly half to three-quarters of that decline attributable to the format's earlier institutional exit rather than to AI itself. The paper shows that credentialing markets can substantially preserve signaling value through an AI shock, but only if institutions actively replenish and reweight credentials, since official lifetime-count tiers discard 13-16% of the information a recency-weighted index would capture.
