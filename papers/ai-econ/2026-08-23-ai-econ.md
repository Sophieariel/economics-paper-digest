# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-23

## 1. Growth Without Us: Machine Consumers, Corporate Circularity, and the Decoupling of GDP from Humanity after AGI [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Sahil Sharma
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20231
**Summary:** The author builds a formal post-AGI growth model in which corporations own populations of AI and robotic agents that both produce and consume energy, compute and upgrades, showing that a closed economy with zero human consumption is not degenerate but corresponds to the classical von Neumann expanding economy, whose growth rate is well-defined and maximal precisely because all output is reinvested. The paper's central "golden-rule decoupling" result is that at the growth-maximizing path the interest rate equals the growth rate, so any positive human consumption out of wealth causes humans' ownership share of the machine economy to decay exponentially, separating aggregate GDP from human welfare even as output surges. It concludes that in such a regime employment policy becomes irrelevant to human living standards and ownership policy is the binding lever, offering a growth-theoretic account of what full automation implies for the GDP-welfare link.

## 2. Power Couple? AI Growth and Renewable Energy Investment [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Luyi Gui, Tinglong Dai
**Date:** first posted 2026-03-08; revised 2026-08-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26678
**Summary:** The authors model the strategic interaction between an AI developer's capability-scaling decisions and investment in renewable energy capacity as a game, asking when rising AI compute demand crowds in clean power versus deepens reliance on fossil generation. The outcome turns on whether the market value of greater AI capability grows faster than the energy needed to reach it: when value outpaces energy costs the developer keeps pushing the capability frontier regardless of power source, and rising climate damages create an "adaptation trap" that reinforces demand for frontier AI despite emissions, whereas when energy costs grow faster than value, renewable investment cuts the cost of further capability gains and can support an all-renewable "adaptation pathway." A calibrated case study shows both regimes are empirically plausible, implying that whether AI growth helps or hurts decarbonization depends on renewable capacity keeping pace with compute-demand growth.
