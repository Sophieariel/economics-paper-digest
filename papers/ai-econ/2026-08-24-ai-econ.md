# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-24

## 1. AI in Search Reduces Publisher Referrals Without Improving User Experience: Experimental Evidence [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Stephanie T. Wang, Jeffrey Gleason, Yakov Bart, Christo Wilson, Danaé Metaxa
**Date:** 2026-08-18
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18352
**Summary:** The authors ran a preregistered field experiment with 1,100 participants on Google Search, randomly varying exposure to AI Overviews and AI Mode to estimate their causal effect on user behavior and on traffic to publisher websites. Removing the AI-generated summaries raised click-through rates to publishers, while an AI Mode-only experience further suppressed click-throughs and eroded users' trust in the information they found. The results are direct experimental evidence that embedding generative AI in the dominant search platform reallocates online attention away from the publishers who supply its underlying content, with real economic consequences for the outlets that sustain the information ecosystem.

## 2. Power Couple? AI Growth and Renewable Energy Investment [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Luyi Gui, Tinglong Dai
**Date:** first posted 2026-03-08; revised 2026-08-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26678
**Summary:** The authors build a game-theoretic model in which an AI developer's capability-scaling decisions interact with a renewable-energy investor's capacity choices, to ask whether AI growth accelerates or undermines the shift to clean power. When the market value of added AI capability grows at least as fast as the energy it requires, developers keep pushing toward frontier capability even on fossil power, and rising climate damages strengthen that incentive by making AI more valuable for adaptation, an "adaptation trap" in which renewable investment enables further AI growth without displacing fossil generation. When energy needs instead grow faster than capability value, energy costs bind, and a calibrated case study shows renewable investment can instead lower the cost of further capability gains while cutting emissions, an "adaptation pathway" that the authors argue is empirically plausible alongside the trap.

## 3. Growth Without Us: Machine Consumers, Corporate Circularity, and the Decoupling of GDP from Humanity after AGI [Productivity & Growth]

**Author:** Sahil Sharma
**Date:** 2026-08-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20231
**Summary:** The paper models a hypothetical post-AGI economy in which corporations own populations of AI and robotic agents that both produce and consume energy, compute and upgrades among themselves, and asks whether such an economy can keep growing without any human demand. Framing this as a von Neumann expanding economy, the author shows growth becomes bounded by fabrication throughput and energy capture rather than human population growth, permitting growth rates one to two orders of magnitude higher than today's, while a "golden-rule decoupling" result shows that at the maximal growth rate any human consumption out of wealth causes humans' ownership share of the machine economy to decay exponentially. The policy conclusion is that in such a regime employment policy becomes irrelevant and the human share of aggregate output is set entirely by ownership rules, making ownership policy the central lever for whether growth translates into human welfare.
