# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-27

## 1. AI in Search Reduces Publisher Referrals Without Improving User Experience: Experimental Evidence [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Stephanie T. Wang, Jeffrey Gleason, Yakov Bart, Christo Wilson, Danaé Metaxa
**Date:** 2026-08-18
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18352
**Summary:** Using a preregistered field experiment with 1,100 participants on Google Search, the authors randomly vary exposure to Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode to estimate the causal effect of AI-generated search summaries on user behavior, perceptions, and the click-through traffic that reaches publishers. They find that removing AI Overviews and AI Mode increases click-throughs to publisher sites, while an AI-Mode-only search experience lowers click-through rates and also reduces users' trust in and satisfaction with the information Google surfaces. The paper offers experimental, rather than purely observational, evidence that embedding generative AI into search reallocates attention away from the publishers whose content trains and feeds these systems, with direct implications for platform competition policy and the economics of the online content ecosystem.

## 2. The Reverse Big Push: Generative AI and Self-Fulfilling Automation [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Soumen Banerjee, Jianguo Wang
**Date:** 2026-08-26
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25602
**Summary:** The authors build a general-equilibrium model of a local service economy in which generative AI relocates automation's fixed training cost onto a model provider, while a firm that instead retains human workers carries continuing payroll that itself supports demand across sectors. Because payroll income feeds back into aggregate demand, human-augmented and automated production can become strategic complements, generating multiple self-fulfilling equilibria — a high-demand, human-staffed equilibrium and a low-demand, automated one — with the realized path pinned down by coordination dynamics rather than technology alone. Calibrating to professional-services revenue-to-payroll ratios, the paper shows how highly autonomous AI uses can tip an economy into the low-employment region and derives a policy mix (an adoption wedge plus a temporary bridge) for cases where that low-demand state is self-sustaining.

## 3. Algorithmic Recommendations and Strategic Pricing [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Shota Ichihashi, Alex Smolin
**Date:** first posted 2023-09-21; revised 2026-08-26
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12122
**Summary:** The authors study a market in which an algorithm recommends at most one product to a buyer based on privately known product values and sellers' posted prices, without being able to charge transfers or compel purchases, asking what welfare and pricing outcomes such algorithmic mediation can support. They show that across objectives ranging from total surplus to buyer surplus, algorithmic recommendations can implement the same welfare outcomes as a full direct-revelation mechanism, though the resulting pricing, competitive intensity, composition of trade, and market segmentation differ from a no-recommendation benchmark. The results speak directly to the market design of AI shopping assistants and similar tools that mediate product discovery, showing how a recommendation rule can substitute for missing pricing instruments in shaping competition.

## 4. Power Couple? AI Growth and Renewable Energy Investment [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Luyi Gui, Tinglong Dai
**Date:** first posted 2026-03-08; revised 2026-08-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26678
**Summary:** The authors build a game-theoretic model of the interaction between AI developers' capability-scaling decisions and renewable-energy investment, where the outcome hinges on whether the market value of added AI capability grows faster or slower than the energy needed to produce it. Under "market-led scaling," developers keep pushing toward frontier capability even on fossil power, and rising climate damages can strengthen this incentive by making AI more valuable for adaptation — an "adaptation trap" where renewables enable further AI growth without displacing fossil use; under "resource-led scaling," energy costs bind more tightly, so renewable investment can let rising AI value justify enough clean-capacity expansion to power AI growth entirely with renewables. A calibrated case study shows both regimes are empirically plausible, implying that whether AI and clean energy behave as genuine complements depends on the relative growth rates of compute demand and renewable capacity.

## 5. Growth Without Us: Machine Consumers, Corporate Circularity, and the Decoupling of GDP from Humanity after AGI [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Sahil Sharma
**Date:** 2026-08-20
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20231
**Summary:** The paper models a hypothetical post-AGI economy in which corporations own populations of AI and robotic agents that both produce and consume energy, compute, and maintenance, forming a closed inter-corporate economy structurally equivalent to the classical von Neumann expanding-economy model with a well-defined, reinvestment-driven maximal growth rate. Because economic agents can be manufactured rather than born, the binding constraint on growth shifts from human demographic reproduction to fabrication throughput and energy capture, permitting growth rates orders of magnitude above historical norms while output and human welfare separate completely, collapsing welfare to a single state variable — the human ownership share of the corporate network. A "golden-rule decoupling theorem" shows that at maximal growth the interest rate equals the growth rate, so any positive human consumption out of wealth causes that ownership share to decay exponentially, leading the author to conclude that in such an economy ownership policy, not employment policy, determines whether humans retain any claim on aggregate output.
