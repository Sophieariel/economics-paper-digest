# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-28

Only two papers cleared the relevance bar today; the digest is shorter than usual rather than padded to five.

## 1. Trusting AI in Competitive Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jussi Keppo, Yuze Li, Gerry Tsoukalas, Nuo Yuan
**Date:** 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26539
**Summary:** In a laboratory experiment with 273 sellers competing across 91 three-seller oligopoly markets over 30 rounds, the authors randomize the presence of AI pricing recommendations and the gender composition of the market to identify how trust in algorithmic advice shapes pricing outcomes. They find that AI recommendations raise prices by 29% and profits by 39% in female-only markets but have no significant effect in male-only or mixed-gender markets, with a hidden Markov model showing that profitable rounds predict rising adherence to the AI in female-only markets and declining adherence otherwise. The results imply that algorithmic pricing tools can generate composition-dependent market power, so platform governance and antitrust oversight of AI pricing recommenders needs to account for the human side of adoption, not just the algorithm itself.

## 2. AI agents in Algorithmic Electricity Markets: On the Emergence of Tacit Collusion [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jakub Seredyński, Georgios Tsaousoglou
**Date:** 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26896
**Summary:** The authors model electricity market bidding as a repeated game with imperfect public monitoring and simulate participants' strategies using multi-agent reinforcement learning, then apply a multi-dimensional set of criteria beyond simple profit-versus-Nash-equilibrium comparisons to test whether independently learning algorithms can sustain supra-competitive outcomes. They find that in oligopolistic, repeated-interaction electricity markets, learning agents do learn to sustain tacitly collusive outcomes even without being instructed to coordinate. The paper extends the empirical algorithmic-collusion literature from retail and platform pricing to a critical infrastructure market, with direct implications for how energy regulators should monitor AI-driven bidding for competition-law violations.
