# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-30

## 1. AI agents in Algorithmic Electricity Markets: On the Emergence of Tacit Collusion [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jakub Seredyński, Georgios Tsaousoglou
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26896
**Summary:** The authors model electricity-market bidding as a repeated game with imperfect public monitoring and train multi-agent reinforcement-learning bidders to represent an increasingly algorithmic, oligopolistic electricity market. Using a multi-dimensional set of collusion indicators that goes beyond simple profit-versus-Nash-equilibrium comparisons, they find that independently learning agents can sustain supra-competitive prices consistent with tacit collusion even though none were designed or instructed to coordinate. The results suggest autonomous learning bidders pose a structural competition-policy risk in already-oligopolistic markets such as electricity, since collusive-like outcomes can emerge purely from independent learning rather than explicit agreement.

## 2. Trusting AI in Competitive Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jussi Keppo, Yuze Li, Gerry Tsoukalas, Nuo Yuan
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26539
**Summary:** In a laboratory experiment with 273 sellers competing in 91 three-seller oligopoly markets over 30 rounds, the authors randomly vary whether sellers receive AI pricing recommendations and the market's gender composition (female-only, male-only, mixed) to identify how trust in algorithmic advice evolves and feeds back into prices. They find AI recommendations raise prices by 29% and profits by 39% in female-only markets but have no significant effect in male-only or mixed markets, with a hidden Markov model showing profitable rounds predict rising adherence to the AI's advice in female-only markets and declining adherence elsewhere — a composition-specific pattern of learned trust and self-serving attribution that runs counter to prior findings on gender and AI trust. The results imply algorithmic pricing tools can generate different competitive outcomes depending on who uses them, so competition and platform regulators should scrutinize the human side of algorithmic pricing tools, not just the algorithms themselves.
