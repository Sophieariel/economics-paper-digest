# AI Economics Papers — 2026-08-31

## 1. The Global Transition – The Impact of Demographics and AI on Economic Power [Productivity & Growth]

**Authors:** Seth G. Benzell, Laurence J. Kotlikoff, Victor Yifan Ye
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-17
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35618
**Summary:** Benzell, Kotlikoff, and Ye build a 17-region dynamic life-cycle general-equilibrium model spanning three labor-skill groups, internationally mobile capital, and firms' automation-adoption decisions to project how demographic change and AI diffusion will reshape global economic power through 2100. Updating to 2024 UN demographic projections — especially sharply lower Chinese fertility — the model implies China's share of global GDP falls from 25.6% to 14.9% by 2100 while the US share rises from 11.2% to 14.4%, with faster or transformative AI adoption further reinforcing US dominance. The results show that projected shifts in global economic hegemony are highly sensitive to both demographic assumptions and the pace of AI-driven automation, making AI diffusion a first-order variable in long-run growth and geopolitical-power forecasting.

## 2. Trusting AI in Competitive Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jussi Keppo, Yuze Li, Gerry Tsoukalas, Nuo Yuan
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26539
**Summary:** Keppo, Li, Tsoukalas, and Yuan run a laboratory experiment with 273 sellers competing in 91 three-seller oligopoly markets over 30 rounds, randomizing whether sellers receive AI pricing recommendations and varying the market's gender composition to identify how trust in algorithmic advice evolves under genuine competitive feedback. They find the recommendations raise prices by 29% and profits by 39% in female-only markets but have no significant effect in male-only or mixed-gender markets, with a hidden Markov model showing that profitable rounds predict rising adherence to AI advice in female-only markets and declining adherence otherwise — a composition-specific learned-trust dynamic that reverses prior findings on gender and AI reliance. The results imply that algorithmic pricing tools can generate real anticompetitive price effects, and that platform governance and regulatory oversight of AI-assisted pricing needs to account for who is using the algorithm, not just how the algorithm is built.

## 3. AI agents in Algorithmic Electricity Markets: On the Emergence of Tacit Collusion [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jakub Seredyński, Georgios Tsaousoglou
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26896
**Summary:** Seredyński and Tsaousoglou model electricity-market bidding as a repeated game with imperfect public monitoring and let multi-agent reinforcement-learning agents — standing in for market participants who increasingly delegate bidding to autonomous algorithms — learn strategies in oligopolistic wholesale-electricity settings. Using a multi-dimensional set of collusion criteria that goes beyond simple profit comparisons against Nash-equilibrium benchmarks, they find that independently learning agents can sustain supra-competitive, tacitly collusive outcomes even though none of them was ever instructed to coordinate. The finding is a warning for market design and antitrust enforcement in increasingly algorithmic wholesale-electricity markets, since collusive-looking price patterns can emerge purely from decentralized learning rather than explicit coordination.
