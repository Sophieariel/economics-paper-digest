# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-01

## 1. Impact of AI Search Summaries on Website Traffic: Evidence from Google AI Overviews and Wikipedia [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Mehrzad Khosravi, Hema Yoganarasimhan
**Date:** first posted 2026-02-05; revised 2026-08-26
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18455
**Summary:** Using Google AI Overviews' staggered geographic rollout and Wikipedia's multilingual article structure, the authors run a difference-in-differences design comparing external-search referrals to English Wikipedia articles against referrals to the same articles in German and French, where AI Overviews were not yet default. They find that default availability of AI Overviews reduced English-language search traffic to Wikipedia by roughly 5% relative to the non-AIO comparison languages. The results offer causal evidence that AI-generated search summaries reallocate attention away from the publishers whose content underlies them, with direct implications for content monetization, search platform design, and competition policy.

## 2. Trusting AI in Competitive Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jussi Keppo, Yuze Li, Gerry Tsoukalas, Nuo Yuan
**Date:** 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26539
**Summary:** In a laboratory experiment with 273 sellers competing across 91 three-seller oligopoly markets over 30 rounds, the authors vary whether sellers receive AI pricing recommendations and the gender composition of each market to study how trust in algorithmic advice evolves when its payoff depends on rivals' responses. They find that AI recommendations raise prices by 29% and profits by 39% in female-only markets but have no significant effect in male-only or mixed-gender markets, with a hidden Markov model showing that profitable rounds build adherence to the AI in female-only markets while eroding it elsewhere — a composition-specific learned-trust dynamic that reverses prior findings on gender and AI reliance. The results suggest that platform governance and regulatory oversight of algorithmic pricing tools need to account for the human side of adoption, not just the algorithm itself.

## 3. The Price of Intelligence: A Quality-Adjusted Price Index for AI Services [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Louis Yiven Zhu
**Date:** 2026-08-30
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29843
**Summary:** The author builds quality-adjusted price indices for the AI inference market from 21,024 posted-price observations across 3,208 models and 86 providers, linking them to a latent quality index estimated from 4,605 benchmark scores in place of conventional hedonic product characteristics. Standard matched-model methods (as used by statistical agencies for software) show inference prices falling only 0.10 log points a year, but the quality-adjusted index falls at 0.73 log points a year, meaning 87% of the true price decline is invisible to current measurement; counted per completed task, buyer-side prices have stopped falling at all because reasoning models consume tokens faster than token prices drop. The mismeasurement has direct consequences for how economists gauge competition, concentration, and productivity growth in the AI services market.

## 4. AI agents in Algorithmic Electricity Markets: On the Emergence of Tacit Collusion [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jakub Seredyński, Georgios Tsaousoglou
**Date:** 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26896
**Summary:** The authors model strategic bidding in oligopolistic electricity markets as a repeated game with imperfect public monitoring, using multi-agent reinforcement learning to simulate participants who adopt autonomous learning-based bidding algorithms, and propose a multi-dimensional set of criteria beyond simple profit-versus-Nash-equilibrium comparisons to detect tacit collusion. They find that independently learning agents can converge on supra-competitive outcomes consistent with tacit collusion even though none were instructed to coordinate. The results warn that as electricity markets become algorithmic, market structure and monitoring rules designed for human bidders may no longer prevent collusive-like outcomes, a concern directly relevant to competition policy in AI-mediated markets more broadly.
