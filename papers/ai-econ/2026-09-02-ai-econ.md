# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-02

## 1. The Price of Intelligence: A Quality-Adjusted Price Index for AI Services [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Louis Yiven Zhu
**Date:** 2026-08-30
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29843
**Summary:** The author builds quality-adjusted price indices for the AI inference market from a panel of over 21,000 posted-price observations across 3,208 models and 86 providers, linking prices to a latent quality index estimated from benchmark performance. Measured the conventional way, inference prices fell only 0.10 log points a year, but the quality-adjusted index shows a 0.73 log-point annual decline — meaning 87% of the true price drop is invisible to standard matched-model methods — while per-completed-task prices for buyers have actually stopped falling as reasoning models consume more tokens. The findings have direct implications for how economists measure competition, concentration, and productivity in the fast-moving AI services market, and a pre-registered robustness check shows the quality ladder is not an artifact of benchmark contamination.

## 2. AI Agents in Algorithmic Electricity Markets: On the Emergence of Tacit Collusion [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jakub Seredyński, Georgios Tsaousoglou
**Date:** 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26896
**Summary:** The authors model electricity market bidding as a repeated game with imperfect public monitoring and use multi-agent reinforcement learning to simulate how autonomous, learning-based bidding agents behave in oligopolistic wholesale electricity markets. They propose a multi-dimensional set of criteria, beyond simple profit-versus-Nash-equilibrium comparisons, to test whether the resulting bidding behavior amounts to tacit collusion. Their simulations show that supra-competitive, collusion-like outcomes can emerge even though the agents were never instructed to collude, raising concrete competition-policy concerns as electricity markets increasingly rely on algorithmic participants.

## 3. Algorithmic Collusion by Large Language Models [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Sara Fish, Yannai A. Gonczarowski, Ran I. Shorrer
**Date:** first posted 2024-03-31; revised 2026-08-31
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806
**Summary:** The authors run controlled experiments with LLM-based pricing agents competing in oligopoly settings and find that these agents autonomously and quickly converge to supracompetitive prices and profits without being told to collude. They show that small, seemingly innocuous variations in the natural-language prompts given to the agents substantially change the degree of supracompetitive pricing, and develop new behavioral-analysis techniques that trace this to price-war concerns embedded in the agents' reasoning; the results extend to auction settings as well. The paper argues these dynamics pose distinct challenges for any future regulation of LLM-based and AI-based pricing agents more broadly.

## 4. Trusting AI in Competitive Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Jussi Keppo, Yuze Li, Gerry Tsoukalas, Nuo Yuan
**Date:** 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26539
**Summary:** In a laboratory experiment with 273 sellers competing across 91 three-seller oligopoly markets, the authors test how access to AI pricing recommendations affects prices and profits, varying the gender composition of each market. They find AI advice raises prices by 29% and profits by 39% in female-only markets but has no significant effect in male-only or mixed-gender markets, and a hidden Markov model shows that in female-only markets profitable rounds predict growing trust in the AI's advice while the opposite pattern holds elsewhere. The results imply that platform governance and antitrust oversight of AI pricing tools need to account for how the human side of a market shapes algorithmic pricing outcomes, not just the algorithm itself.

## 5. Impact of AI Search Summaries on Website Traffic: Evidence from Google AI Overviews and Wikipedia [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Mehrzad Khosravi, Hema Yoganarasimhan
**Date:** first posted 2026-02-05; revised 2026-08-26
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18455
**Summary:** Using Google AI Overviews' staggered geographic rollout together with Wikipedia's multilingual structure, the authors run a difference-in-differences design comparing external search referrals to English-language Wikipedia articles against referrals to the same articles in German and French. They find that the default availability of AI Overviews reduced English-language search traffic to Wikipedia by 5.45% and by 4.82% relative to the German and French comparisons, respectively. The results provide causal evidence that AI-generated search summaries reallocate user attention away from the publishers underlying their content, with direct implications for content monetization, search-platform competition, and policy.
