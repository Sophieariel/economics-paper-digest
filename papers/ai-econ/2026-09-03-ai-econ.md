# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-03

## 1. Impact of AI Search Summaries on Website Traffic: Evidence from Google AI Overviews and Wikipedia [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Mehrzad Khosravi, Hema Yoganarasimhan
**Date:** first posted 2026-02-05; revised 2026-08-26
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18455
**Summary:** Exploiting the staggered geographic rollout of Google's AI Overviews and the multilingual structure of Wikipedia, the authors run a difference-in-differences comparison of external search referrals to English-language Wikipedia articles against referrals to the same articles in German and French, which serve as controls. They find that default availability of AI Overviews reduced English-language search traffic to Wikipedia by about 5.45% relative to the German comparison and 4.82% relative to the French comparison. The design delivers causal, market-level evidence that embedding generative AI summaries in search reallocates traffic away from the publishers and reference sites that supply the underlying content, with direct implications for platform competition and the economics of online content provision.

## 2. Algorithmic Collusion by Large Language Models [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Sara Fish, Yannai A. Gonczarowski, Ran I. Shorrer
**Date:** first posted 2024-03-31; revised 2026-08-31
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806
**Summary:** The authors run controlled pricing experiments with LLM-based agents competing in oligopoly settings to test whether such agents autonomously reach collusive outcomes without ever being instructed to collude. They find that LLM pricing agents quickly and consistently settle on supracompetitive prices and profits, that seemingly minor variation in prompt wording substantially changes how collusive the pricing becomes, and that a learned aversion to price wars helps explain the pattern; the results carry over to auction settings as well. Because firms are already beginning to deploy LLM-based pricing tools, the paper's behavioral-analysis methods and findings speak directly to the antitrust and regulatory challenges posed by AI-driven pricing.

## 3. The Price of Intelligence: A Quality-Adjusted Price Index for AI Services [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Louis Yiven Zhu
**Date:** 2026-08-30
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29843
**Summary:** The paper constructs quality-adjusted price indices for the AI inference market from a panel of more than 21,000 posted-price observations spanning 3,208 models and 86 providers, linking prices to a latent quality index estimated from benchmark performance in place of the conventional product characteristics used in hedonic price indices. This lets the author track how fast the effective price of AI intelligence has fallen once quality gains are netted out, and shows that the answer differs sharply from what posted sticker prices alone would suggest. The exercise is a first attempt at a proper cost-of-living-style deflator for AI services, a measurement tool that matters for tracking productivity, competition and market structure in the fast-moving market for AI inference.

## 4. Who Delegates to AI? Evidence from 53,000 Agent Configurations [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Hyeongjae Lee, Jihyang Cheon, Lanu Kim
**Date:** 2026-08-19
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20425
**Summary:** Rather than measuring which occupational tasks AI could technically perform, the authors build an Agentic Adoption Index that captures whether workers have actually committed tasks to AI by building them into a workflow, using semantic similarity between roughly 53,000 shared agent configurations from a skills marketplace and O*NET task descriptions. They find that the occupations where this delegated adoption concentrates differ sharply from those flagged as most exposed by prior technical-feasibility measures, and that adoption peaks among bachelor's-level, upper-middle-wage occupations rather than the most educated or highest-paid ones. Technical feasibility alone does not explain the shortfall among highly educated occupations, suggesting that task codifiability and professional discretion shape the real pace and pattern of AI diffusion in the workplace.

## 5. Competitive Market Behavior of LLMs [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Pawel Struski, Jakub Swistak, Inez Okulska, Przemyslaw Biecek
**Date:** 2026-09-02
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02580
**Summary:** The authors replace human subjects with LLM agents in a classic double-auction market experiment to test whether a market mechanism designed for people still delivers an efficient allocation when the traders are AI agents. They find that markets populated by LLM agents converge more slowly, or fail to converge at all, to competitive equilibrium compared with markets of human traders, and that trading behavior varies substantially across model families and between buyer and seller roles. A textual analysis of the agents' reasoning traces further shows that the decision to execute a trade, rather than keep incrementally adjusting prices, is associated with a shift from strategic reasoning toward urgency, raising concrete concerns about deploying LLM agents as participants in real market mechanisms.
