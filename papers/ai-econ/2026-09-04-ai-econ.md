# AI Economics Papers — 2026-09-04

## 1. Individualized Algorithmic Advice as a Strategic Signal on Competitive Markets [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Tobias R. Rebholz, Maxwell Uphoff, Christian H. R. Bernges, Florian Scholten
**Date:** first posted 2025-11-12; revised 2026-08-31
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09454
**Summary:** In a controlled Cournot-competition experiment with 129 participants, the authors vary whether an algorithmic advisor recommends the competitive-equilibrium quantity or a collusively biased, below-equilibrium quantity. They find that biased advice reliably steers players toward sustained underproduction and supracompetitive profits, and that participants converge faster to individualized advice than to advice framed as common knowledge, suggesting perceived ownership over algorithmic guidance sharpens its influence. The results show experimentally that even non-communicating algorithmic advisors can function as a coordination device for tacit collusion, reinforcing the case for regulatory scrutiny of AI-based recommendation and pricing tools.

## 2. AI Worsens Climate Change, Integrated Assessment Shows [Trade & Welfare]

**Authors:** Huiying Ye, Richard S. J. Tol, Fangzhi Wang
**Date:** 2026-08-25
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24670
**Summary:** The authors extend the DICE integrated assessment model to separately track how AI investment affects emissions, output and climate damages, distinguishing a modest "ICT-like" AI growth path from an economy-transforming "Industrial-Revolution-like" one. Calibrating to current evidence, they find AI development is a net emitter under today's low abatement effort, adding 0.1°C to 2100 warming under the ICT scenario and 0.8°C under the IR scenario, with climate damages offsetting roughly one-fifth to one-quarter of AI's economic gains. Because the investment trade-off between AI and abatement is driven mainly by AI's economic upside rather than its direct emissions footprint, the paper argues that climate mitigation and AI investment are complements rather than substitutes for policy.

## 3. Does Rank Still Matter? Position Bias When AI Agents Shop on Our Behalf [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Davood Wadi, Yu Ma
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-24; revised 2026-08-28
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22697
**Summary:** Randomizing the order of 100 hotel listings across 5,000 sessions with four different LLM shopping agents, the authors compare how position affects what AI agents inspect and buy relative to documented human field behavior. They find that AI agents examine listings far more exhaustively than humans and essentially never decline to purchase, and that while position still affects inspection it does so weakly and non-monotonically, with middle-ranked listings least likely to be inspected rather than the bottom of the page. Because every model converges on the same undominated option regardless of position, the results imply that as AI shopping agents intermediate consumer purchases, the economic value of top-of-page placement — a mainstay of digital advertising and platform revenue — may erode in favor of underlying product attributes.

## 4. Sophistication in GenAI Use: Field Evidence from a Large Firm [Adoption & Diffusion]

**Authors:** Nicholas J. Hallman, Zachary T. Kowaleski, Anu Puvvada, Jaime J. Schmidt
**Date:** 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27364
**Summary:** Drawing on 713,564 proprietary prompts and responses from nearly 4,000 back-office employees at a large firm over eight months, the authors construct a measure of how sophisticated employees' generative-AI use is and track it across seniority levels, functional areas and AI training exposure. They find that more senior employees use GenAI more sophisticatedly, consistent with domain expertise complementing the technology, that sophistication is concentrated in strategy, digital-innovation and project-management functions, and that neither time nor formal training produces lasting gains in sophistication. The findings suggest that within-firm diffusion of generative AI is bottlenecked less by access than by workers' ability to use it well, a friction that formal training alone does not resolve.

## 5. One Capability or Many? Testing the Economic Validity of Frontier AI Evaluation [Firms & Competition]

**Authors:** Louis Yiven Zhu
**Date:** 2026-08-29
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29420
**Summary:** Using a hash-pinned snapshot of 421 frontier-model configurations scored on twelve benchmarks, four of them explicitly economic, the author fits a latent-variable model to test whether AI leaderboards' economic benchmarks measure a distinct capability or simply reflect general progress over time. A single general factor explains most of the common variance and tracks release date more than compute, and under a pre-registered dimensionality rule the economic benchmarks fail to form a separate factor, though a held-out prediction test shows they still add incremental information beyond the general trend. Because leaderboards used by firms and regulators to gauge AI's economic readiness are shown to be substantially a calendar-time trend, the paper cautions that small gaps between contemporaneous models are being read as capability differences when they mostly reflect release timing.
