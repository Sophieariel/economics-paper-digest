## 1. Same Occupations, Different Clocks: Separating Remote Work from Generative AI for Labor-Market Entrants [Employment]
**Authors:** Joshua Mask
**Date:** July 7, 2026
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7075479
**Abstract:** This paper asks whether the weak labor market for recent college graduates reflects generative AI, remote work, or both — a question that is hard to answer because the occupations most reachable by large language models substantially overlap with the occupations that shifted to remote work during the pandemic. Using CPS data on labor-market entrants, the author fixes each entering cohort's occupational exposure at its pre-pandemic occupation mix and dates each treatment to its own shock — remote work to 2020, generative AI to ChatGPT's November 2022 release — in order to separate the two "clocks" that have moved together in the data. A one-standard-deviation increase in remote-work exposure reduces entry-level hourly wages by 1.9%, a penalty already present among cohorts who entered before ChatGPT existed, indicating it is not an AI effect. By contrast, no detectable wage effect of generative AI exposure emerges for entrants. College employment in AI-exposed occupations does fall starting with the graduating class of 2022, but the decline is absorbed once demographic controls are added, consistent with reallocation across occupations rather than a net employment loss for labor-market entrants attributable to AI.

## 2. Can LLMs Hire Fairly? Racial Bias in Resume Screening [Hiring]
**Authors:** Zhenyu Gao, Wenxi Jiang, Yutong Yan
**Date:** June 27, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28978
**Abstract:** The authors audit fourteen leading large language models for racial and gender discrimination in hiring using the paired-resume correspondence methodology of Kline, Rose, and Walters (2022), testing 24,024 paired job postings across all fourteen models. The sole 2023-vintage model in the sample reproduces the pro-White callback gap long documented in field experiments on labor-market discrimination (+2.12 percentage points, significant at the 1% level). Every model released in 2024 or later, however, shows either a null racial gap or a statistically significant reversal favoring Black applicants, reaching -3.01 percentage points. Gender-bias patterns follow a similar generational trajectory. The results document a marked directional shift in algorithmic hiring discrimination across successive LLM releases, with direct implications for firms increasingly relying on LLM-based resume screening and for regulators evaluating disparate-impact risk in algorithmic hiring tools.

## 3. Let Me Check on You: Job Quality Under AI and Human Oversight [Hiring]
**Authors:** Milena Nikolova
**Date:** July 2026 (IZA DP No. 18782)
**Source:** https://docs.iza.org/dp18782.pdf
**Abstract:** This paper presents causal evidence on how AI-based workplace safety and monitoring systems affect workers' perceptions of job quality. The author conducted a preregistered vignette study with 2,172 Dutch respondents, who evaluated otherwise identical workplaces under three oversight regimes: human supervisors only, AI-only monitoring, or a hybrid AI-human system. Both AI-only and hybrid systems reduced perceived job satisfaction, work meaningfulness, and the perceived social value of the job relative to human-only supervision; contrary to expectation, the hybrid model did not mitigate these negative effects. Workers also viewed AI oversight as less respectful of privacy and dignity, even though they rated it as comparably effective to human supervision. Wages showed little variation across the three conditions. The findings suggest that workplace AI's effects on job quality operate through non-monetary channels — dignity, meaning, and perceived fairness — that lie outside the safety and productivity gains usually emphasized in the algorithmic-management literature, with implications for policy debates over AI monitoring and worker surveillance.

## 4. Adoption and Impact of Command-Line AI Coding Agents: A Study of Microsoft's Early 2026 Rollout of Claude Code and GitHub Copilot CLI [Firm Adoption]
**Authors:** Emerson Murphy-Hill, Jenna Butler, Alexandra Savelieva
**Date:** July 1, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01418
**Abstract:** The authors study the internal, early-2026 rollout of command-line AI coding agents (Claude Code and GitHub Copilot CLI) to tens of thousands of software engineers at Microsoft, using telemetry on tool adoption, coding activity, and output. Peer networks — rather than demographic characteristics — were the strongest predictor of initial adoption, and prior coding activity predicted sustained tool use more than demographic factors did. Engineers who adopted the command-line agents merged roughly 24% more pull requests than they would have otherwise, and this productivity gain persisted over a four-month observation window rather than fading, suggesting the effect reflects durable workflow change rather than a novelty effect. The study offers large-scale, firm-level field evidence on how generative AI tools diffuse through an organization's internal social network and translate into measurable knowledge-worker output, complementing smaller randomized-trial evidence on AI coding assistants with an at-scale natural rollout.

## 5. Algorithmic Monocultures in Hiring [Hiring]
**Authors:** Rishi Bommasani, Sarah H. Bana, Kathleen A. Creel, Dan Jurafsky, Percy Liang
**Date:** May 26, 2026 (arXiv preprint; accepted to ACM FAccT 2026) — *outside the strict 30-day window; included as the closest available high-quality substitute after an extensive search turned up no closer match on algorithmic hiring/management.*
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27371
**Abstract:** Employers increasingly screen job applicants using algorithms built by a small number of third-party vendors, raising the possibility of "algorithmic monoculture," in which reliance on a shared, deterministic algorithm causes the same individuals — and disproportionately the same demographic groups — to be rejected across many employers and postings. Using a dataset of roughly 3 million applicants submitting about 4 million applications, all screened by algorithms from a single vendor, the authors find that 14.74% and 25.87% of applications from Asian and Black applicants, respectively, are submitted to positions where the algorithm's screening decisions meet U.S. employment-discrimination standards for adverse impact. Exploiting the deterministic nature of the algorithm, the authors simulate the outcomes applicants would receive had they applied to other positions scored by the same vendor, finding that about 4% of applicants who apply to ten or more positions are rejected by all of them at a rate higher than chance alone would predict — evidence of correlated, homogeneous rejection driven by the shared algorithm rather than by applicant quality. The results imply that job seekers screened by dominant hiring-algorithm vendors may need to apply unusually broadly to ensure a human ultimately reviews their application, with direct relevance for antidiscrimination policy toward concentrated algorithmic hiring markets.
