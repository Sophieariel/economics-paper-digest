All five are dated June or July 2026, within the last 30 days: four IZA Discussion Papers and one DIW Berlin Discussion Paper.

## 1. Evidence on the Adoption of Artificial Intelligence: The Role of Skills Shortage [Firm Adoption]
**Authors:** Paolo Carioli, Dirk Czarnitzki, Gastón P. Fernández
**Date:** July 2026 (IZA DP No. 18793)
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18793/evidence-on-the-adoption-of-artificial-intelligence-the-role-of-skills-shortage
**Abstract:** This paper asks whether firms facing skills shortages turn to AI as a way of coping with unmet workforce needs. Using German survey data from the Mannheim Innovation Panel, the authors measure skills shortages through firms' unfilled job vacancies across different skill categories and use an instrumental-variables strategy to address the endogeneity of skills shortages with respect to AI adoption. They find a positive and significant effect of skills shortage on AI adoption, on the breadth of AI methods a firm uses, and on the breadth of the areas in which AI is applied within the firm. Shortages specifically in academic-qualification and STEM-related occupations are the strongest drivers of firm-level AI adoption. Rather than emphasizing displacement, the results suggest that — at least in this setting — AI functions as a complementary tool that firms deploy to overcome binding labor-market constraints, with implications for how policymakers think about the relationship between tight labor markets and technology adoption.

## 2. Has AI Widened Employment Gaps? Tracking Early-Career Employment by Occupational Exposure in Norway [Employment]
**Authors:** Øystein Hernaes, Andreas Ravndal Kostøl
**Date:** June 2026 (IZA DP No. 18767)
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18767/has-ai-widened-employment-gaps-tracking-early-career-employment-by-occupational-exposure-in-norway
**Abstract:** Using Norwegian private-sector administrative employment records through February 2026, the authors track whether occupations more exposed to generative AI have diverged from less-exposed occupations in employment growth since ChatGPT's release in October 2022. They find that employment in the most AI-exposed occupations has grown by 0.1% since October 2022, compared with 0.3% in the least-exposed occupations — a small but directionally negative gap rather than a large divergence. When the sample is narrowed to young, early-career workers and occupations are split into complete exposure quintiles, the estimated relative decline for the most-exposed group is larger in magnitude but statistically imprecise, so the authors stop short of concluding that AI has clearly widened early-career employment gaps. The authors maintain a public dashboard (kiindeksen.no) that updates the underlying employment-by-exposure comparisons monthly as new administrative data arrive, offering a live, continuously updated complement to point-in-time causal estimates elsewhere in the literature.

## 3. Becoming The Man Without Qualities? Deskilling in the Age of Artificial Intelligence [Skills & Tasks]
**Authors:** Michele Cantarella, Giuseppe Molinari, Chiara Strozzi
**Date:** June 2026 (IZA DP No. 18751)
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18751/becoming-the-man-without-qualities-deskilling-in-the-age-of-artificial-intelligence
**Abstract:** This paper studies how AI is reshaping the skill content of occupations using longitudinal O*NET data spanning 2011–2025, combined with two original measures of AI exposure — one tracking progress on generative-AI capability benchmarks and one tracking the intensity of AI-related scientific attention directed at each occupation's tasks. The authors document a dual and seemingly contradictory pattern: within occupations, greater AI exposure is associated with higher proficiency requirements for the capabilities that remain in demand, even as, at the occupation level, more AI-exposed jobs show a compression in the overall breadth of capabilities required of workers. In other words, AI exposure appears to be narrowing the range of skills an exposed job calls for while simultaneously raising the bar on the skills that survive this narrowing. The authors interpret this as evidence that AI is catalyzing occupational restructuring toward more specialized, narrower capability profiles rather than uniformly broadening or eliminating worker skill requirements — a distinct pattern from simple task automation or upskilling narratives.

## 4. When AI Does the Work: Does Attribution Shape Meaning and Effort? [Hiring]
**Authors:** Milena Nikolova, Viliana Milanova, Feicheng Wang
**Date:** July 2026 (IZA DP No. 18784)
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18784/when-ai-does-the-work-does-attribution-shape-meaning-and-effort
**Abstract:** The authors conduct a preregistered survey experiment in the United States (N = 1,511) and the Netherlands (N = 2,117) to isolate how attribution — rather than the quality of the output itself — shapes how workers value creative work. Participants evaluated identical public-health campaign slogans that were randomly attributed to either an AI system or a human professional. AI attribution modestly reduced participants' perceived meaningfulness of the task and made them 13% less likely to volunteer a slogan of their own, even though the underlying content was identical across conditions. The findings indicate that AI's effects on work are not confined to productivity or task substitution: simply knowing that AI produced a piece of work changes how much people value the human contribution to it and how willing they are to exert voluntary effort on similar tasks, with implications for how organizations communicate and manage AI-assisted work products internally.

## 5. AI Adoption by Human Experts: Evidence from Primary Care Physicians [Skills & Tasks]
**Authors:** Shan Huang, Renke Schmacker, Hannes Ullrich
**Date:** 2026 (DIW Berlin Discussion Paper No. 2168)
**Source:** https://www.diw.de/de/diw_01.c.1011533.de/publikationen/diskussionspapiere/2026_2168/ai_adoption_by_human_experts__evidence_from_primary_care_physicians.html
**Abstract:** AI can raise productivity by extracting information from rich data, yet little is known about how experts weigh AI-generated signals against established decision-support tools. The authors conduct a nationwide survey experiment with 372 Danish primary care physicians (21.5% of all clinics), who make diagnostic and treatment decisions on urinary-tract-infection vignettes before and after receiving a diagnostic signal. Holding accuracy constant, they randomize between subjects whether the signal appears as an AI prediction or as a commonly used dipstick test result. Physicians update their beliefs 41% less in response to the AI signal than to the dipstick signal, consistent with AI skepticism, and roughly one-third of physicians ignore the AI tool entirely; linked administrative data show these non-adopters resemble adopters on most observables, including clinical practice and prescribing patterns, except for lower baseline technology use at their clinics. When physicians do use the AI tool, they ignore asymmetry in informativeness between positive and negative signals and, when shown both the AI signal and a redundant second signal, exhibit correlation neglect. These frictions in information processing lead to increased antibiotic prescribing when the AI signal is present. The findings highlight the importance of training and information design for AI implementation among skilled experts, with implications for how AI decision-support tools are rolled out across professional and personnel-economics settings more broadly.
