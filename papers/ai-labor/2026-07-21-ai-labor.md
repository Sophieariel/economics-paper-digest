## 1. Experimental Evidence on the Learning Impact of Generative AI [Skills & Tasks]
**Authors:** Zara Contractor, Germán Reyes
**Date:** July 2026 (IZA DP No. 18792; arXiv:2607.08849)
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18792/experimental-evidence-on-the-learning-impact-of-generative-ai
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a randomized experiment examining how generative AI influences undergraduate learning. Participants studied an unfamiliar topic and wrote analytical essays either with or without AI access, then took unaided assessments immediately and one week later. AI access produced immediate test score improvements of 0.27 standard deviations on knowledge assessments, with gains persisting after one week. Essay quality showed minimal changes during AI-assisted writing but improved in style and relevance one week later, when students write unaided. Students who used AI for concept explanation (augmentation) experienced larger delayed improvements than those using it for text generation (automation). The researchers identify two mechanisms behind these effects: students redirected effort from drafting toward reading and information-seeking, and reported heightened learning enjoyment. The findings speak directly to how generative AI reshapes human-capital accumulation and skill formation — a central input into workers' longer-run labor market outcomes — and to design choices (augmentation- versus automation-oriented AI use) that determine whether AI exposure helps or erodes durable learning.

## 2. When the Scaffold Stays On: AI, Practice Style, and Screening in Elite Skill Formation [Skills & Tasks]
**Authors:** Song Yao
**Date:** June 2026, revised July 5, 2026 (arXiv:2606.06253)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06253
**Abstract:** This paper studies how generative AI affects skill development depending on how it is used during practice, distinguishing users who let AI substitute for deliberate practice from those who use it to augment learning. Using data from competitive programming platforms (ICPC, IOI, and Codeforces), the author investigates whether AI-prohibited evaluation — the credentialing "gate" used at competition boundaries — can separate these two usage modes among participants who trained with AI assistance available. The central finding is that AI-style practice is compatible with reaching frontier skill levels; the risk of skill erosion is concentrated specifically in the substitute-mode of AI use; and that mode is separable from augmentation-mode use by screening mechanisms of the kind already standard at credential boundaries (e.g., closed-book exams, in-person competitions). The results speak to a core personnel-economics and human-capital question raised by generative AI: how employers, credentialing bodies, and educational institutions can design screening and evaluation systems that reward durable skill formation rather than AI-dependent performance, with direct implications for hiring and promotion criteria in AI-exposed, high-skill occupations.

## 3. Cheaper AI, More Informality? A Dual Labor Market Model for Developing Economies [Policy]
**Authors:** Gabriel Montes-Rojas, Fernando Toledo, Juan Manuel Rodríguez Repeti
**Date:** July 16, 2026 (arXiv:2607.15381)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15381
**Abstract:** The paper examines the labor market consequences of falling AI costs in developing economies, where a large share of employment is informal. The authors build and calibrate a dual labor market model incorporating imported AI technology, country risk, and a formal/informal employment margin, and show that the labor market consequences of cheaper AI hinge critically on the elasticity of substitution between AI capital and formal-sector workers. When AI substitutes for formal workers, cheaper AI weakens formal labor demand and pushes displaced workers into the informal sector, which absorbs them as an employment buffer. When AI instead complements formal labor, cheaper AI expands formal employment and amplifies output, wages, investment, and capital accumulation. The model is calibrated to an economy with substantial informality to quantify these divergent scenarios. The findings have direct implications for labor market policy in emerging and developing economies: whether governments should worry about AI-driven informalization, or instead facilitate AI adoption to expand formal employment, depends on empirically pinning down which regime — substitution or complementarity — characterizes a given economy's exposed sectors.

## 4. The Algorithmic-Human Manager: AI, Apps, and Workers in the Indian Gig Economy [Hiring]
**Authors:** Omir Kumar, Krishnan Narayanan
**Date:** June 18, 2026 (arXiv:2606.19975)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19975
**Abstract:** This study examines how AI-driven algorithmic management systems shape blue-collar gig work in India, using a mixed-methods approach that combines semi-structured interviews with 16 gig workers and 21 platform, policy, and advocacy stakeholders. The authors document a central paradox: algorithmic management systems improve work accessibility and operational efficiency for platforms, but simultaneously generate significant concerns around fairness, transparency, and worker dignity. The systems are found to be opaque by design, to produce inequitable outcomes across workers, and to be structured in ways that do not reward additional labor with proportionate pay. Based on these findings, the authors propose an "Algorithmic-Human Manager" governance framework that combines algorithmic efficiency with meaningful human oversight, intended to inform platform companies, policymakers, and labor advocates designing more equitable algorithmic-management and personnel-economics policy for gig and platform economies across India and the broader Global South.

## 5. An AI Adoption Gap Among French Firms? [Firm Adoption]
**Authors:** Sarah Mouabbi, Sebastian Stumpner
**Date:** May 28, 2026 (Banque de France Eco Notepad, Post No. 451) — outside the strict 30-day window; flagged per note above
**Source:** https://www.banque-france.fr/en/publications-and-statistics/publications/ai-adoption-gap-among-french-firms
**Abstract:** Using firm-level data from the ECB's Survey on Access to Finance of Enterprises (SAFE) covering twelve euro-area countries, the authors document that French firms report substantially lower AI adoption than their eurozone peers: 23% of French firms report moderate-to-significant AI use versus 39% across the euro area as a whole. The gap holds across firm sizes and sectors and is especially pronounced among larger enterprises. Rather than reflecting skills shortages, French firms more frequently cite data-privacy and ethical concerns as barriers to adoption, and report lower planned AI-investment shares (7.2% versus 9.1% for euro-area peers), suggesting the adoption gap may widen going forward. AI-adopting firms across the sample anticipate higher employment growth in both manufacturing and services, though the authors find that anticipated productivity gains appear directed toward profits rather than passed through to consumer prices or wages. The results bear on labor-demand and labor-policy questions: differential national AI-adoption trajectories driven by regulatory and cultural factors (rather than skills constraints) may translate into diverging employment growth and wage-bargaining outcomes across otherwise-similar European labor markets.
