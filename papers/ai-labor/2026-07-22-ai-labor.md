## 1. Helping People Choose Careers in the Age of AI [Skills & Tasks]
**Authors:** Jennifer L. Steele, Isabella Cruz
**Date:** July 16, 2026 (arXiv:2607.15506)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15506
**Abstract:** How should people choose careers when artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming the nature of work? The authors first compare six recent projections of occupational exposure to task automation with AI, examining their methods and assumptions, and then propose a new empirical model of occupational AI exposure based on 2025 query data from Anthropic and OpenAI. They find marked heterogeneity in model predictions, though models published since 2020 show positive relationships among AI exposure, salaries, and occupational complexity. To reduce uncertainty due to heterogeneous assumptions about task-automation potential, they average the projections from five models, including their own. Using these averages, they report on likely tradeoffs between salaries and AI exposure across interest categories, O*NET Job Zones, and job fields. Jobs in healthcare practice show the strongest balance of higher pay with lower AI exposure. Among jobs making high use of Anthropic's Claude, those that use it as a complement rather than a substitute for human work are modestly higher-paying, though whether this pattern holds will depend on usage norms adopted in each field.

## 2. Tracking AI-Related Job Loss Using Unemployment Insurance Claims Data in California [Employment]
**Authors:** Till von Wachter, Ben Hyman, and the California Policy Lab research team (UCLA site)
**Date:** June 25, 2026
**Source:** https://capolicylab.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Tracking-AI-Related-Job-Loss-Using-Unemployment-Insurance-Claims-Data-in-California.pdf
**Abstract:** This report introduces the California AI-Unemployment Tracker (CAIT), which links confidential unemployment insurance (UI) claims data from the California Employment Development Department with two established occupational AI-exposure measures (covering both potential task displacement and current AI implementation rates) to monitor labor-market disruption in near-real time. Analyzing UI claims from November 2022 through May 2026, the authors find no statewide surge in layoffs among workers in highly AI-exposed occupations. However, targeted disruptions are apparent among specific populations: UI claims rose for college-educated workers in AI-exposed fields, particularly those holding advanced degrees, and claims from AI-exposed occupations in the San Francisco Bay Area show a sharp, sustained increase not mirrored statewide. The financial-activities and information sectors — where AI adoption has been fastest — show accelerating payroll declines in 2026, though the information sector had returned to baseline by late 2025. The authors frame the tracker as an early-warning system rather than a definitive causal impact estimate, cautioning that post-pandemic industry shifts and broader macroeconomic conditions may also drive the observed patterns. The tool will post monthly updates with demographic breakdowns by region, industry, education, age, race/ethnicity, and gender, offering a template for other states to monitor AI-driven labor-market disruption using administrative data.

## 3. What Capital After Labor? Forecasting the Talent ROI Transition in the Human-AI Era [Firm Adoption]
**Authors:** Kwan Soo Shin, In Seok Kang
**Date:** June 18, 2026, four days outside the strict 30-day window (arXiv:2606.19846) — flagged per note above
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19846
**Abstract:** AI augmentation breaks the accounting link between labor time and productive contribution, yet firms continue to evaluate talent through time-based overhead bundles. This paper develops a forecasting framework for the transition from time-based talent accounting to output-based talent ROI in the human-AI era, and uses Korea's staged 52-hour workweek mandate as an empirical early-warning case for the pre-transition overhead-pressure regime. In a DART panel of 365 listed Korean firms (2,281 firm-year observations), the SG&A-to-revenue ratio rose from 18.26% in 2018 to 20.06% in 2020, corrected mildly in 2021–2022, and peaked at 20.10% in 2024. Two-way fixed-effects estimates (+1.56 pp), pooled event-study estimates (+4.21 pp at t=+3), and Callaway–Sant'Anna doubly-robust staggered difference-in-differences estimates (+4.51 pp at t=+4) converge on a positive overhead-pressure signature, with a 2015–2017 backward-extension sample used to rule out pre-existing trend confounds. The authors read this as the first empirically documented signature of a regime in which time-based talent accounting still dominates while AI augmentation and labor-time compression jointly raise firm overhead, and forecast that firms which shift to output-based talent-ROI accounting will outperform time-based peers by 1.5–2.0 percentage points in firm-level TFP growth by 2032 — with direct implications for how firms organize work, compensate labor, and structure hiring as AI reshapes the relationship between hours worked and value produced.
