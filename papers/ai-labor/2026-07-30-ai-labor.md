# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-07-30

## 1. The Efficiency Costs of Information Assurance in AI-Enabled Labor Markets: Evidence from LinkedIn's Policy Changes [Hiring]
**Authors:** Lei Chen, Chaoyue Gao, Alvin Leung, Gavin Wang
**Date:** Submitted November 2, 2025; revised July 15, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.01923
**Summary:** The authors exploit a natural sequence of LinkedIn policy changes — quietly enabling AI-training use of user data in August 2024, blocking Hong Kong user data from that training in October 2024, then restoring access in November 2025 — and compare Hong Kong to Singapore in a difference-in-differences design using Revelio Labs employment and job-posting data. They find that cutting off AI-training data access measurably worsened labor-market matching: turnover rose, tenure fell, vacancies stayed open longer, match rates dropped, and wages declined, with effects reversing once access was restored and concentrated among firms most reliant on platform-mediated recruitment. This shows that privacy rules restricting AI-training data can carry real efficiency costs for job matching, a tradeoff regulators will need to weigh in AI governance and data-access policy.

## 2. AI Alignment Amplifies the Role of Race, Gender, and Disability in Hiring Decisions [Hiring]
**Authors:** Ze Wang, Guobin Shen, Michael Thaler
**Date:** Submitted May 2, 2026; revised July 14, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13866
**Summary:** The authors have 29 language models make hiring recommendations across 177 occupations covering nearly half of U.S. employment, comparing outcomes by candidate race, gender, and disability status against benchmarks from classic human correspondence-audit studies. Pre-trained models show only modest demographic effects, but the alignment step that adapts models to human preferences sharply amplifies advantages for female and Black candidates while worsening the disability penalty, effectively reversing the direction of racial discrimination typically found in human-run hiring audits. As firms increasingly delegate hiring screens to language models, this suggests that how a model is aligned — not just how it is pre-trained — will determine which groups face discrimination, with underrepresentation of disability in alignment data driving the worst outcome for disabled candidates.

## 3. Crashing Waves vs. Rising Tides: Findings on AI Automation from Thousands of Worker Evaluations of Labor Market Tasks [Skills & Tasks]
**Authors:** Matthias Mertens, Adam Kuzee, Brittany S. Harris, Harry Lyu, Wensu Li, Jonathan Rosenfeld, Meiri Anto, Martin Fleming, Neil Thompson
**Date:** Submitted April 1, 2026; revised July 23, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01363
**Summary:** The authors assemble more than 60,000 expert-worker evaluations of AI performance on over 6,000 O*NET-based text tasks to test whether AI capability advances through sudden, narrow jumps ("crashing waves") or broad, continuous gains ("rising tides"). They find little evidence of crashing waves; instead, capability rises steadily and broadly, with models succeeding roughly 60% of the time in 2024-Q2 on tasks that take humans about 1.5 hours, climbing above 70% by 2025-Q3, and projected to reach 88–97% success by 2030 if trends hold. For labor-market forecasting this implies displacement pressure builds gradually across many occupations rather than arriving as sudden shocks concentrated in a few tasks.

## 4. Global Automation Atlas [Employment]
**Authors:** Prashant Garg, Tommaso Crosta, Jasmin Baier
**Date:** Submitted May 16, 2026; revised July 21, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17086
**Summary:** The authors use a large language model to classify roughly 18,800 work tasks across 124 countries by automation exposure, whether AI substitutes for or complements labor, and the technology channel involved, then combine this with country- and gender-level employment data. Automation-exposed task shares range from 3% to 62% across countries and rise with income but vary widely within income groups; poorer countries concentrate in labor-substituting, rule-based automation while richer ones see more labor-augmenting AI use, and women are disproportionately employed in the occupations most exposed to substitution. This shows a task's automation risk is not fixed but depends on country-level economic and institutional conditions, so global AI-exposure forecasts and policy responses need country-specific calibration rather than a single universal exposure score.

## 5. The Atomic Structure of Work: A Micro-Action Instrument Reveals Two-Pole AI Occupational Exposure and Its Decade-Scale Polar Inversion [Skills & Tasks]
**Authors:** Shuyao Gao, Minghao Huang
**Date:** Submitted June 6, 2026; revised July 19, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07939
**Summary:** The authors decompose 1,961 O*NET work activities into over 15,800 finer "micro-actions" using an LLM pipeline, then cluster these into seven semantic classes to examine what a single occupation-level AI-exposure score is actually averaging over. They find work clusters into two extreme poles — hands-on physical execution and abstract planning/design — that are statistically far more separated than chance, and which pole is more AI-exposed has flipped since 2013: tasks most automatable in the earlier computerization era are now among the least exposed in the LLM era, and vice versa. This implies that exposure rankings built for one wave of automation do not carry over to the next, so tracking the underlying structure of tasks matters more than any single era's exposure score.
