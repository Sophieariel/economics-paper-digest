# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-07-31

## 1. Voice AI in Firms: A Natural Field Experiment on Automated Job Interviews [Hiring]
**Authors:** Brian Jabarian, Luca Henkel
**Date:** Submitted July 30, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28222
**Summary:** The authors ran a large-scale natural field experiment in which 70,000 job applicants were randomly assigned to be interviewed by human recruiters or by AI voice agents, with human recruiters making the final hiring decision in both arms. Applicants interviewed by AI were 12% more likely to receive job offers, and this translated into more job starts and higher retention with no decline in hired-worker productivity, because AI interviews were more structured and consistent while remaining responsive to individual applicants. This shows that automating the information-collection stage of hiring, rather than the decision itself, can improve hiring quality through standardization.

## 2. The Turing Valley: How AI Capabilities Shape Labor Income [Employment]
**Authors:** Enrique Ide, Eduard Talamàs
**Date:** Submitted August 29, 2024; revised July 30, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16443
**Summary:** The authors model production where knowledge is tacit and multidimensional, so problems cannot be assigned in advance to whichever of human or AI is best suited to solve them. They show that making AI more uniformly capable (less "jagged") can raise, rather than erode, labor's marginal product and wages, because a better AI screens out problems it can resolve on its own and concentrates scarce human time on the problems only humans can solve. This implies that the widely used human-versus-AI-only performance gap is a poor proxy for AI's true effect on labor demand, complicating simple narratives that stronger AI necessarily depresses wages.

## 3. When Do AI Gains Become Broadly Shareable? A Policy Threshold for AI-Driven Automation [Policy]
**Authors:** Aran Nayebi
**Date:** Submitted May 24, 2025; revised July 29, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18687
**Summary:** The author extends a standard task-automation growth model with an AI-capability parameter, calibrated to U.S. quantities, to identify which institutions determine whether AI-driven productivity gains become broadly shared income rather than concentrated rents. Capability growth alone is not decisive: moving from low to moderate public capture of AI rents (around 33%) can substitute for substantial further AI capability gains, while full nationalization yields diminishing returns and competition policy carries its own distributional tradeoffs. Framed as a policy stress test rather than a forecast, the paper gives policymakers concrete thresholds for when AI automation gains would require public intervention to be widely shared.
