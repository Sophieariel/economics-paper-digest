# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-08-04

## 1. Navigating the skill diversity frontier: How skill complexity explains worker resilience [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Mar Carpanelli, Jedrzej Duszynski, Fabian Stephany
**Date:** Submitted August 3, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02102
**Summary:** Using longitudinal LinkedIn data on 2.4 million U.S. workers and nearly 17,000 skills, the authors build a network-based measure of each worker's skill portfolio and decompose it into specialisation (depth) and diversity (breadth), then define a "diversity frontier" capturing the most breadth attainable at a given depth. They find specialisation predicts sorting into higher-wage occupations, while workers close to the diversity frontier are more likely to learn new skills, get promoted, and move into occupations with lower automation exposure than equally specialised peers with narrower portfolios. The results suggest resilience to AI-driven disruption comes from combining depth and breadth rather than either alone, offering a data-driven tool for identifying reskilling pathways.

## 2. Automation, Income Incidence, and Capital Accumulation in Incomplete Markets [Inequality]

**Authors:** Erhan Bayraktar
**Date:** Submitted May 6, 2026; revised July 31, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05127
**Summary:** This paper builds an incomplete-markets general equilibrium model in which a sector chooses how much to automate, trading off productivity gains against adoption costs, while heterogeneous households face uninsurable skill risk and save in capital. It shows automation's aggregate and distributional effects hinge on the "incidence scenario": high exposure combined with adverse reskilling, capital obsolescence, and concentrated ownership of automation rents lowers stationary consumption and capital, while strong productivity gains, low obsolescence, and broad ownership raise them instead. A diagnostic calibrated to current U.S. evidence on AI adoption, investment, and labor-income pass-through places the U.S. economy near the boundary between these two scenarios, underscoring that automation's welfare consequences are not predetermined but depend on how its rents and risks are distributed.

## 3. "Rich-Get-Richer"? Platform Attention and Earnings Inequality using Patreon Earnings Data [Inequality]

**Authors:** Ilan Strauss, Jangho Yang, Mariana Mazzucato
**Date:** Submitted September 30, 2025; revised July 15, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.26523
**Summary:** Using monthly Patreon earnings as a proxy for the attention creators capture across social platforms, the authors fit power-law tails to creators' earnings distributions and find a Pareto exponent near 2, closer to concentrated capital income than to typical labor income, consistent with a compounding "rich-get-richer" dynamic. They show that when recommendation algorithms tilt more attention toward top creators, the resulting gains come disproportionately at the expense of the creator "middle class," and that inequality across platforms is converging toward similarly heavy-tailed distributions as algorithmic curation displaces social-graph-driven discovery. The findings tie the design of platform recommendation algorithms directly to earnings concentration in creator labor markets, a mechanism increasingly relevant as generative and recommendation AI shape income opportunities in digital gig work.

## 4. The Scaling Paradox in Human-AI Collaboration [Firm Adoption]

**Authors:** Anyan Qi, Mengxin Wang
**Date:** Submitted August 1, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00818
**Summary:** The authors develop an analytical model of when improvements in AI capability ("scaling") actually translate into better joint human-AI performance, showing this depends critically on whether humans accurately perceive the AI's true capability. When humans over-perceive AI capability, a "scaling paradox" can emerge in which a more capable AI system reduces overall team performance and firm profit; under-perception still yields gains from scaling but at a much slower rate. The results imply firms benefit more from managing worker beliefs about AI (through cost internalization and perception-alignment policies) than from simply deploying larger models, reframing AI adoption as a behavioral and organizational problem rather than a purely technological one.
