# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-08-07

## 1. Does generative AI narrow education-based productivity gaps? Evidence from a randomized experiment [Inequality]

**Authors:** Guillermo Cruces, Diego Fernandez Meijide, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro Galvez, Maria Lombardi
**Date:** Submitted August 4, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04198
**Summary:** In a randomized online experiment with 1,174 adults aged 25-45, participants completed a workplace-style problem-solving task with or without a generative AI assistant, followed by an unassisted module. AI raised performance for everyone but the gains were much larger for less-educated participants, closing about three-quarters of the initial education-based performance gap (from 0.548 to 0.139 standard deviations), with chat logs showing lower-education users leaning more on direct AI help while higher-education users deployed it more effectively. The persistence results are more mixed: some of the improvement carried over once AI was removed for lower-education participants, though a sizable gap re-emerged, and follow-up gains required combining AI use with sustained personal effort rather than passive delegation — evidence that generative AI can compress skill-based productivity gaps in task execution without eliminating the underlying returns to human capital.

## 2. Nine Raters, One Index: Carrying LLM Disagreement into Labour-Market Estimates [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Golo Henseke, Rhys Davies, Alan Felstead, Duncan Gallie, Francis Green, Ying Zhou
**Date:** Submitted July 30, 2025; revised August 6, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22748
**Summary:** The authors test how much the choice of large language model matters for measuring occupational exposure to AI, having nine LLMs from three vendors score 1,100 task-occupation cells and weighting the results by worker-reported task importance from the British Skills and Employment Survey. Exposure rankings are fairly stable across models, but absolute exposure levels vary enormously — the share of British jobs scoring as highly exposed ranges from under 0.1% to 38% depending on which model is used — and downstream estimates built on these scores, such as a post-2022 wage gradient and effects on online vacancy postings, shift sign and significance depending on the rater, with no model identifying a clear generative-AI effect once teleworkability trends are separated out. The paper's contribution is a method for exposing and propagating this LLM-rater disagreement through empirical labor-market estimates, a caution for a fast-growing literature that treats single-model AI-exposure scores as settled measurement.
