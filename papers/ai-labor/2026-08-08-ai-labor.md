# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-08-08

## 1. Does generative AI narrow education-based productivity gaps? Evidence from a randomized experiment [Inequality]

**Authors:** Guillermo Cruces, Diego Fernandez Meijide, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro Galvez, Maria Lombardi
**Date:** Submitted August 4, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04198
**Summary:** The authors ran a randomized online experiment with 1,174 adults who completed a workplace-style problem-solving task with or without a generative AI assistant, followed by an unassisted module. AI assistance raised performance for everyone but disproportionately helped less-educated participants, closing about three-quarters of the initial productivity gap between higher- and lower-education workers, even though chat logs show higher-education participants used the tool more effectively. Part of the gain persists once AI is removed, though a sizable education gap re-emerges, suggesting generative AI compresses skill-based productivity differences in task execution without eliminating the underlying human-capital gap.

## 2. Rethinking Automation Risk: AI Applicability and Occupational Outcomes, 2019–24 [Employment]

**Authors:** Kristen Broady, Caleb Dunson, Anthony Barr
**Date:** Posted July 2026 (exact day not stated on source page)
**Source:** Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago Working Paper No. 2026-12, https://doi.org/10.21033/wp-2026-12
**Summary:** The authors combine established computerization-risk estimates with newer AI-applicability scores and match both to detailed US occupational employment and wage data spanning 2019–2024. They find occupations with high AI applicability saw overall employment and wage growth over the period, while occupations with higher automation-risk scores grew employment more slowly than low-risk occupations even as wages rose across every risk group. This pattern suggests that, through 2024, elevated AI exposure is showing up as occupational restructuring rather than the direct job losses that simple automation-risk measures would predict.

## 3. Nine Raters, One Index: Carrying LLM Disagreement into Labour-Market Estimates [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Golo Henseke, Rhys Davies, Alan Felstead, Duncan Gallie, Francis Green, Ying Zhou
**Date:** Submitted July 30, 2025; revised August 6, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22748
**Summary:** The authors test how much the choice of LLM "rater" matters for building AI task-exposure indices by scoring 1,100 task-occupation cells with nine different models and weighting them by worker-reported task importance from the British Skills and Employment Survey. Occupation rankings are far more stable across raters than absolute exposure levels, and when the indices are applied to real outcomes (post-2022 pay growth and online vacancy postings), the estimated generative-AI effect is sensitive to which rater is used, with the pooled estimate statistically indistinguishable from zero. The paper offers economists a procedure for exposing and propagating this LLM-rater disagreement rather than treating any single AI-exposure score as ground truth.

## Excluded, borderline

- **From Social Coding to Agentic Coding** (arXiv 2608.03585) — LLM multi-agent simulation of an open-source community, not an observed field or natural experiment on real workers.
- **The Main Barrier to AI Adoption in the Public Sector Is Lack of Training** (arXiv 2606.01517) — single-organization before/after case study with self-reported metrics, no comparison group.
- **AI-Increased Talent Retention Strategies** (arXiv 2607.19733) — conceptual literature-review framework paper, no empirical research design.
- **The Context Access Divide** (arXiv 2607.08495) — theoretical/probabilistic model of AI-mediated inequality, no empirical labor-market data.
- **Hiring Discrimination and the Task Content of Jobs** (arXiv 2604.01933) — large-scale resume audit with real causal design, but no AI/automation dimension in the treatment or outcomes.
- **Job Transformation, Specialization, and the Labor Market Effects of AI** (IZA DP 18565, Freund & Mann) — strong task-exposure/wage design, but posted April 2026, outside the 30-day window.
