# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-08-10

## 1. Better Together: Quantifying the Benefits of AI-Assisted Recruitment [Hiring]

**Authors:** Ada Aka, Emil Palikot, Ali Ansari, Nima Yazdani
**Date:** first posted 2025-07-08; revised 2026-08-07
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08029
**Summary:** Two field experiments at a recruitment platform test whether giving recruiters an LLM-generated "AI Interview Report," alongside or instead of resumes, changes hiring outcomes. Candidates shortlisted with AI interview information pass a blind final human interview at 17.5-20 percentage points higher rates than resume-only candidates, with the largest gains for junior applicants whose resumes carry the least signal, even though 75% of invited candidates never complete the AI interview. The results show AI-generated interview evidence adds real screening value exactly where conventional resume signals are weakest, but shifts part of the screening burden from firms onto applicants.

## 2. Does generative AI narrow education-based productivity gaps? Evidence from a randomized experiment [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Guillermo Cruces, Diego Fernandez Meijide, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro Galvez, Maria Lombardi
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-04
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04198
**Summary:** A randomized online experiment with 1,174 adults assigns a workplace-style problem-solving task with or without a generative AI assistant, then measures unassisted performance afterward. AI assistance raises performance for everyone but especially for less-educated participants, closing about three-quarters of the initial education-based performance gap (from 0.548 to 0.139 standard deviations), and the gains are not purely from delegating to the AI since assisted participants do not perform worse once the tool is removed. The findings suggest generative AI can substantially compress education-linked productivity gaps in task execution, even as underlying human-capital differences continue to shape unassisted performance and how effectively workers use the tool.

## 3. Nine Raters, One Index: Carrying LLM Disagreement into Labour-Market Estimates [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Golo Henseke, Rhys Davies, Alan Felstead, Duncan Gallie, Francis Green, Ying Zhou
**Date:** first posted 2025-07-30; revised 2026-08-06
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22748
**Summary:** The authors have nine LLMs from three vendors rate AI-exposure for 1,100 task-occupation cells, combine these ratings with worker-reported task importance from the British Skills and Employment Surveys, and test how much the choice of rating model changes downstream labor-market estimates. Exposure rankings are fairly stable across models (rank correlations 0.74-0.92), but exposure levels vary enormously, and this disagreement matters downstream: a negative post-2022 pay gradient survives across all nine raters, but the sign of a post-2022 online-vacancy gradient flips depending on the rater and how teleworkability is modeled, with neither application identifying a clear generative-AI effect. The paper offers a general procedure for exposing and propagating LLM-rater disagreement into empirical estimates, cautioning that AI-exposure measures built on a single LLM's judgments can be fragile inputs for policy-relevant conclusions.
