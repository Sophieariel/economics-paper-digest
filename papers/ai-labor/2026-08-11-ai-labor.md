# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-08-11

## 1. Better Together: Quantifying the Benefits of AI-Assisted Recruitment [Hiring]

**Authors:** Ada Aka, Emil Palikot, Ali Ansari, Nima Yazdani
**Date:** Submitted July 8, 2025; revised August 7, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08029
**Summary:** Using two field experiments at a recruitment platform, the authors show that giving recruiters LLM-generated structured "AI Interview Report" ratings — essentially AI-conducted screening interviews administered at scale — raises the rate at which shortlisted candidates pass a blind human final interview by roughly 18-20 percentage points relative to resume-only screening. The gains concentrate where resumes are least informative: adding the AI ratings raises predictive accuracy far more for junior candidates than for experienced ones, and the effect holds even though three-quarters of invited applicants never complete the AI interview, an attrition pattern that itself tracks job-search motivation rather than predicted performance. The findings indicate AI interviews add genuinely new information exactly where conventional hiring signals are weakest, effectively shifting part of the screening cost from firms onto applicants.

## 2. Does generative AI narrow education-based productivity gaps? Evidence from a randomized experiment [Inequality]

**Authors:** Guillermo Cruces, Diego Fernandez Meijide, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro Galvez, Maria Lombardi
**Date:** Submitted August 4, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04198
**Summary:** In a randomized online experiment with 1,174 adults completing a workplace-style problem-solving task with or without a generative AI assistant, the authors find that AI raises performance for everyone but disproportionately for less-educated participants, closing about three-quarters of the initial education-based performance gap. Chat logs show lower-education participants lean on the AI for more direct assistance while higher-education participants use it more strategically, yet the effect is not purely delegation: on a later unassisted module, the less-educated group retains part of its improvement, though a sizable gap re-emerges once the tool is removed. The results suggest generative AI can meaningfully compress observed productivity differences tied to education in the short run, even as underlying human-capital differences continue to shape how effectively workers exploit the tool.

## 3. Nine Raters, One Index: Carrying LLM Disagreement into Labour-Market Estimates [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Golo Henseke, Rhys Davies, Alan Felstead, Duncan Gallie, Francis Green, Ying Zhou
**Date:** Submitted July 30, 2025; revised August 6, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22748
**Summary:** The authors rate 1,100 task-occupation cells with nine different large language models from three vendors and combine these with worker-reported task importance from British skills surveys to build AI-exposure measures, finding that occupation rankings are fairly stable across models (pairwise rank correlations of 0.74-0.92) while the absolute share of jobs classified as highly exposed swings from under 0.1% to 38% depending purely on which model is used. When these measures are used to test for a post-2022 generative-AI effect on wages or online vacancy postings, the sign and significance of the estimated effect shifts across raters and specifications, and neither application robustly identifies a true AI effect once model-choice uncertainty is carried through. The paper's contribution is methodological: a procedure for exposing and propagating LLM-rater disagreement into downstream labor-market estimates, a caution for the growing literature that relies on a single LLM to measure AI task exposure.
