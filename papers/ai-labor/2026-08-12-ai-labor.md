# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-08-12

## 1. Better Together: Quantifying the Benefits of AI-Assisted Recruitment [Hiring]

**Authors:** Ada Aka, Emil Palikot, Ali Ansari, Nima Yazdani
**Date:** Submitted July 8, 2025; revised August 7, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08029
**Summary:** Across two field experiments on a recruitment platform, the authors test what happens when LLM-conducted structured interviews are added to a hiring pipeline alongside resumes. Candidates whose files include an AI Interview Report pass the (human, blind-to-condition) final interview at rates 17.5–20 percentage points higher than those screened on resumes alone, with the largest predictive gains for junior candidates whose resumes carry the least signal. The interviews shift screening costs onto applicants — three-quarters of invited candidates never complete one — but completion itself turns out to signal job-search motivation rather than interview skill, so the attrition is informative rather than merely a barrier.

## 2. Does generative AI narrow education-based productivity gaps? Evidence from a randomized experiment [Inequality]

**Authors:** Guillermo Cruces, Diego Fernandez Meijide, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro Galvez, Maria Lombardi
**Date:** Submitted August 4, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04198
**Summary:** In a randomized experiment with 1,174 adults completing a workplace-style problem-solving task with and without a generative AI assistant, the authors find AI raises performance for everyone but by more for less-educated participants, cutting the initial 0.548-SD education-based performance gap by roughly three-quarters. Chat logs show lower-education participants leaning on the AI for substantive help while higher-educated participants use it more efficiently, and the gains are not pure delegation: part of the improvement persists once the AI assistant is removed, especially when heavy AI use was paired with sustained effort. The result suggests generative AI can compress observed productivity differences across education levels in task execution, even as it leaves the underlying human-capital gap in unassisted work only partly closed.

## 3. Nine Raters, One Index: Carrying LLM Disagreement into Labour-Market Estimates [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Golo Henseke, Rhys Davies, Alan Felstead, Duncan Gallie, Francis Green, Ying Zhou
**Date:** Submitted July 30, 2025; revised August 6, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22748
**Summary:** The authors have nine different LLMs from three vendors rate AI-exposure for 1,100 task-occupation cells and combine each rater's scores with worker-reported task importance from the British Skills and Employment Survey, treating the choice of rater itself as a source of measurement uncertainty. Occupation rankings are fairly stable across raters (pairwise rank correlations of 0.74–0.92), but exposure levels are not — the share of British jobs scoring above the exposure threshold ranges from under 0.1% to 38% depending on which model did the rating. Applying this rater-multiverse to real wage and vacancy-posting outcomes, they find no robust evidence that generative AI exposure has yet moved either one, a caution against treating any single LLM-based exposure score as ground truth in labor-market research.

## 4. Scientific exploration, collaboration and labor division in the large language model era [Firm Adoption]

**Authors:** Xiang Zheng, Xi Hong, Jialin Liu, Chaoqun Ni
**Date:** Submitted July 23, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20923
**Summary:** Linking PubMed and OpenAlex records for over 775,000 scientists and CRediT contribution statements from 137,000 multi-author papers, the authors compare research behavior before and after ChatGPT's 2022 release. After 2022, scientists — especially established researchers and those from non-English-speaking low- and middle-income countries — increasingly published in intellectually distant fields they had not previously worked in, while research teams took on narrower, more differentiated roles with less overlap between coauthors. Authors with stronger AI-writing signals show the largest post-2022 gains in interdisciplinary exploration, suggesting LLM diffusion is reshaping not just individual output but how scientific labor is organized and divided within teams.
