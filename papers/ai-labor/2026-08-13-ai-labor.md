# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-08-13

## 1. Execution and Evaluation: A New Occupational Measure and Long-Run Employment Gradients [Employment]

**Authors:** Li Gan
**Date:** Submitted July 23, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20807
**Summary:** Li Gan builds a new occupation-level measure from all 19,265 O*NET task statements, separating tasks where AI mainly executes output from tasks where a human must still evaluate whether that output is correct, and shows the execution share is reproducible across model coders and adds information beyond existing O*NET evaluation-activity measures. In a harmonized employment panel, execution-heavy white-collar occupations have grown more slowly in every window since 2012, a decline that predates ChatGPT and looks like a secular trend rather than an AI-era shock. The vintage-valid AI-capability gradient does steepen after 2022, but the paper is explicit that this timing is descriptive, not causally attributed to AI, offering a measurement tool rather than a verdict on AI-driven job loss.

## 2. From Social Coding to Agentic Coding: Productivity and Relational Reconfiguration in Open-Source Communities [Firm Adoption]

**Authors:** Mengying Zhou, Yongjie Yin, Yang Chen
**Date:** Submitted August 4, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03585
**Summary:** Using an LLM-based multi-agent simulation seeded with real GitHub activity from over a thousand developers, the authors compare four weeks of open-source project life with and without coding agents added to the workflow. Agents raise completed tasks by 39% and cut median completion time from 45 to 20 minutes, but adoption reaches only 26% and the gains concentrate among developers who were already the most active and well-connected. Direct human-to-human interaction falls from 32% to 12% of activity while agent-mediated work rises to replace it, and the resulting public commit/discussion record covers far less of what later contributors need — evidence that coding agents can raise throughput while eroding the shared knowledge base that open-source communities rely on as informal labor markets for talent and reputation.

## 3. Cheaper AI, More Informality? A Dual Labor Market Model for Developing Economies [Employment]

**Authors:** Gabriel Montes-Rojas, Fernando Toledo, Juan Manuel Rodríguez Repeti
**Date:** Submitted July 16, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15381
**Summary:** The authors build a small open-economy DSGE model with a formal-informal dual labor market, imported AI capital, and country risk, calibrated to a developing economy where informality is pervasive, to ask what falling AI prices do to formal employment. The pivotal parameter is the elasticity of substitution between AI capital and formal labor: when AI substitutes for formal workers, cheaper AI weakens formal labor demand and pushes workers into informality as an employment buffer, but when AI complements formal labor, the same price decline expands formal employment, wages, investment, and output. The model implies that whether AI helps or harms labor markets in developing economies hinges on this largely unmeasured substitution parameter, cautioning against assuming AI's labor effects in rich countries will transfer directly to economies with large informal sectors.
