# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-08-25

## 1. How Organizations Use AI: Evidence from ChatGPT [Firm Adoption]

**Authors:** Aaron Chatterji, David Holtz, Neel Rakholia, Prasanna Tambe, Gawesha Weeratunga
**Date:** Submitted August 12, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12236
**Summary:** The authors link ChatGPT Enterprise account records to worker roles, message-level task classifications, and public-company financial data through March 2026, covering over 1,500 organizations and 17 million messages at the six-month adoption horizon. They find enterprise adoption is growing fast and is concentrated among larger, more valuable, R&D- and SG&A-intensive public firms, with usage spanning job functions and seniority but especially intense among early-career workers, and covering a broad range of knowledge-work tasks from writing to technical work. The scale and granularity of the linked usage-and-financial data make this an unusually direct look at how firms are actually integrating generative AI into daily workflows, showing wide variation in adoption speed, breadth, and purpose across organizations.

## 2. Organizational Technology Ladders: Remote Work and Generative AI Adoption [Firm Adoption]

**Author:** Gregor Schubert
**Date:** Submitted August 12, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11626
**Summary:** Using U.S. job-posting data and an instrumental-variables strategy built on predicted local labor-market pressure to offer remote work, the author estimates that a 10-percentage-point rise in firms' 2021-2022 remote hiring increased the share of their job postings mentioning generative AI in 2023-2024 by 0.4 points across firms and 0.7 points across occupations within firms. The proposed mechanism is a "technology ladder": adopting remote work shifted hiring toward technical and managerial capabilities that made converting AI exposure into actual adoption cheaper, and firms with return-to-office mandates (a signal of low realized remote productivity) show a much larger AI-adoption response to prior remote work. The findings suggest that firms' technology choices are path-dependent, with one generation's technology adoption reshaping organizational capacity in ways that condition uptake of the next.

## 3. Bye-bye, Bluebook? Automating Legal Drudgery With AI-Augmented Rule Following [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Matthew Dahl, Eric Martínez
**Date:** Submitted May 5, 2025; revised August 16, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.02763
**Summary:** The authors build a 2,058-query benchmark of Bluebook legal-citation formatting, one of the most common forms of repetitive "drudgery" work in the legal profession, and find frontier language models produce a fully compliant citation only 42.6% of the time zero-shot, with even a reasoning model scoring far below the human candidates competing in five top law reviews' editor-selection contests. Simply giving models the citation rules produces only modest gains, but a neuro-symbolic system that parses citations into structured elements and hands formatting to a deterministic rule engine lifts accuracy by 32.4 points to as much as 85.5%. The results show frontier models alone remain well short of replacing this specific junior-lawyer task, but pairing them with symbolic rule engines offers a more credible path to automating it.

## 4. Women Worry, Men Adopt? Gendered Risk Perceptions and Generative AI Adoption [Inequality]

**Authors:** Fabian Stephany, Jedrzej Duszynski
**Date:** Submitted January 7, 2026; revised July 30, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03880
**Summary:** Drawing on two waves (2023-2024) of the nationally representative UK Public Attitudes to Data and AI Tracker (N=9,172), the authors combine gender-specific random forest models with score-matched comparisons across repeated cross-sections to explain why men report substantially more frequent personal generative-AI use than women. The gender gap in adoption is largest among respondents most worried about AI's societal harms, especially to mental health and the environment, and exceeds 45 percentage points among younger, digitally fluent people with high risk concern; in matched comparisons, greater optimism about AI's societal impact predicts larger gains in women's uptake. The authors interpret unresolved concerns about AI's societal risks, not just access or skills gaps, as a distinct behavioral channel widening gender inequality in who captures GenAI's productivity and career benefits.

## 5. Governing Delegation to Generative Artificial Intelligence: Human Direction, Work-Related Orientation, and Modes of Use [Skills & Tasks]

**Author:** Jorge Fábrega
**Date:** Submitted August 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17624
**Summary:** Using aggregate monthly Anthropic Economic Index cells for April-May 2026, the author distinguishes "specified delegation" (direction given upfront via instructions) from "iterative coproduction" (direction given through mid-task corrections) and tests how each varies with work-related versus personal use across Claude's API and Claude.ai surfaces. Shifting ten percentage points of usage from personal to work-related use is associated with a 2.76-point rise in specified delegation via the API versus 1.45 points via Claude.ai, while iterative coproduction moves in opposite directions across the two modes (a 0.45-point difference). The paper argues that measuring where human direction leaves observable traces, rather than just how much AI executes, is a more informative lens on how workers are actually governing AI delegation in practice.
