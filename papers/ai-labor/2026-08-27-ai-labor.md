# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-08-27

## 1. Counterfactual, Per-Decision Bias Auditing for Automated Hiring: Localizing and Explaining Disparate Impact in Applicant Tracking Systems [Hiring]

**Author:** Jay Barach
**Date:** Submitted August 21, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21537
**Summary:** The author introduces the AI Bias Firewall (AIBF), which audits applicant tracking systems one hiring decision at a time by neutralizing a candidate's protected-attribute proxies, re-scoring the decision, and measuring the resulting counterfactual shift, rather than relying only on population-level fairness metrics or unconnected local explainers like SHAP. Tested on two real datasets (Adult and COMPAS) rather than synthetic data, the per-decision shifts faithfully reproduce known group-level disparities and flag attribute-flipped decisions far more precisely than group-based review, surfacing 55% of harmed candidates by examining just 5% of decisions. Even after correcting flagged decisions, the disparate impact ratio rises but falls short of legal parity, because features labeled as merit still carry residual correlation with protected attributes — a caution against treating post-hoc correction as a complete fix for biased hiring algorithms.

## 2. Bye-bye, Bluebook? Automating Legal Drudgery With AI-Augmented Rule Following [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Matthew Dahl, Eric Martínez
**Date:** Submitted May 5, 2025; revised August 16, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.02763
**Summary:** The authors build the first benchmark for AI performance on a ubiquitous form of legal drudgery — Bluebook citation formatting — testing frontier language models on 2,058 queries and pitting a "reasoning" model against human editors from five leading law reviews. Frontier models produce a fully compliant citation only 42.6% of the time zero-shot, fall well below human editors' competition scores, and improve only modestly when given the citation rules directly, casting doubt on retrieval-augmented generation as a fix. A neuro-symbolic system that has a model parse text into structured citation elements and hands formatting to a deterministic rule engine closes much of the gap (up to 85.5% accuracy), suggesting that automating even routine legal tasks currently needs language models paired with symbolic tools rather than relying on the models alone.

## 3. Using profiles of cognitive capability to assess AI suitability for workplace tasks [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Jonathan Prunty, Marko Tešić, Patrick Quinn, José Hernández-Orallo, Lucy Cheke
**Date:** Submitted August 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25623
**Summary:** The authors build a pipeline that profiles both AI systems and workplace tasks along a shared set of core cognitive capabilities, inferring an AI's capabilities from a cognitive-demand-annotated benchmark battery and eliciting the relative importance of those same capabilities for real jobs from domain experts. Validating the approach on synthetic agents, they profile six AI systems and collect task-requirement ratings from 410 employees across six occupational domains, finding that AI systems vary more across cognitive dimensions than across model families, while real workplace activities converge on a shared cognitive core. The resulting suitability scores give organizations a more granular scoping tool than aggregate benchmark scores or fast-aging human judgment for deciding which tasks to automate, keep with humans, or share between the two.

## 4. Detecting Soft Skills in ML Engineering Roles CVs [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Aidin Azamnouri, Nouran Ayad, Justus Bogner, Stefan Wagner
**Date:** Submitted August 10, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10046
**Summary:** Using an LLM-based extraction pipeline validated against human annotation, the authors mine 300 CVs of ML engineers, data scientists, and software engineers for soft skills conveyed both explicitly and through narrative, then test 13 hypotheses drawn from employer-side literature about how these skills vary by role, seniority, and disclosure style. Candidates convey soft skills through narrative roughly three times more often than through keyword lists — especially for leadership, coordination, and mentoring, the competencies employers value most — and seniority nearly triples the odds a candidate articulates leadership, while software engineers describe it at half the rate of their peers despite it being assumed universal. The findings show keyword-based CV screening systematically undercounts real soft-skill signal in technical candidates, a measurement gap with direct consequences for how ML-adjacent hiring pipelines filter applicants.
