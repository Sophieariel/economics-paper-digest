# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-08-29

## 1. The Pulse Beneath the Job Title: Monthly Readings of Requirements and Tasks from 750 Million Chinese Job Ads [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Qin Chen, Ying Fang, Xiangyu Wang, Leo Yang Yang
**Date:** first posted 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26924
**Summary:** Using 752.6 million job postings from China's five largest recruitment platforms between 2022 and 2026, the authors build two standardized catalogs — 20,721 requirements and 44,479 tasks — that separate what a job asks candidates to bring from what it asks them to do, something occupational directories and keyword-based postings research conflate. Scoring every catalogued task for how readily a language model could absorb it and matching those scores back onto each ad lets the market be read month by month rather than once every few years. Counting occupations makes AI-exposed work look stable or even growing, while counting the underlying tasks reveals that far more of that work is actually disappearing, showing that occupation-level measures understate how AI is reshaping jobs from within.

## 2. Small Changes, Big Impact: Demographic Bias in LLM-Based Hiring Through Subtle Sociocultural Markers in Anonymised Resumes [Hiring]

**Authors:** Bryan Chen Zhengyu Tan, Shaun Khoo, Bich Ngoc Doan, Zhengyuan Liu, Nancy F. Chen, Roy Ka-Wei Lee
**Date:** first posted 2026-03-05; revised 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05189
**Summary:** The authors stress-test LLM-based resume screening by expanding 100 neutral, job-matched résumés into 4,100 variants that differ only in job-irrelevant sociocultural markers — languages, co-curricular activities, volunteering, hobbies — signaling ethnicity and gender in a Singapore hiring context, then score every variant with 18 different LLMs. Even with explicit identifiers like names redacted, these subtle proxies alone shift hiring scores in demographically patterned ways across most models tested, meaning anonymization does not neutralize demographic bias in automated screening. The result undercuts a common defense of LLM-based applicant screening — that redacting protected-class fields makes it fair — and points to real discrimination risk in hiring pipelines that rely on it.

**Excluded, borderline:** Several August-window candidates were considered and set aside for lacking either an AI angle or an economist-recognizable empirical design: a randomized job-search-targeting experiment and its companion paper on job-seeker belief biases (Crépon, Frot & Gaillac) have no AI component; a Brazilian Pix instant-payment wage-inequality study and a South Korean gender-wage-gap-at-entry paper are labor economics without an AI mechanism; an algorithmic-allocation "Accuracy Trap" paper covers child-welfare and cancer-referral rationing, not labor markets; an AI-skill-formation "Stock-Formation Gap" paper is a single-author research-agenda proposal with no new empirical finding; an AI-AI creative-task co-creation study is a lab benchmark with judge ratings but no market or firm outcome; and a trafficking-recruitment-detection paper applies LLMs to job-board text but reports crime-pattern findings rather than a labor-market result.
