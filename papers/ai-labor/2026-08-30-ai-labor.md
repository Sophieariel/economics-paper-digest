# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-08-30

## 1. The Pulse Beneath the Job Title: Monthly Readings of Requirements and Tasks from 750 Million Chinese Job Ads [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Qin Chen, Ying Fang, Xiangyu Wang, Leo Yang Yang
**Date:** Submitted August 27, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26924
**Summary:** The authors mine 752.6 million job ads posted on China's five leading recruitment platforms from 2022–2026 to build two matched catalogs — 20,721 standardized candidate requirements and 44,479 standardized job tasks — separating what a posting asks of a candidate from what the hire will actually do, and score each task for how far a language model could absorb it. They show that occupation titles mask real variation (an "accountant" post spans a staircase of certification tiers tied to different pay) and, crucially, that counting occupations says language-model-exposed work is disappearing while counting tasks says far less of it actually is. The divergence is a caution for AI-exposure research: using occupation-level measures as the unit of analysis can substantially overstate how much AI is displacing exposed work, since task bundles within an occupation reshuffle rather than vanish.

## 2. Small Changes, Big Impact: Demographic Bias in LLM-Based Hiring Through Subtle Sociocultural Markers in Anonymised Resumes [Hiring]

**Authors:** Bryan Chen Zhengyu Tan, Shaun Khoo, Bich Ngoc Doan, Zhengyuan Liu, Nancy F. Chen, Roy Ka-Wei Lee
**Date:** Submitted March 5, 2026; revised August 27, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05189
**Summary:** The authors build a stress-test audit of 18 LLMs used for resume screening, generating 4,100 resume variants from 100 anonymized Singapore job applications that differ only in job-irrelevant sociocultural markers such as language, hobbies, and volunteering, then evaluate both head-to-head comparisons and score-and-shortlist outcomes. Even with explicit identifiers redacted, models infer ethnicity and gender from these markers with high accuracy and systematically favor candidates carrying markers associated with Chinese and Caucasian men, and prompting the model to explain its reasoning paradoxically amplifies rather than reduces the bias. The results show that today's PII-redaction practices are not sufficient to prevent demographic discrimination in AI-assisted hiring pipelines, since subtle cultural signals surviving anonymization can materially skew automated screening outcomes.
