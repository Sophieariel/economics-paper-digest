# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-09-02

## 1. What Work Does Generative AI Do? [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Alexander Bick, Adam Blandin, David J. Deming, Tyler R. Schumacher
**Date:** first posted August 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35677)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35677
**Summary:** Using a nationally representative worker survey, the authors build the first task-specific measures of generative AI adoption, linking use to particular occupations and job tasks rather than to occupations as a whole. They find adoption is "widespread but shallow": genAI shows up across most occupations and tasks, yet within any given task fewer than half of workers who could use it actually do, and existing task-exposure scores explain only part of this variation. The results suggest that who adopts AI within an occupation matters as much as which tasks are theoretically automatable, complicating simple exposure-based predictions of AI's labor-market effects.

## 2. Canaries in the Gold Mine: Early Productivity Gains from Artificial Intelligence Creating Organization Capital [Firm Adoption]

**Authors:** Tania Babina, Alex X. He, Renhao Jiang
**Date:** first posted August 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35684)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35684
**Summary:** The authors construct a firm-level measure of AI investment based on employers' hiring of workers with AI skills, spanning traditional machine learning through generative and agentic AI, drawn from job-posting data. They find AI investment is associated with productivity growth only in recent years, not over the prior decade, with gains concentrated in firms whose AI-skilled hires build durable "organization capital" — firm-specific knowledge accumulated through learning-by-doing. The paper argues that early AI productivity gains come less from the technology itself than from the organizational learning it triggers, a mechanism that could shape how quickly and to whom AI's benefits accrue.

## 3. Performance Manipulation: Labor Market Implications in AI-assisted Era [Hiring]

**Authors:** Xiaoyun Qiu, Yang Yu, Haifeng Xu
**Date:** first posted 2026-04-24; revised 2026-08-29
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22230
**Summary:** The authors model agents choosing between "creative" effort (non-routine work that rewards private expertise) and "mechanistic" effort (routine, rule-based work that AI heavily augments), and show that performance-based screening breaks down once AI capability grows large enough that low-type agents can profitably crowd out creative signals with AI-augmented mechanistic output. They validate the model empirically with a language-model-based method that scores nearly 1,500 Kaggle competition scripts for creative versus mechanistic effort, finding patterns consistent with the theory. The results imply that as AI lowers the cost of faking competence on measurable tasks, employers and evaluators need more sharply skewed reward structures to keep screening informative.
