# AI & Labor Markets Papers — 2026-09-03

## 1. Performance Manipulation: Labor Market Implications in AI-assisted Era [Skills & Tasks]

**Authors:** Xiaoyun Qiu, Yang Yu, Haifeng Xu
**Date:** Submitted 2026-04-24; revised 2026-08-29
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22230
**Summary:** Qiu, Yu, and Xu build a game-theoretic model of workers who split effort between genuinely valuable creative work and easily measured routine work whose payoff AI can inflate without real expertise. They show that performance-based screening stays informative only as long as evaluations retain enough of a creative component, and collapses into an uninformative pooling equilibrium once AI-driven productivity on routine tasks grows large enough to crowd out creative effort — an over-investment driven disproportionately by lower-ability workers. Using a novel LLM-based method to measure both effort types across roughly 1,500 Kaggle competition submissions, they find empirical support for the model and show that steeper reward structures can partly offset the erosion of screening value.

## 2. Small Changes, Big Impact: Demographic Bias in LLM-Based Hiring Through Subtle Sociocultural Markers in Anonymised Resumes [Hiring]

**Authors:** Bryan Chen Zhengyu Tan, Shaun Khoo, Bich Ngoc Doan, Zhengyuan Liu, Nancy F. Chen, Roy Ka-Wei Lee
**Date:** Submitted 2026-03-05; revised 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05189
**Summary:** The authors stress-test 18 LLM-based resume screening pipelines by building 4,100 resume variants from 100 anonymized, job-aligned resumes, varying only job-irrelevant sociocultural markers such as language, hobbies, and volunteering across four ethnicities and two genders. Despite the removal of explicit identifiers, the models still infer demographic attributes with high accuracy and systematically favor candidates carrying markers associated with Chinese and Caucasian men, with language markers driving inferred ethnicity and extracurricular markers driving inferred gender. Prompting the models to explain their reasoning does not fix this and can even amplify the bias, showing that anonymization alone is an insufficient safeguard for automated hiring tools.

## 3. The Pulse Beneath the Job Title: Monthly Readings of Requirements and Tasks from 750 Million Chinese Job Ads [Employment]

**Authors:** Qin Chen, Ying Fang, Xiangyu Wang, Leo Yang Yang
**Date:** Submitted 2026-08-27
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26924
**Summary:** Chen et al. mine 752.6 million job postings from China's five largest recruitment platforms between 2022 and 2026 to separate what a job title formally requires from what the job actually asks a worker to do, building two standardized, monthly-updated catalogs — 20,721 requirements and 44,479 tasks — at far finer granularity than official occupational directories. Each task is scored for how readily a language model could perform it, letting the authors track AI exposure at the task level rather than the coarser occupation level. Occupation-level counts show the most AI-exposed work disappearing from job ads, while task-level counts show a much smaller decline, evidence that occupations are being restructured around AI rather than eliminated wholesale, and that occupation-based automation-risk measures can overstate displacement.
