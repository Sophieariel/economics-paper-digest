# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-07-20

## 1. Return of the Solo Author: The Changing Division of Labor in Science in the Age of Generative AI [AI for Science]
**Authors:** Akira Matsui
**Date:** July 12, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10780
**Abstract:** The study examines how generative AI affects collaboration patterns in scientific research. Analyzing over 300 million works across 26 fields, the researcher finds that the decades-long decline in solo authorship halted and partially reversed with ChatGPT's public release in late 2022. This reversal varies significantly by discipline — strongest where coauthors' contributions are most replaceable by AI, and weakest in fields requiring physical collaboration. Rather than representing new researchers entering the field, the increase stems from established authors and those who previously published only collaboratively who are now producing solo-authored papers. These solo works remain topically aligned with their collaborative research but tend toward narrower scope and computational focus. The findings suggest AI substitutes for human scientific labor, particularly affecting the cognitive division of work within individual papers, with implications for how the organization of scientific teams and the internal division of labor within research projects are being reshaped by generative AI.

## 2. Distributed Denial of Science: How Indirect Data Poisoning of AI Systems Can Industrialize Scientific Fraud [Peer Review & Publication]
**Authors:** Bálint Gyevnár, Atoosa Kasirzadeh, Nihar B. Shah
**Date:** July 12, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10712
**Abstract:** Scientific fraud is the instrument of doubt that malicious entities can use to establish controversy in science. Historically, it required the resources of a company: deep pockets, ghostwritten articles, and corrupt academics. Today, Artificial Intelligence (AI) is increasingly automating scientific research, so the authors ask: Can a remote adversary weaponize the honest use of AI in science to compromise scientific integrity? They envision and empirically evaluate a new attack, indirect data poisoning, in which an adversary corrupts an open dataset and uploads the poisoned variant to a public repository. Autonomous research agents may independently retrieve and process this data, turning honest scientists into the unpaid and unwitting distributors of fraud at scale. Across five socially-salient topics, from hiring discrimination to the safety of autonomous vehicles, three widely used frontier AI systems (Claude Code with Claude Opus 4.7, Codex with GPT-5.5, Gemini CLI with Gemini 3.1 Pro), and 450 ethically contained experimental runs, the authors find that poisoning succeeds in 49.56% of runs, while the rate of poisoning detection is only 6.0%. The attack requires no topic-specific trigger-words, agent access, indirect prompt injection, or fabricated papers — only the open data ecosystem and misleading metadata. To mitigate the attacks, the authors propose and evaluate two measures: a scientist persona and a data provenance audit with five checks (referencing papers, social markers, statistical anomalies, related datasets, poisoning caution). They find that the persona still leaves 16.67% of runs with a poisoned conclusion, but provenance auditing reduces attack success rate to zero. The results suggest that indirect data poisoning may enable scientific fraud at unprecedented scale, but these attacks can be mitigated with suitable auditing by agents during data retrieval — with direct implications for the economics of trust and verification in an AI-augmented research pipeline.

## 3. A Practitioner's Guide to Using Large Language Models and Generative AI in Economic History [Research Productivity]
**Authors:** Andreas Ferrara
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35374
**Abstract:** This paper introduces researchers to large language models and their applications in economic history research. It presents a step-by-step workflow for turning a research idea into working code and data and describes four primary methods of LLM interaction: chat interfaces, editor-integrated assistants, agentic coding tools, and APIs. The guide walks practitioners through sequential decisions, including whether LLMs suit their needs, data privacy considerations, model selection, prompt engineering, context management, and cost control. It addresses validation, reproducibility, documentation, and the use of LLM-generated measures in regression analyses. The paper reviews recent research demonstrating how these technologies extract, link, harmonize, and classify historical data at scale, and provides four worked examples with replication files: emotion classification in paintings, record linkage for census data without names, newspaper analysis of the 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act, and emotional scoring of Franklin D. Roosevelt's wartime speeches. Summary tables and checklists are designed to facilitate adoption by empirical economists, offering a concrete account of how generative AI is reshaping the research-production workflow in a major field of economics.

## 4. Judges Matter More Than Papers in Post-Publication Research Assessment [Peer Review & Publication]
**Authors:** Robert Ward, Alex Jones, Lutz Bornmann
**Date:** July 8, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09783
**Abstract:** Research assessment relies on expert evaluations, yet human judgement is noisy, and it is unclear whether differences in assessment arise primarily from differences in genuine research quality or from unwanted differences between evaluators. While numerous studies highlight disagreement and biases in research assessment, they have not quantified judge-related noise relative to variation in the evaluated works. Here the authors show, in a large post-publication peer review database, that research assessment is driven more by differences between evaluators than by differences in the evaluated research. They partition variance in 239,521 research quality ratings assigned by 12,649 judges to 193,128 papers from the H1 Connect post-publication peer review platform. Using multilevel models, they decompose judge-related variation into differences in overall severity and differences in the weighting of scientific attributes. They find that judge-related effects accounted for substantially more variance in ratings than the evaluated papers: in the most detailed model, judge-level effects and judge-specific slopes explained 61% of the total variance, whereas combined paper- and journal-level effects accounted for only 7%. By contrast, examined measures of directional bias, such as author gender and global affiliation, explained less than 1% of the variance. The authors conclude that assessment outcomes were shaped more by the judges than by the papers themselves, demonstrating the necessity of noise audits in high-stakes scientific evaluation — with direct relevance for the design of peer review and evaluation systems, including proposals to incorporate AI judges into research assessment.

## 5. Credibility of Science: Relevance of Large-Team Science [Econ of Science]
**Authors:** Matthias Waldauf, Anna Dreber, Jürgen Huber, Michael Kirchler
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://ideas.repec.org/p/inn/wpaper/2026-04.html
**Abstract:** This study examines public perception of scientific research through an experiment involving over 7,500 participants across seven countries with varying levels of trust in science. The researchers presented study findings in three formats: a small classical single-team study, a large-sample-size study, or a many-labs (many-teams) study with 21 research teams working independently. The key findings indicate that participants regarded both large-sample and many-labs research as substantially more credible, generalizable, and socially relevant compared to single-team studies. Respondents particularly valued the involvement of many research teams and having many study participants as key advantages, and perceptions of many-labs and large-sample studies showed no statistically significant differences from one another. Results varied by country, highlighting the value of cross-cultural research on science credibility and underscoring the need to communicate the benefits of large-team, replication-oriented metascience to the public — a central input into how funders and institutions weigh investments in team-based versus individual-investigator science.
