# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-07-22

## 1. Estimating the Economic Effects of Federally Funded R&D [Econ of Science]
**Authors:** Sheila Campbell, Jaeger Nelson, Eli A. Schrag, Heidi L. Williams, Caleb K. Wroblewski
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35472
**Abstract:** In recent years, Congress has repeatedly considered legislation that would change federal R&D funding levels and the tax treatment of R&D costs, and the Congressional Budget Office has developed analytical frameworks for estimating how such changes affect the economy and the federal budget. This paper, co-authored by a team of CBO staff (including Heidi Williams, who also holds NBER and Dartmouth affiliations), documents the agency's current modeling framework in detail, focusing on two distinct approaches: an "R&D capital stock" approach, which treats publicly funded R&D like physical capital investment subject to depreciation and long, delayed payoffs, and an "R&D components" approach, which instead traces how funding expands the research workforce and its skill level. Applying both approaches to a common policy scenario — a sustained $30 billion annual increase in federal R&D spending over a decade — the paper finds that, over a 30-year horizon, the present value of GDP rises by roughly $3.57 to $3.82 for every federal dollar spent, depending on whether the increase is deficit-financed or deficit-neutral. The capital-stock approach implies effects that build gradually but persist, while the components approach implies stronger near-term gains that fade over time. As a rare instance of a government-agency accounting framework for the returns to public science funding, laid out in enough detail to be scrutinized and reused, the paper is a direct and timely input into ongoing Congressional debates over federal research-funding levels.

## 2. Female Participation in Science in the Past 125 Years: An Analysis of the Matilda Effect Over Time [Scientist Labor Market]
**Authors:** Felix Bittmann, Lutz Bornmann
**Date:** July 16, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15059
**Abstract:** The "Matilda effect" describes the systematic under-recognition of women's scientific contributions relative to men's. The authors investigate its historical evolution using a comprehensive bibliometric dataset of over 220 million publications (1900–2025) drawn from OpenAlex, inferring the gender of roughly 60 million individual authors. To quantify under-recognition specifically, they compare the overall share of female authors to the share of female corresponding authors, reasoning that corresponding authorship — which denotes intellectual leadership and primary credit for a study — is a more direct measure of recognized scientific standing than co-authorship alone. The analysis shows that overall female participation in science rose from roughly 10% in the 1910s to over 40% in recent years. For most of the twentieth century, women were disproportionately excluded from corresponding authorship even as their overall participation grew, confirming a persistent historical Matilda effect; strikingly, this exclusionary gap has narrowed and begun to invert over the past two decades. Despite this aggregate progress, sharp disparities remain: the physical sciences and several Asian countries continue to lag, while the health sciences and Latin America are approaching gender parity. The paper offers one of the largest-scale, longest-horizon empirical accounts to date of how gender shapes credit and career standing in the scientific labor market — a foundational input for policies aimed at the composition and functioning of the scientific workforce.

## 3. Structural Estimation with Unstructured Data [Research Productivity]
**Authors:** Sara Casella, Jesús Fernández-Villaverde, Stephen Hansen, Ryohei Oishi, Minchul Shin
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35487
**Abstract:** Standard macroeconomic data do not cleanly separate the systematic and nonsystematic components of policy, limiting what structural models can identify from numerical series alone. The authors show that incorporating unstructured text data into the structural estimation of a dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) model can sharpen this distinction, using Federal Reserve FOMC transcripts as their text source. They augment a standard state-space model with a measurement block that links structural shocks to text-derived time series, using a spike-and-slab prior so that the data itself selects which text-derived series are informative rather than imposing this by assumption. Applied to a medium-scale New Keynesian model of the U.S. economy, incorporating text both improves out-of-sample predictive performance and materially alters structural inference: the text-augmented model estimates a lower response of the policy rate to inflation, higher price stickiness, and lower price indexation, implying a flatter and less backward-looking Phillips curve than the text-free benchmark. Though the application is monetary policy rather than science itself, the paper is a direct contribution to the growing methodological literature on incorporating AI-adjacent, unstructured text data into the empirical economics toolkit — precisely the kind of shift in research method that is reshaping how economists (including those who study science) extract structural, causally interpretable evidence from previously unusable text corpora.

## 4. Measuring AI Innovation with Trademark Data [Econ of Science]
**Authors:** Carolina Castaldi, Fulvio Castellacci, Andrea Fronzetti Colladon, Ludovica Segneri, Francesco Venturini
**Date:** July 21, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18795
**Abstract:** Researchers, managers, and policymakers are exploring different approaches and data sources to map the development and diffusion of artificial intelligence, with patent counts serving as the dominant existing measure. In this research note, the authors argue that trademark data offer a valuable complement: AI trademarks can reveal the extent and ways in which companies exploit AI technologies to develop new goods and services, capturing market-facing deployment and diffusion in ways that patents — which register upstream invention — do not. Trademark data also offer a timely and globally available data source covering all economic sectors, including services, where patenting is comparatively rare. The authors present an empirical illustration using Italian firms, demonstrating how AI trademarks can be used at different levels of analysis — firm, sector, and region — to address emerging questions about the direction and pace of AI's diffusion through the economy. As a methodological contribution to measuring where and how a general-purpose technology is actually being put to use, the paper speaks directly to the "direction of innovation" strand of the economics of science and technology literature, extending it to the specific case of AI.

## 5. Two Vocabularies, One Phenomenon: Metadata Bias in AI Evidence Synthesis on Fertility Decline [AI for Science]
**Authors:** Boyana Buyuklieva, Veronica-Nicolle Hera
**Date:** July 10, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09409
**Abstract:** Large language models are increasingly used to synthesize scientific literature for evidence-based policy, but their outputs depend on how well underlying bibliographic databases index the relevant research — a dependency the authors argue is systematically underappreciated. Using fertility decline as a case study, they compare two vocabularies describing closely related phenomena in OpenAlex: clinical terminology (infertility, IVF, subfertility; n=101,645 records) versus social-scientific terminology (childlessness, fertility intentions; n=3,646 records). They find that the socially framed literature is consistently less "machine-legible": its output is skewed toward books and dissertations, which are less thoroughly indexed and harder for retrieval systems to parse than the journal-article-heavy clinical literature. Open-access rates are similar across the two vocabularies (43.1% vs. 41.3%), so the gap is not simply an access problem — it is one of indexing depth and metadata quality. The authors conclude that, even before any coverage bias enters the picture, LLM-based evidence-synthesis tools already systematically miss more of the socially framed literature than the clinically framed literature on the same underlying phenomenon. The paper is a concrete, empirical warning about a structural failure mode in AI-assisted literature review and evidence synthesis — a tool increasingly used inside the research and policy pipeline itself — with implications for which strands of science become visible to, and reinforced by, AI-augmented research workflows.
