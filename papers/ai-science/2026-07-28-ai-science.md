# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-07-28

## 1. Randomness in large language models: What researchers need to know (and report) [AI for Science]
**Authors:** Guillaume Coqueret, Joan Llull, Florian Oswald, Christophe Pérignon, Christoph Scheuch, Lars Vilhuber
**Date:** July 27, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24372
**Abstract:** Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to generate data for research. Typical use cases are classifications, annotations, information extraction, and generation of numerical scores. Unlike conventional measurements, LLM outputs can vary across repeated requests even when the prompt and apparent model settings remain unchanged. This variation arises from deliberate sampling, silent model updates, numerical rounding, or expert routing. Setting a dedicated temperature parameter to zero removes deliberate sampling when that option is available, but it does not eliminate the other sources of randomness. Exact reproduction is therefore generally not possible when using proprietary application programming interfaces. Local execution of open-weight models offers greater control, but reproducibility still depends on the complete hardware and software stack. The authors — several of whom serve as data editors at leading economics and finance journals (the Econometric Society, the Journal of Political Economy, and the Review of Financial Studies) and as officers of Cascad, a nonprofit agency that certifies the reproducibility of research articles in economics and management — draw out the consequences of this randomness for empirical research that treats LLM outputs as measurements, and set out what researchers should know about these sources of variation and how they should report them when using LLMs to generate research data.

## 2. Evaluating the Impact of Reviewer Guideline Design on LLM-Based Automated Peer Review [Peer Review & Publication]
**Authors:** Haowen Li, Yoichi Ishibashi, Masafumi Oyamada
**Date:** July 22, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22553 (accepted to ACL 2026 Findings)
**Abstract:** As large language models are increasingly deployed to automate scholarly peer review, the design of the guidelines given to these automated reviewers has received little systematic scrutiny even though it plausibly shapes the resulting scores as much as the choice of underlying model. The authors evaluate how different reviewer-guideline designs affect the alignment between LLM-generated review scores and human reviewer judgments across multiple models and conference settings, comparing official conference review guidelines against custom, rubric-style prompts engineered to imitate human reviewer behavior. They find that rubric-style, reviewer-imitating guidelines often push LLM scores farther from human judgments, whereas automated reviewers instructed with official conference criteria achieve the strongest alignment with human scores. The results indicate that the reliability of LLM-based peer-review automation depends heavily on evaluation-guideline design, and caution against ad hoc rubric engineering as a substitute for official reviewing criteria in automated review pipelines — evidence with direct bearing on the economics of trust and quality control as journals and conferences experiment with AI-assisted refereeing.

## 3. Detecting LLM-Generated Tokens in Human–LLM Coauthored Text [Peer Review & Publication]
**Authors:** Yangjun Lu, Hongyi Zhou, Fabian Spill, Kai Ye, Chengchun Shi, Jin Zhu
**Date:** July 23, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21458
**Abstract:** The rise of human-AI collaborative writing has created a growing need for fine-grained detection methods that support localizing likely LLM-generated content in mixed-authorship documents, including scientific manuscripts. Existing methods for detecting LLM-generated text mainly focus on document-level classification and cannot identify which parts of a text are generated by an LLM. This paper introduces a token-level method that builds on existing token-level detection scores. The key idea is to smooth adjacent token scores to reduce their variability while using an adaptive Lepski-type rule to select the smoothing bandwidth according to the local authorship structure. The method is simple to implement, does not require token-level labeled data for training, and theoretically achieves favorable mean-squared-error performance in estimating the underlying authorship signal — a practical tool for auditing the provenance of AI-assisted scientific and scholarly text as journals grapple with disclosure and integrity standards for LLM-assisted authorship.
