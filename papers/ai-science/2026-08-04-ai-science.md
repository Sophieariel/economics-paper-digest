# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-04

## 1. A robust association between LLM use and scientific productivity: Assessing stopping-time selection [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Keigo Kusumegi, Xinyu Yang, Paul Ginsparg, Mathijs de Vaan, Toby Stuart, Yian Yin
**Date:** July 31, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28968
**Summary:** This paper is a rejoinder to a critique arguing that the observed correlation between LLM adoption and scientific productivity is an artifact of how researchers date "adoption" (a stopping-time selection bias). The authors show that even after calibrating the critique's own placebo test to realistic detection rates, the measured productivity boost is far too large to be explained by the artifact, and the result holds under several alternative research designs that never rely on picking an adoption date. Because placebo tests on pre-ChatGPT data return null effects while the real data keeps showing a positive, robust link, the paper strengthens the case that LLM use is genuinely associated with higher scientific output rather than a measurement artifact.

## 2. Bi-National Academic Funding and Collaboration Dynamics: The Case of the German-Israeli Foundation [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Amit Bengiat, Teddy Lazebnik, Philipp Mayr, Ariel Rosenfeld
**Date:** October 3, 2025 (v1); revised July 31, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02743
**Summary:** Using bibliometric records from the German-Israeli Foundation covering 642 grants, roughly 2,400 researchers, and over 50,000 publications, the authors trace co-authorship ties before, during, and after each award to test whether bi-national funding builds lasting cross-border research partnerships. They find grants reliably spark new collaborations, with nearly half of previously non-collaborating teams starting to co-publish during the funded period, but this activity falls off quickly once funding ends and only a small share of teams sustain high, lasting collaboration. Team composition and prior publication records predict very little about which partnerships endure, suggesting bi-national science funding is better at igniting short-term collaboration than at building durable international research ties.

## 3. Conservative adventurers have more future academic impact [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Xingsheng Yang, Zhaoru Ke, Xiangyi Xiao, Qing Ke, Haipeng Zhang, Fengnan Gao
**Date:** June 29, 2023 (v1); revised July 31, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16643
**Summary:** Analyzing nearly 32 million papers across physics, biomedicine, and chemistry, the authors identify a research strategy they call "conservative adventuring": switching topics frequently, but only among closely related subfields rather than jumping to distant ones. Scientists who follow this pattern earn up to 19% more citations on their future papers than peers with other exploration styles, even after controlling for confounders that plagued earlier, conflicting studies of topic-switching and performance. Researchers who deliberately shift into this behavior see similarly large gains, leading the authors to argue it functions as an adoptable career strategy for balancing exploration and exploitation.
