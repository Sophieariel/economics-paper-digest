# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-08

## 1. War in the Abstract: The Rise and Consequences of Militarized Language in Scientific Communication [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Sovesh Mohapatra, David Lydon-Staley, Dani S. Bassett
**Date:** June 22, 2026 (v1); revised August 4, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23462
**Summary:** Analyzing 21.4 million abstracts from OpenAlex and PubMed between 2010 and 2025, the authors track a steady rise in war-themed vocabulary in scientific writing, up 48% and 32% respectively, with the increase accelerating after 2019 and tracking real-world conflict intensity by country and year. A companion experiment with over 800 participants and 32,000 trials shows that war-framed abstracts are read as less credible and generate less willingness to fund or support the underlying research. The findings suggest scientists increasingly reach for militarized rhetoric to convey urgency, but that framing may be quietly undermining the persuasive power and funding prospects of the work it describes.

## 2. Scientific Discovery in the Age of AI and Supercomputing [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Stefano Bianchini, Aldo Geuna, Fazliddin Shermatov
**Date:** November 16, 2025 (v1); revised August 6, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.12686
**Summary:** Using metadata from more than five million publications across 27 fields (2000–2024), the authors examine how combining AI methods with high-performance computing relates to breakthrough science, measured by novelty and top-citation status. They find this AI-plus-HPC combination is associated with disproportionately more frontier research than either capability used alone, but that access to both is increasingly concentrated in a handful of countries and institutions, chiefly the US and China. The paper argues that future scientific progress depends as much on policies that broaden access to compute and AI expertise as on the underlying technology itself.

## 3. AI-Assisted Peer Review Across Research Communities: From Reviewer AI Policies to LLM Review Quality [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Alexander M. Fichtl, Lukas Ellinger, Josefin Kelber, Kryštof Olík, Georg Groh
**Date:** August 4, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03581
**Summary:** The authors first survey reviewer-facing AI policies at 111 AI/NLP conferences and medical journals, finding the two communities regulate AI-assisted reviewing very differently, then evaluate AI-generated reviews at ICLR 2026 and Nature Communications against real submissions and human reviews. Current LLMs produce fluent, detailed reviews but systematically skew overly positive, offer generic criticism, and ground their claims unevenly in the manuscript's actual evidence. The paper warns that simple aggregate quality scores mask these weaknesses and calls for multi-dimensional evaluation before AI review tools are trusted more broadly.

## 4. Adversarial Fast-Moving Real-World Domains as Test Beds for Benchmarking AI Scientist Capabilities [AI for Science]

**Authors:** William Bolton, Philip Torr
**Date:** August 4, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03569
**Summary:** To sidestep the contamination and hindsight problems of retrospective AI-scientist benchmarks, the authors test models on genuinely unresolved, fast-moving domains: proposing Formula 1 car-design innovations for the 2026 season and Magic: The Gathering decks from a freshly updated card pool, both scored against real outcomes that arrived after the models made their predictions. Even the best models matched only a minority of real-world expert innovations (10 of 40 in F1) and recovered decks that correlated with, but rarely matched, what human pros actually chose. The results indicate that today's AI scientists are not limited by idea generation so much as by filtering and prioritizing which ideas are actually good.

## 5. An Axiomatic Benchmark for Evaluation of Scientific Novelty Metrics [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Miri Liu, ChengXiang Zhai
**Date:** April 16, 2026 (v1); revised August 5, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15145
**Summary:** Because ground-truth novelty labels don't exist, the authors build a benchmark that instead tests whether novelty metrics behave correctly under controlled changes to a comparison pool of papers, such as scores falling as more of a paper's content is already covered elsewhere. Testing ten systems, including embedding-based and LLM-based metrics, they show current methods largely detect surface-level redundancy but fail to catch deeper conceptual overlap, and that even human ICLR reviewer novelty scores are entangled with perceived quality rather than novelty alone. The paper argues reliable novelty assessment, useful for both peer review and evaluating AI-generated research ideas, will require combining embedding and LLM-based approaches rather than relying on either alone.
