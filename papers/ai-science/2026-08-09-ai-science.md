# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-09

## 1. A Vision for the Future of an AI-Integrated Research Ecosystem [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Ryan E. Dougherty, Natalie Kiesler
**Date:** August 5, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05438
**Summary:** The authors argue that generative AI now touches every stage of the research lifecycle, from writing to reviewing, and that policy responses like ACM's authorship-disclosure rules address only a narrow slice of the deeper strains building up in the publication system. Drawing on their own experience as authors, reviewers, and editors, they sketch two contrasting visions of scholarly communication in 2036 organized around what papers are for, how they get reviewed, who reviews them, and what incentives hold the system together. Rather than policing AI use, they call for building infrastructure around provenance, calibration, and accountability, and lay out three grand challenges for the community to take up.

## 2. Vibe Compiler: A Research-Logic Synthesis Tool That Runs without Prompt Engineering [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Riichiro Mizoguchi, Tomoki Aburatani, Kento Koike, Machi Shimmei
**Date:** August 6, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05545
**Summary:** Worried that generative AI assistance can erode researchers' own critical judgment, the authors build the "Vibe Compiler," a tool that turns a researcher's rough ideas into structured research logic using a sixteen-parameter ontology of academic argument. Rather than filling gaps in reasoning itself, the system flags where the logic breaks down and prompts the researcher with reflective questions, aiming to keep the human directing and validating the AI-generated reasoning rather than passively accepting it. A prototype built on NotebookLM and Gemini, used to help write the paper itself, suggests that what most improves AI-assisted research is a good knowledge structure rather than more elaborate prompting.

## 3. Studying People to Study AI: Expert Perspectives on the Epistemic Fit and Barriers of Human Research in AI Safety & Ethics [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Jessica Y. Bo, Paula Akemi Aoyagui, Shalaleh Rismani, Dipto Das, Syed Ishtiaque Ahmed, Ashton Anderson
**Date:** August 6, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05656
**Summary:** Surveying 93 and interviewing 17 AI safety and ethics researchers, the authors examine why human-subjects research stays marginal in a field that leans heavily on technical benchmarks and model simulations. They find researchers broadly value human research in principle, but its adoption is held back by perceived validity concerns, resource constraints, disciplinary habits, and an infrastructure built around technical methods, with technical researchers particularly skeptical and least likely to collaborate across disciplines. The pattern shapes which questions the field pursues, and the authors propose ways to give human-subjects methods firmer institutional footing without resorting to token "human-washing."
