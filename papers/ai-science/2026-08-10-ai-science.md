# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-10

## 1. War in the Abstract: The Rise and Consequences of Militarized Language in Scientific Communication [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Sovesh Mohapatra, David Lydon-Staley, Dani S. Bassett
**Date:** June 22, 2026 (v1); revised August 4, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23462
**Summary:** Analyzing 21.4 million scientific abstracts from OpenAlex and PubMed (2010-2025), the authors document a 48% and 32% rise, respectively, in militaristic vocabulary ("attack," "battle," "combat"), with the increase accelerating sharply after 2019 and tracking real-world conflict intensity across countries and years. A companion experiment with 801 participants finds that war-framed abstracts are rated as less credible and generate less funding willingness and policy support than neutrally framed versions of the same research, despite a small bump in perceived urgency. The findings suggest scientific communication is drifting toward a rhetorical style that may be quietly undermining the persuasiveness and trust it depends on.

## 2. Scientific Discovery in the Age of AI and Supercomputing [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Stefano Bianchini, Aldo Geuna, Fazliddin Shermatov
**Date:** November 16, 2025 (v1); revised August 6, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.12686
**Summary:** Using metadata from over five million publications across 27 fields (2000-2024), the authors show that research combining AI with high-performance computing is substantially more likely to introduce novel ideas and become highly cited than work using either capability alone or neither. They also document sharply growing disparities in access to supercomputing and AI expertise, increasingly concentrated in the US and China, with the EU27 as an aggregate remaining competitive. The results imply that future scientific progress depends not just on algorithmic advances but on policies that broaden access to computational resources across the global research system.

## 3. An Axiomatic Benchmark for Evaluation of Scientific Novelty Metrics [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Miri Liu, ChengXiang Zhai
**Date:** April 16, 2026 (v1); revised August 5, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15145
**Summary:** The authors build a benchmark that tests novelty-scoring metrics against three logical axioms (scores should fall as a comparison pool covers more of a paper's content, rise as the pool loses relevance, and fall as the pool moves later in time) rather than relying on noisy proxies like citations or peer-review scores. They find that even ICLR reviewers' own novelty ratings are entangled with perceived quality, and that across ten automated metrics, surface-level redundancy is well handled but conceptual redundancy is not. The work matters for the growing push toward AI scientists, since unreliable novelty detection risks wasting compute and attention on already-explored ideas.

## 4. AI-Assisted Peer Review Across Research Communities: From Reviewer AI Policies to LLM Review Quality [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Alexander M. Fichtl, Lukas Ellinger, Josefin Kelber, Kryštof Olík, Georg Groh
**Date:** August 4, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03581
**Summary:** The authors survey reviewer-facing AI policies at 111 leading AI/NLP conferences and medical journals, finding substantial regulatory divergence between the two communities, then evaluate AI-generated reviews against human ones at ICLR 2026 and Nature Communications. Current LLMs produce fluent, detailed reviews but show systematic weaknesses, including overly positive recommendations, generic criticism, and uneven grounding in evidence, and aggregate quality scores alone can mask these problems. The study argues for multi-dimensional evaluation standards as AI-assisted review becomes more common in scholarly publishing.

## 5. Adversarial Fast-Moving Real-World Domains as Test Beds for Benchmarking AI Scientist Capabilities [AI for Science]

**Authors:** William Bolton, Philip Torr
**Date:** August 4, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03569
**Summary:** To sidestep contamination problems in existing AI-scientist benchmarks, the authors test models' idea-generation ability in two fast-moving, verifiable domains: Formula 1 car design and Magic: The Gathering deck-building, where real-world expert outputs provide ground truth. Models generate plausible ideas but rarely match what real experts actually chose; the best model matched only 10 of 40 real F1 design innovations across 166 proposals, though selected cards did correlate with those favored by top competitive Magic decks. The results suggest the binding constraint on AI scientists is not generating ideas but filtering and prioritizing them, a distinction relevant to how much of the research pipeline AI can realistically automate.
