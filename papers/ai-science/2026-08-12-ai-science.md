# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-12

## 1. Contemporary AI lacks the imagination to diverge or negate in science [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Honglin Bao, Siyang Wu, Xiao Liu, Sida Li, Shiyun Cao, James A. Evans
**Date:** June 6, 2026 (v1); revised August 10, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08251
**Summary:** In the largest scientist-in-the-loop test of AI-generated research ideas to date, the authors had over 6,700 working scientists rate LLM-generated hypotheses drawn from their own recent papers across biology, medicine, chemistry, and social science. They find non-reasoning models cluster into a narrow "hivemind" of similar ideas and that no model spontaneously proposes null hypotheses the way human scientists do, while automated LLM judges track human quality ratings only weakly. A companion analysis of 39 million papers shows null-result claims and idea diversity contracting after ChatGPT's release, suggesting today's AI remains a collaborator whose judgment still depends on human grounding rather than an independent engine of scientific imagination.

## 2. Does ChatGPT score research quality differently by gender? [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Kayvan Kousha, Mike Thelwall
**Date:** August 10, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09552
**Summary:** Using nearly 90,000 UK Research Excellence Framework-rated journal articles with author names withheld from the model, the authors test whether ChatGPT's research-quality scores differ by the first author's gender. They find male-first-authored papers score slightly higher than female-first-authored papers in most subject areas, especially in health, science, and engineering fields, with the gap often larger than in the human REF scores it is compared against. The effect isn't explained by differences in writing style, leading the authors to caution against relying on LLMs for research evaluation without further bias auditing.

## 3. AI and the Research Team [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Johan Fourie
**Date:** August 9, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08437
**Summary:** This paper builds a span-of-control model to explain why AI adoption is linked to larger research teams overall even as some highly codifiable fields, like parts of mathematics, now see individual researchers with AI producing lab-scale results alone. It shows team size responds to AI capability in a hump shape: AI's falling execution costs expand a team's efficient scale, but automating codifiable tasks shrinks each member's necessary contribution, so team size eventually peaks and then declines as automation coverage approaches completeness. The model forecasts that fully codifiable fields could hit this peak team size somewhere between 2026 and 2030, offering a testable account of how AI is expected to reshape the structure of scientific labor.

## 4. Generalization and the Rise of System-level Creativity in Science [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Hongbo Fang, James Evans
**Date:** September 22, 2025 (v1); revised August 8, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.03240
**Summary:** Analyzing citation networks from tens of millions of publications in OpenAlex and Web of Science, the authors split scientific contributions into foundational, extensional, and generalizing work based on how they get reused downstream. They find discipline-bound foundational and extensional contributions dominated through the mid-20th century but have declined since the 1990s, while cross-disciplinary generalizations that transplant ideas across fields have risen sharply, a shift they argue the internet and now large language models may be accelerating. The result complicates the popular narrative of declining scientific disruption, suggesting innovation has instead relocated from within disciplines to the connections between them.

## 5. Evolution of funding for collaborative health research towards higher-level patient-oriented research [Science Policy]

**Authors:** David Fajardo-Ortiz, Bart Thijs, Wolfgang Glanzel, Karin R. Sipido
**Date:** August 14, 2023 (v1); revised August 7, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.07162
**Summary:** Using large-scale text analysis of both funded project descriptions and their resulting publications, the authors compare how EU health research funding (Framework Programmes and ERC Life Sciences) and NIH collaborative funding (P01, U01, UM1) have shifted since 2008 toward more translational, patient-oriented research. They find the EU's programmatic funding shows a pronounced move toward population-level and health-systems research, while investigator-driven schemes at both the EU (ERC) and NIH remain dominated by basic biomedical research, and the resulting publications shift less than the funding priorities themselves. The study shows that funder-level policy intentions only partially translate into realized research output, a gap relevant to any science-policy effort to steer research direction through funding design.
