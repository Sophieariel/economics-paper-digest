# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-13

## 1. Most biomedical publications show signs of LLM-assisted writing [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Lena Holzwarth, Rita González-Márquez, Dmitry Kobak
**Date:** August 11, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10715
**Summary:** The authors develop a word-frequency-based statistical method to estimate how much LLM-assisted writing has crept into scholarly texts, then apply it to the full text of open-access biomedical papers on PubMed Central. They find that by the end of 2025, 89% of papers carry a detectable excess of LLM-associated vocabulary, with usage roughly twice as common in Discussion sections (68%) as in Methods sections (32%), though even Methods sections exceed 50% prevalence. The results suggest LLM-assisted writing has become the norm rather than the exception in biomedical publishing, a finding the authors argue should inform disclosure and integrity policies.

## 2. AI and the Research Team [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Johan Fourie
**Date:** August 9, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08437
**Summary:** This paper builds a span-of-control model of how AI reshapes the size and structure of research teams, addressing the apparent tension between AI being linked to larger teams overall while also enabling solo mathematicians to produce research-grade output on their own. The model shows team size is quasi-concave in AI capability: AI lowers the cost of running a larger lab even as it automates away tasks that once required extra team members, producing at most one peak in team size as automation coverage rises. Under the paper's priors, fully codifiable fields are forecast to hit that peak sometime between 2026 and 2030, offering a testable framework for how automation could eventually shrink rather than grow the modern research team.

## 3. Who Uses Open-Weight Models? China and the Shifting Geography of AI in Science [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Zackary Okun Dunivin
**Date:** August 11, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11090
**Summary:** Using a text-mining pipeline over 21 million full-text articles from the Semantic Scholar Open Research Corpus, the author tracks which large language models scientists actually use versus merely mention in their papers through mid-2026. Open-weight model adoption is rising steadily, reaching 44% of single-model-family papers in 2026, but this growth is disproportionately driven by high-quality open-weight Chinese models: researchers at Chinese institutions have 2.23 times the odds of using an open-weight model, accounting for 44% of the overall increase since 2023. The paper argues this is not a general shift toward open science but a geopolitically shaped realignment of which AI systems become scientific infrastructure.

## 4. Does ChatGPT score research quality differently by gender? [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Kayvan Kousha, Mike Thelwall
**Date:** August 10, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09552
**Summary:** The authors test ChatGPT for gender bias in research evaluation by having it score nearly 90,000 UK Research Excellence Framework (REF) 2021 journal articles with author identity withheld. They find male-first-authored papers received slightly higher ChatGPT scores than female-first-authored papers in most subject areas, especially in health, science, and engineering, a gap that was often larger than the corresponding difference in official REF panel scores. Because the bias could not be explained by differences in writing style, the authors caution against relying on LLMs for research quality assessment without further scrutiny.

## 5. You can just review things: A digital ethnography of informal peer review [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Jay Patel, Joel Chan
**Date:** April 18, 2026 (v1); revised August 8, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16764
**Summary:** Through a months-long digital ethnography across 15 online communities, the authors study "informal peer review" — public, unmediated critique of published research by outsiders ranging from casual error-spotters to dedicated fraud sleuths. Drawing on 1,080 codes from case studies and reviewer interviews, they find this activity is loosely organized across ad hoc digital spaces, relies on distinctive investigative strategies, and routinely meets resistance from authors, publishers, and journal editors. The authors conclude that informal peer review is a fragile but potentially scalable evidence infrastructure, and call for tools and norms that better connect it to the formal scholarly publishing system.
