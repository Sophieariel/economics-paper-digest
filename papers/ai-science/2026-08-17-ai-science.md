# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-17

## 1. Prestige over merit: An adapted audit of LLM bias in peer review [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Anthony Howell, Jieshu Wang, Luyu Du, Julia Melkers, Varshil Shah
**Date:** September 18, 2025 (v1); revised August 11, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15122
**Summary:** The authors adapt a resume-audit design to peer review, building a multi-role LLM simulation that evaluates identical high-quality manuscripts under randomized author identities (institutional prestige, gender, race) across the physical, biological, and social sciences. Revealing author identity lowers LLM rejection recommendations by roughly a quarter of the mean rate despite unchanged content, with institutional prestige the dominant driver — low-prestige affiliations receive lower quality scores in every field, and low-prestige female authors fare worst of all. A companion test on synthetic CVs shows the same prestige-linked disparities baked into the model's background assumptions, suggesting the bias comes from status priors embedded in training data rather than from paper quality itself.

## 2. Co-leading Teams Drive Scientific Novelty in Large-scale Research Infrastructures [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Mingze Zhang, Yizhan Li, Hao Peng, Zexia Li
**Date:** August 13, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13195
**Summary:** Using 273,109 publications from 76 large-scale research infrastructures (LSRIs) worldwide, the authors classify papers by how in-house staff scientists collaborate with external user teams — absent, participating, or co-leading — and link this to a machine-learning measure of scientific novelty. Novelty is highest when staff formally co-author as co-leaders rather than mere participants, peaks at roughly balanced staff-user team composition, and follows a "knowledge saturation effect": novice users gain from any staff participation, but experienced users need full co-leadership to keep producing novel work. The findings offer concrete guidance for how big-science facilities should structure staff-user collaboration to sustain scientific creativity.

## 3. Patterns of Research Funding Across Research Subjects: The Case of NSERC [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Rabeeh Parhizkari, Gita Ghiasi
**Date:** August 11, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11484
**Summary:** The authors track how Canada's NSERC funding agency has allocated grant money across natural sciences and engineering subfields over time, asking whether the drive to maximize scientific output reinforces cumulative advantage for already-prominent fields. They find funding declining in recent years for many subjects within the pure sciences and mathematics, while social sciences and medical sciences — which started from a lower average funding base per application — have seen rising support. The results point to a policy tension between rewarding emerging high-growth fields and sustaining historically strong but currently deprioritized ones.

## 4. Unveiling the Predators: Contemporary Approaches to Identifying Illegitimate Open Access Journals in the Academic Publishing Ecosystem [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Robert Šamárek, Radek Martinek
**Date:** August 11, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10739
**Summary:** This paper reviews existing methods for flagging predatory open-access journals, from manual blacklists to machine-learning classifiers, and finds them limited by inconsistent definitions, binary blacklist/whitelist thinking, and poor scalability. As an alternative, the authors propose modeling the academic publishing ecosystem as a graph of authors, articles, journals, and publishers, using centrality, community detection, and anomaly detection to surface illegitimate publishing practices. The approach is presented as a framework rather than a validated tool, aimed at making predatory-journal detection more transparent and scalable than existing list-based approaches.

## 5. Auditable AI-Assisted Research Writing: An Engineering Discipline with Pre-Registered Process Observation [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Yang Zhou, Chengqun Yu
**Date:** August 11, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10858
**Summary:** The authors propose an engineering discipline for making AI-assisted research writing auditable at the point of production, combining git-based provenance sealing, hash-bound authorship tracking, automated compliance gates, and cross-model role separation between drafting and critique. Metrics for adherence are pre-registered before each case is observed, and in their prospective test case the frozen stopping rule triggered a "No-Go" halt on the observed project by its own design. The work is offered as an early, self-critical proof of concept — with a released reproducibility package — for building accountability directly into AI-assisted research workflows rather than detecting AI involvement after the fact.
