# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-18

## 1. Science under Threat? A Natural Experiment in Economics [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Dominic Rohner, Oliver Vanden Eynde, Philine Widmer
**Date:** August 14, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14164
**Summary:** The authors exploit the early-2025 release of a U.S. government "blacklist" of politically sensitive words as a natural experiment to test whether academic economists self-censor under political pressure. They find that economists at universities heavily dependent on federal funding sharply reduced their use of the flagged terms relative to peers at less federally dependent institutions and UK-based scholars, with the decline concentrated in content on gender, race, and environment. The effect reflects genuine changes in paper content rather than mere word substitution, and varies by department quality and author gender and ethnicity, showing that political pressure can reshape the direction of academic research even without formal sanctions.

## 2. Prestige over merit: An adapted audit of LLM bias in peer review [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Anthony Howell, Jieshu Wang, Luyu Du, Julia Melkers, Varshil Shah
**Date:** August 11, 2026 (v2 revision; originally posted September 18, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15122
**Summary:** The authors adapt a resume-audit design to test whether LLMs simulating editors and reviewers reproduce status-based biases in peer review, evaluating identical high-quality manuscripts under randomized author identities across the physical, biological, and social sciences. Revealing author identity lowers rejection recommendations overall, but papers attributed to low-prestige institutions receive systematically lower quality scores in every field, a penalty that survives multiple-testing correction, with an added intersectional penalty for female authors at low-prestige institutions. Synthetic CVs generated for the same profiles encode similarly large prestige-linked disparities, suggesting the bias stems from prestige-linked priors embedded in LLM training data rather than genuine differences in manuscript quality.

## 3. Co-leading Teams Drive Scientific Novelty in Large-scale Research Infrastructures [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Mingze Zhang, Yizhan Li, Hao Peng, Zexia Li
**Date:** August 13, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13195
**Summary:** Using 273,109 publications from 76 global large-scale research infrastructures, the authors classify papers by how deeply in-house staff scientists are integrated into user teams and relate this to scientific novelty. Papers where staff scientists co-lead rather than merely participate show a distinct novelty premium, which peaks at a balanced staff-user team composition and appears to reflect a tradeoff between complementary expertise and epistemic lock-in. The ideal collaboration structure also shifts with user experience: novice teams benefit from staff participation alone, while experienced teams need deeper staff co-leadership to keep producing novel work, offering guidance for managing shared scientific infrastructure.

## 4. Patterns of Research Funding Across Research Subjects: The Case of NSERC [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Rabeeh Parhizkari, Gita Ghiasi
**Date:** August 11, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11484
**Summary:** The authors track how Canada's NSERC funding agency has distributed research grants across subject areas within the natural sciences and engineering over time, asking which fields gain funding momentum and which stagnate. They find that many subject groups losing funding are concentrated in pure sciences and mathematics, while social sciences and medical sciences, despite starting from a lower average funding base, have seen rising funding over time. The results illustrate how funding allocation can reinforce cumulative advantage across research subjects and offer a basis for designing more equitable funding policy.

## 5. A dataset of article processing charges from 14 scholarly publishers, 2019-2025 [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Lisa Matthias, Diego Chavarro, Eric Schares, Juan Pablo Alperin, Margaret Rose, Molly Frost, Flavia Camargo, Jonas Höfting, Leigh-Ann Butler, Nina Schönfelder, Stefanie Haustein
**Date:** August 14, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14116
**Summary:** The authors compile a dataset of article processing charges (APCs) collected annually from the price lists of 14 major scholarly publishers between 2019 and 2025, drawing on both publisher websites and Wayback Machine snapshots. The dataset covers 12,540 unique journals and nearly 70,000 journal-year price observations across seven currencies, capturing how open-access publishing fees have evolved over time. It is intended to support more precise scientometric analysis of the true cost of gold and hybrid open-access publishing and to inform library collection-development decisions.
