# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-19

## 1. Science under Threat? A Natural Experiment in Economics [Science Policy]

**Authors:** Dominic Rohner, Oliver Vanden Eynde, Philine Widmer
**Date:** August 14, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14164
**Summary:** The authors exploit the early-2025 release of a U.S. government "blacklist" of flagged words as a natural experiment to test whether academic economists self-censor under political pressure. They find that economists at universities heavily dependent on federal funding sharply cut their use of the flagged terms relative to less federally dependent institutions and UK-based scholars, with the drop concentrated in content about gender, race, and environment and reflecting real changes in paper content rather than mere word substitution. The results show that academic freedom can bend quickly to political pressure even without formal sanctions, with effects varying by department quality, author gender, and ethnicity.

## 2. The Geography of Research: The Trade-Off Between Knowledge Production and Access [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Sitian Liu, Yichen Su
**Date:** August 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.15981
**Summary:** Using bibliographic data, the authors separate individual researcher effects from location effects to study what would happen if U.S. researchers relocated from legacy research hubs toward growing population centers. They find robust agglomeration effects tied to a researcher's home institution, meaning moving people away from established research clusters would generally reduce their output, though the loss is often small enough to be offset by broader public access to knowledge. Counterfactual simulations suggest that modestly reallocating researchers toward under-served large metro areas could raise aggregate research output rather than reduce it, informing debates on where to site or expand research capacity.

## 3. Estimating global article processing charges paid to 14 publishers for open access between 2019 and 2025 [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Lisa Matthias, Eric Schares, Juan Pablo Alperin, Leigh-Ann Butler, Sherry Kuang, Nina Schönfelder, Stefanie Haustein
**Date:** August 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16322
**Summary:** The authors combine a curated dataset of publicly listed article processing charge (APC) prices from 14 major academic publishers with OpenAlex counts of open-access articles to estimate global APC spending. They calculate that $15.08 billion (2025 USD) was spent on APCs between 2019 and 2025, with annual inflation-adjusted spending roughly quadrupling from $0.9 billion to $3.7 billion and over 85% of that spend concentrated among a handful of large publishers. The study also finds hybrid-journal OA fees exceed gold-OA fees and that median fees paid exceed median listed prices, offering rare quantitative visibility into an otherwise opaque cost driver of scholarly publishing.

## 4. Co-leading Teams Drive Scientific Novelty in Large-scale Research Infrastructures [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Mingze Zhang, Yizhan Li, Hao Peng, Zexia Li
**Date:** August 13, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13195
**Summary:** Analyzing 273,109 publications from 76 global large-scale research infrastructures (LSRIs), the authors use a machine-learning classifier to sort papers by how in-house staff scientists collaborate with external user teams: not involved, participating, or co-leading. They find a clear "novelty premium" for teams that formally integrate staff scientists as co-authors, which is strongest when staff scientists co-lead rather than merely participate, and peaks at a roughly balanced staff-user team composition. The premium also shifts with user experience — newcomers gain most from staff participation alone, while experienced users need staff co-leadership to keep producing novel work — offering concrete guidance for how LSRIs should structure collaborations.

## 5. A Pathway to General-Purpose Scientific AI: Multimodal Comprehension of Scientific Images [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Jennifer D'Souza, Fahad Ahmed, Cecilia Andrea Bustamante Andrade, Lina Frolova, Poorani Gnanasambandan, Dilshad Hussain, Muhammad Uzair Khan, Nkembeng Kevin Nkengfoa, Paul Praveen J., Fabio Priante, Sjoerd Franciscus van der Werf, Thomas Frederik Jan van Roeden
**Date:** August 14, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14075
**Summary:** Building on the ALD/E-ImageMiner benchmark and its associated ICDAR 2026 competition — 1,951 expert-annotated scientific figures from 205 publications — the authors lay out a research agenda for AI systems that can genuinely comprehend scientific images rather than just classify them. They frame benchmark tasks along a spectrum from basic visual reading to domain-grounded reasoning and evidential justification, proposing Bloom-taxonomy-informed question design to push toward deeper scientific understanding. The paper argues that machine-actionable comprehension of figures and tables is a prerequisite for AI systems to meaningfully participate in scientific research and evaluation, and sketches next steps including cross-document synthesis and hypothesis evaluation.
