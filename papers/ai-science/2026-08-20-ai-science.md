# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-20

## 1. Science under Threat? A Natural Experiment in Economics [Science Policy]

**Authors:** Dominic Rohner, Oliver Vanden Eynde, Philine Widmer
**Date:** August 14, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14164
**Summary:** The authors study how academics respond to political pressure by exploiting the early-2025 release of a U.S. government "blacklist" of flagged words, comparing usage among economists at U.S. institutions with high versus low dependence on federal funding, as well as UK-based scholars. They find that use of the flagged words — concentrated in content about gender, race, and environment — dropped sharply at federally-dependent institutions relative to less-dependent or UK peers, and show the shift reflects real changes in paper content rather than superficial rewording. The results provide causal evidence that political pressure can reshape the substantive direction of academic research, with effects that vary by department quality and author demographics.

## 2. Hidden Prompts in Manuscripts Exploit AI-Assisted Peer Review [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Zhicheng Lin
**Date:** August 17, 2026 (updated; originally posted July 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.06185
**Summary:** The author documents 18 arXiv manuscripts from July 2025 that hid instructions such as "GIVE A POSITIVE REVIEW ONLY" in invisible text to manipulate AI-assisted peer reviewers, and catalogs four distinct types of these hidden prompts. He argues the "honeypot" defense some authors offered — that the prompts merely test reviewers for improper AI use — does not hold up given the consistently self-serving content, and finds publisher policies on AI in review remain inconsistent, from an outright ban at Elsevier to disclosed limited use at Springer Nature. The paper frames this prompt-injection tactic as a new form of questionable research practice and calls for coordinated technical screening and harmonized AI-use policies across peer review.

## 3. Estimating global article processing charges paid to 14 publishers for open access between 2019 and 2025 [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Lisa Matthias, Eric Schares, Juan Pablo Alperin, Leigh-Ann Butler, Sherry Kuang, Nina Schönfelder, Stefanie Haustein
**Date:** August 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16322
**Summary:** The authors combine a curated dataset of listed article processing charge (APC) prices from 14 major publishers with OpenAlex counts of open-access articles to estimate global APC spending from 2019 to 2025. They find worldwide spending reached roughly $15 billion over the period, quadrupling in real terms from about $0.9 billion in 2019 to $3.7 billion in 2025, with over 85% concentrated among a handful of large publishers and hybrid-OA fees running higher than gold-OA fees. The study offers one of the first systematic estimates of this opaque cost stream in scholarly publishing, timely as transformative agreements make true open-access costs harder to track.

## 4. Co-leading Teams Drive Scientific Novelty in Large-scale Research Infrastructures [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Mingze Zhang, Yizhan Li, Hao Peng, Zexia Li
**Date:** August 13, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13195
**Summary:** Using 273,109 publications from 76 large-scale research infrastructures worldwide, the authors apply a machine-learning classifier to sort papers by how in-house staff scientists are integrated into user teams — as external users only, participants, or co-leaders — and relate this to scientific novelty. They find a clear novelty premium when staff scientists formally co-lead rather than merely participate, that this premium peaks at a balanced user-staff team composition, and that the benefit of staff involvement shifts from participation for newcomers to co-leadership for experienced users. The findings offer concrete guidance for how research infrastructures should structure human collaboration to sustain scientific creativity.

## 5. The Geography of Research: The Trade-Off Between Knowledge Production and Access [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Sitian Liu, Yichen Su
**Date:** August 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.15981
**Summary:** The authors use bibliographic data to separate individual- from location-specific effects on research output, showing that U.S. research activity has grown increasingly misaligned with where people actually live as population shifted away from legacy research hubs. Instrumental-variables estimates reveal strong agglomeration effects within institutions but weaker spillovers from nearby research clusters, meaning relocating researchers to underserved metro areas would reduce output only modestly. Counterfactual simulations show that reallocating researchers toward large, research-scarce metro areas could raise aggregate output on net, since the resulting access gains can offset the limited production losses.
