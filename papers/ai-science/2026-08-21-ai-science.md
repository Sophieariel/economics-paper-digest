# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-21

## 1. Quo Vadis? Scientific Discovery in the Age of Artificial Intelligence [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Petr O. Jedlicka
**Date:** August 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17970
**Summary:** The author surveys how AI capabilities in reasoning, planning, and long-horizon task execution are diffusing across scientific fields, and proposes a typology running from narrow scientific AI tools through AI assistants and agents to hybrid systems that combine computation with physical experimentation. Drawing on recent advances in mathematics, physics, chemistry, the life sciences, and the social sciences, the paper argues current systems remain limited by technical, epistemic, and institutional constraints even as their use expands. It concludes that AI's growing role in discovery raises open questions about how cognitive labor will be divided between human researchers and machines.

## 2. Artifact-centered Claim-aware Observability for Autonomous Scientific Agents [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Xiangyu Yin, Ming Du, Michael H. Prince, Mathew J. Cherukara
**Date:** August 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18312
**Summary:** The authors argue that as autonomous AI agents increasingly propose research ideas, run experiments, and draft papers, existing logging and provenance tools fail to make the scientific claims and evidence relationships behind agent outputs auditable. They propose an observability framework built around portable, "claim-aware" artifact lineage, treating scientific claims as first-class objects with explicit evidence bindings and verification records that complement existing telemetry and provenance standards like OpenTelemetry and PROV-O. The framework aims to give researchers a way to trace and audit failures that arise from how autonomous scientific systems generate and use evidence.

## 3. Traceable Trust for action-ready artificial intelligence in bioscience [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Huayu Xin, Yizhi Cai, Mukilan Deivarajan Suresh, Gavin Michael Farrell, Iwona Gajda, Charlie Harrison, Conor Houghton, Mato Lagator, Yang Lu, Virginia Portillo, Reyer Zwiggelaar, Sebastian Lobentanzer
**Date:** August 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17997
**Summary:** The authors focus on the moment when a bioscience lab decides to act on an AI model's output — such as a predicted protein structure or recommended strain — and propose "Traceable Trust," a framework for documenting what evidence supports that output, what authority has been delegated to it, and who can override the resulting action. They illustrate the framework through three case studies spanning ecosystem resources, project design, and laboratory action. The paper positions this output-to-action boundary as a key governance juncture for keeping AI-assisted bioscience research trustworthy and reviewable.

## 4. Declining Modularity of Intellectual Bases During the Emergence of Research Areas [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Kazuki Nakajima, Yuya Sasaki, Masaki Aida
**Date:** August 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16602
**Summary:** The authors track how the co-citation network underlying a research area changes as the area emerges, proposing that a decline in network modularity signals the integration of previously separate knowledge communities into a coherent field. Applying the method to higher-order network science, superstring theory, and graph representation learning, they find modularity declines around each area's emergence or transformation, with the superstring theory case aligning with an independently documented historical transition. The results suggest bibliometric modularity can serve as a retrospective, structural marker of how new research directions form, with implications for identifying nascent fields early.

## 5. The State of Scientific Poster Sharing and Reuse [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Aydan Gasimova, Paapa Mensah-Kane, Gerard F. Blake, Sanjay Soundarajan, James ONeill, Bhavesh Patel
**Date:** August 14, 2026 (updated; originally posted April 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.21150
**Summary:** The authors survey 86 platforms hosting scientific posters and find about 150,000 posters shared as of 2024 across the 43 platforms where counts were possible — a relatively low figure given posters' role in early-stage scholarly communication. They show that most platforms lack persistent identifiers and structured metadata such as conference information, and that even where repositories like Zenodo and Figshare support such metadata, researchers rarely supply it, while citation of posters remains rare. The findings point to a gap in FAIR-compliant infrastructure and incentives for sharing early-stage scientific findings via posters.
