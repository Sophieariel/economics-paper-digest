# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-22

## 1. Why AI Detection Fails for Academic Integrity [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Jonathan A. Karr, Grigorii Khvatskii, Ting Hua, Nitesh V. Chawla
**Date:** August 20, 2026 (updated; originally posted August 6, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11256
**Summary:** The authors run a controlled study comparing published English-language abstracts from before and after the rise of LLMs to test how commercial AI-detection tools handle light, guideline-compliant AI editing versus fully AI-drafted text. They find detectors flag minimally-edited human abstracts at alarmingly high rates (38-80%), while a simple "humanizer" tool nearly eliminates detection of fully AI-written text (under 4% flagged). The results show current detectors cannot reliably distinguish authorship intent, and that honest AI-assisted editing risks harsher sanctions than deliberately evasive AI use, undermining their use as standalone misconduct evidence.

## 2. Navigating Epistemic Monocultures in AI-Driven Science: A Simulation Study [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Sina Fazelpour, Joseph O'Brien, Hannah Rubin
**Date:** August 19, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19390
**Summary:** Using an NK-landscape simulation of scientific search, the authors model how AI tools that offer uniform, non-personalized guidance to researchers can homogenize collective inquiry, and where such uniformity helps versus harms discovery. They find uniform AI guidance benefits research only under a narrow combination of problem structure and baseline capability, and otherwise reduces cognitive diversity, while personalized AI guidance broadens the conditions under which help outweighs harm. The paper argues these gains from personalization are not automatic but depend on new institutional norms and practices being adopted alongside the technology.

## 3. Quo Vadis? Scientific Discovery in the Age of Artificial Intelligence [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Petr O. Jedlicka
**Date:** August 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17970
**Summary:** This survey traces the rapid growth of AI capabilities relevant to research and proposes a typology spanning narrow scientific AI tools, AI assistants and agents, and hybrid systems that combine computation with physical experimentation. Drawing on scientometric evidence and examples across mathematics, physics, chemistry, the life sciences, and the social sciences, the author catalogs recent achievements while highlighting persistent technical, epistemic, and institutional limits on AI-driven discovery. The paper concludes that as AI's role in research grows, it raises open questions about how cognitive labor should be divided between human scientists and machines.

## 4. Declining Modularity of Intellectual Bases During the Emergence of Research Areas [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Kazuki Nakajima, Yuya Sasaki, Masaki Aida
**Date:** August 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16602
**Summary:** The authors propose tracking the modularity of a field's co-citation network over time as a structural signature of how new research areas emerge from the integration of previously separate knowledge communities. Applying the method to higher-order network science, superstring theory, and graph representation learning, they find modularity consistently declines as each area takes shape, with the superstring theory case aligning with an independently documented historical transition. The framework offers researchers and funders an empirical way to detect nascent, cross-disciplinary research areas as they form, rather than only after the fact.

## 5. Traceable Trust for action-ready artificial intelligence in bioscience [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Huayu Xin, Yizhi Cai, Mukilan Deivarajan Suresh, Gavin Michael Farrell, Iwona Gajda, Charlie Harrison, Conor Houghton, Mato Lagator, Yang Lu, Virginia Portillo, Reyer Zwiggelaar, Sebastian Lobentanzer
**Date:** August 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17997
**Summary:** As AI models increasingly predict biomolecular structures, design proteins, and recommend experimental conditions, the authors argue that the moment a lab acts on an AI output is a critical, currently under-governed juncture for trustworthy research. They propose "Traceable Trust," a framework requiring labs to document what evidence backs an AI output, what capability and agency it claims, what threshold authorizes action on it, and who can override that decision. Three case studies spanning ecosystem resources, project design, and laboratory action illustrate how this documentation can be built into everyday research workflows rather than added only after problems occur.
