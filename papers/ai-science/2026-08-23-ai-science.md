# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-23

## 1. Quo Vadis? Scientific Discovery in the Age of Artificial Intelligence [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Petr O. Jedlicka
**Date:** August 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17970
**Summary:** This review traces how AI capabilities in reasoning, planning, and long-horizon task execution are diffusing across scientific fields, drawing on scientometric evidence and a proposed typology running from specialized scientific AI to hybrid systems that combine computation with physical experimentation. It surveys recent AI-driven advances across mathematics, physics, chemistry, the life sciences, and the social sciences, while arguing that current systems remain limited by technical, epistemic, and institutional constraints that carry both near-term and longer-term risks. The paper closes by framing the central open question as how cognitive labor in research will ultimately divide between human scientists and machines.

## 2. Navigating Epistemic Monocultures in AI-Driven Science: A Simulation Study [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Sina Fazelpour, Joseph O'Brien, Hannah Rubin
**Date:** August 19, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19390
**Summary:** The authors build an NK-landscape simulation to test whether AI tools that guide scientists toward promising research directions risk homogenizing the field's collective search process. They find uniform, non-personalized AI guidance helps only under a narrow combination of problem structure and community practices, and otherwise harms discovery by crowding researchers toward the same solutions; personalized guidance broadens the conditions under which AI helps, but only when institutions adapt their norms to support it. The results suggest that the design of AI research assistants, not just their raw capability, will shape whether AI adoption diversifies or narrows the direction of science.

## 3. Declining Modularity of Intellectual Bases During the Emergence of Research Areas [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Kazuki Nakajima, Yuya Sasaki, Masaki Aida
**Date:** August 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16602
**Summary:** Using co-citation networks, the authors track how the "intellectual base" underlying a research area restructures as the area emerges, testing the hypothesis that previously separate knowledge communities integrate — visible as declining network modularity — during formation. Applying the method to higher-order network science, superstring theory, and graph representation learning, they find modularity declines around each field's emergence or transformation, with the superstring theory case aligning with an independently documented historical transition. The work offers a quantitative, retrospective signature for identifying how new research areas take shape, with potential use for early identification of nascent fields.

## 4. Traceable Trust for Action-Ready Artificial Intelligence in Bioscience [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Huayu Xin, Yizhi Cai, Mukilan Deivarajan Suresh, Gavin Michael Farrell, Iwona Gajda, Charlie Harrison, Conor Houghton, Mato Lagator, Yang Lu, Virginia Portillo, Reyer Zwiggelaar, Sebastian Lobentanzer
**Date:** August 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17997
**Summary:** As AI models increasingly predict structures, design proteins, and recommend experimental conditions in the biosciences, the authors propose "Traceable Trust," a framework for governing the moment researchers act on an AI output in the lab. The framework specifies what evidence must support an AI claim, what capability and agency are being delegated, what threshold authorizes action, and who can override it, illustrated through three case studies spanning ecosystem resources, project design, and laboratory action. It is offered as a proportionate, reviewable process for documenting trust at the point where AI outputs begin to steer real scientific work.

## 5. Artifact-centered Claim-aware Observability for Autonomous Scientific Agents [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Xiangyu Yin, Ming Du, Michael H. Prince, Mathew J. Cherukara
**Date:** August 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18312
**Summary:** Motivated by autonomous AI agents that now propose ideas, run experiments, and draft papers, the authors argue that logging model calls is insufficient for auditing these systems because failures are often distributed across an agent's claims, evidence, and artifacts rather than any single call. They propose a compact observability profile — organized around individuals, operators, lineage, and verification records — that treats scientific claims as first-class objects with explicit evidence bindings, complementing rather than replacing existing telemetry and provenance standards like OpenTelemetry, PROV-O, and RO-Crate. The framework aims to make it possible to trace exactly which evidence and steps underlie a claim an autonomous scientific agent produces.
