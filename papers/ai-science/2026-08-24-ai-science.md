# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-24

## 1. Why AI Detection Fails for Academic Integrity [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Jonathan A. Karr, Grigorii Khvatskii, Ting Hua, Nitesh V. Chawla
**Date:** August 20, 2026 (updated; originally posted August 6, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11256
**Summary:** The authors run a controlled study comparing published English-language abstracts from 2013-2015 with 2023-2025 to test how commercial AI detectors score lightly AI-edited text versus fully AI-generated or "humanized" AI text. They find detectors flag guideline-compliant light editing at rates of 38-80%, misclassify a meaningful share of unmodified recent human writing (especially outside STEM fields), and almost completely miss AI-generated text run through humanizing tools, with post-humanization detection under 4%. The results show current detectors penalize honest disclosure more than deliberate evasion, undermining their use as standalone evidence in academic misconduct decisions.

## 2. Navigating Epistemic Monocultures in AI-Driven Science: A Simulation Study [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Sina Fazelpour, Joseph O'Brien, Hannah Rubin
**Date:** August 19, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19390
**Summary:** Using an NK-landscape simulation of scientific search, the authors model how AI tools that give researchers uniform guidance affect the diversity and productivity of a scientific community. They find non-personalized AI assistance helps discovery only under a narrow combination of problem structure and community practices, and otherwise tends to homogenize research approaches and hurt outcomes; randomized suggestions help only for easily decomposable problems, while personalized guidance broadens the conditions under which AI use pays off. The paper argues that realizing AI's promise for science requires deliberate institutional adaptation, since the benefits of personalization are not automatic.

## 3. Quo Vadis? Scientific Discovery in the Age of Artificial Intelligence [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Petr O. Jedlicka
**Date:** August 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17970
**Summary:** This survey traces the rise of AI reasoning and planning capabilities alongside scientometric evidence of AI's spread across disciplines, then proposes a typology running from narrow scientific AI tools to autonomous research agents and hybrid computational-experimental systems. It reviews recent AI-driven advances in mathematics, physics, chemistry, life sciences, and social science while arguing that current systems still face real technical, epistemic, and institutional limits. Its central contribution is framing AI's growing role in discovery as reshaping the division of cognitive labor between human researchers and machines, a framing useful for policymakers weighing near- and long-term risks.

## 4. Declining Modularity of Intellectual Bases During the Emergence of Research Areas [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Kazuki Nakajima, Yuya Sasaki, Masaki Aida
**Date:** August 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16602
**Summary:** The authors track co-citation network modularity over time to test whether emerging research areas form by integrating previously separate knowledge communities, applying the method to higher-order network science, superstring theory, and graph representation learning. They find modularity in each field's intellectual base declines as the area takes shape, with the timing aligning with an independently documented transition in superstring theory, and observe a later re-differentiation in graph representation learning after its emergence period. The framework offers a structural, retrospective signature for identifying how and when new research fields crystallize, useful for research-strategy and early field-detection efforts.

## 5. Conservative adventurers have more future academic impact [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Xingsheng Yang, Zhaoru Ke, Xiangyi Xiao, Qing Ke, Haipeng Zhang, Fengnan Gao
**Date:** August 19, 2026 (v4 update; originally posted June 29, 2023)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16643
**Summary:** Analyzing over 31 million papers in physics, biomedicine, and chemistry, the authors combine a topic-switching-frequency metric with switching-distance measures to relate scientists' exploration-exploitation patterns to future citation impact, controlling for confounders overlooked by earlier studies. They identify "conservative adventurers" — researchers who switch topics frequently but within nearby domains — as earning up to 19% more citations per future paper than other switching patterns, with evidence that scientists who deliberately shift into this pattern see corresponding performance gains. The findings offer an evidence-based strategy for career planning, particularly for junior scientists deciding how broadly to diversify their research topics.
