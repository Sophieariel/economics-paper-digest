# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-25

## 1. Navigating Epistemic Monocultures in AI-Driven Science: A Simulation Study [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Sina Fazelpour, Joseph O'Brien, Hannah Rubin
**Date:** August 19, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19390
**Summary:** Using an NK-landscape simulation of scientific search, the authors model how AI systems that give uniform, non-personalized guidance to researchers affect a community's ability to solve problems collectively. They find such tools help only under a narrow combination of problem structure and baseline research capacity, and can otherwise homogenize inquiry in ways that hurt collective discovery; randomized guidance helps only for decomposable problems, while personalized guidance broadens the conditions under which AI aids diversity. The results caution that realizing AI's benefits for science depends on institutional adaptation and new norms, not just better algorithms.

## 2. Quo Vadis? Scientific Discovery in the Age of Artificial Intelligence [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Petr O. Jedlicka
**Date:** August 18, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17970
**Summary:** This survey traces the growing capabilities of AI systems in reasoning and long-horizon task execution alongside scientometric evidence of AI's spread across scientific fields, then proposes a typology running from narrow scientific-AI tools to full research agents and hybrid computational-experimental systems. It reviews recent AI-driven advances across mathematics, physics, chemistry, the life sciences and the social sciences, while arguing current systems face real technical, epistemic and institutional limits. The paper concludes that as AI takes on more scientific work, the central open question becomes how cognitive labor should be divided between human researchers and machines.

## 3. Why AI Detection Fails for Academic Integrity [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Jonathan A. Karr, Grigorii Khvatskii, Ting Hua, Nitesh V. Chawla
**Date:** August 20, 2026 (updated; originally posted August 6, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11256
**Summary:** The authors test commercial AI-text detectors on published abstracts, comparing a pre-LLM period (2013–2015) with the LLM era (2023–2025) and simulating light, guideline-compliant AI editing. They find detectors flag 38–80% of lightly AI-edited abstracts as misconduct while flagging only 9–15% of genuinely unmodified recent originals, with false-positive rates far higher in non-STEM fields and scores driven by writing style rather than authorship; after simple "humanizing" tools are applied, detection collapses to under 4%. The results imply that honest, disclosed AI assistance is punished more harshly than deliberate evasion, so detector scores should not stand as evidence in misconduct proceedings.

## 4. The Credibility Revolution in Political Science [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Carolina Torreblanca, William Dinneen, Guy Grossman, Yiqing Xu
**Date:** December 2, 2025 (updated August 20, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11542
**Summary:** Using large language models to classify 91,632 political science articles published 2003–2023 across 156 journals, the authors track the spread of design-based causal methods relative to model-based approaches and other credibility-enhancing practices. They find design-based work has risen steadily, driven largely by survey experiments, but the shift is concentrated in top journals and among authors at elite institutions, while practices like placebo tests and power calculations remain rare. The authors conclude the field has undergone a partial, unevenly distributed reform rather than the wholesale credibility revolution often claimed.

## 5. Declining Modularity of Intellectual Bases During the Emergence of Research Areas [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Kazuki Nakajima, Yuya Sasaki, Masaki Aida
**Date:** August 17, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16602
**Summary:** The authors track co-citation network modularity over time to test whether emerging research areas form by integrating previously separate knowledge communities, applying the method to higher-order network science, superstring theory and graph representation learning. In all three fields modularity declines as the area emerges or transforms, including a decline in superstring theory that lines up with an independently documented transition, and one field shows re-differentiation after the integrative phase ends. The findings offer a structural, citation-network signature that could help funders and research strategists identify nascent research areas early.
