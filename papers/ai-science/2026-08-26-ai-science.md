# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-26

## 1. The Credibility Revolution in Political Science [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Carolina Torreblanca, William Dinneen, Guy Grossman, Yiqing Xu
**Date:** August 20, 2026 (updated; originally posted December 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11542
**Summary:** The authors use large language models to classify research design and credibility-enhancing practices across 91,632 political science articles published in 156 journals between 2003 and 2023. They find that design-based studies relying on plausibly exogenous variation have grown steadily while model-based approaches have declined, but the shift is concentrated in top journals and elite institutions and driven mainly by survey experiments, while other credibility practices such as placebo tests and power calculations remain rare. The results suggest political science's "credibility revolution" is real but partial and unevenly diffused, and demonstrate LLM-based classification as a tool for measuring methodological change in a field at scale.

## 2. Navigating Epistemic Monocultures in AI-Driven Science: A Simulation Study [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Sina Fazelpour, Joseph O'Brien, Hannah Rubin
**Date:** August 19, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19390
**Summary:** The authors build an NK-landscape simulation to study whether AI systems that guide scientific search create harmful homogenization, or "epistemic monoculture," across a research community. They find non-personalized AI offering uniform guidance helps only under a narrow combination of problem structure and baseline research capability and is harmful otherwise, while personalized AI can broaden the conditions under which diversity and benefits coexist, though this depends on institutional adaptation such as new norms rather than the algorithm alone. The paper offers a formal framework for anticipating when AI-assisted science risks narrowing the direction of research instead of expanding it.

## 3. Expectations and Practices around AI Disclosure in CS Research [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Arati Mohapatra, Danish Pruthi
**Date:** August 24, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23271
**Summary:** The authors examine AI-use disclosure policies at leading computer science venues, survey 109 CS researchers on when disclosure feels necessary, and analyze 13,867 actual disclosure statements from EMNLP 2025 and ICLR 2026. They find existing policies are vague, that researchers view disclosure as most necessary for research-design tasks and low human involvement, and that real disclosures diverge sharply from these expectations — writing assistance is disclosed frequently despite being seen as less consequential. The study exposes a gap between what disclosure norms are meant to achieve and what happens in practice, with concrete recommendations for tightening policy language.

## 4. Why AI Detection Fails for Academic Integrity [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Jonathan A. Karr, Grigorii Khvatskii, Ting Hua, Nitesh V. Chawla
**Date:** August 6, 2026 (updated August 20, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11256
**Summary:** The authors test commercial AI-text detectors on published English-language abstracts, comparing pre-ChatGPT (2013-2015) and post-ChatGPT (2023-2025) writing across four disciplinary domains, and simulate light AI-assisted editing versus full AI drafting. They find detectors flag light, guideline-compliant AI edits at alarmingly high rates (38-80%) while flagging unmodified recent human writing at lower but non-trivial and field-skewed rates, and that simple humanization tools nearly eliminate detection entirely (under 4% flagged). The findings argue AI-detector scores are unreliable and inequitable as standalone evidence in academic-integrity proceedings, since honest disclosure is punished more than deliberate evasion.

## 5. Beyond FAIR Data: Instrument Traces for Active and Autonomous Scientific Experimentation [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Sergei V. Kalinin, Boris N. Slautin, Yu Liu, Charles Cao
**Date:** August 24, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23039
**Summary:** The authors argue that AI-driven scientific instruments, which now decide what to measure next, generate a new kind of scientific record — an "Instrument Trace" — that needs to be captured alongside sample and data provenance. Reconstructing such a trace from 118,000 timestamped events in a longitudinal microscopy archive spanning 2023-2026, they show conventional saved files reveal experimental campaigns and decision patterns but miss unsaved tuning steps, failures, and the rationale behind algorithmic choices. They propose a prospective trace architecture recording synchronized sample, instrument, and decision histories to support reproducible autonomous experimentation, predictive maintenance, and cross-facility comparison.
