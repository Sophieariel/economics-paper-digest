# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-27

## 1. Beyond FAIR Data: Instrument Traces for Active and Autonomous Scientific Experimentation [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Sergei V. Kalinin, Boris N. Slautin, Yu Liu, Charles Cao
**Date:** August 24, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23039
**Summary:** The authors argue that AI-driven instruments, which now decide what to measure next, generate a new kind of scientific record — the "instrument trace" — that sits alongside sample provenance, data, and analysis workflows. Reconstructing such a trace from 118,000 timestamped events in a three-year microscopy archive, they show conventional lab records capture material campaigns and probe states but miss crucial details like failed runs, exact timing, and the rationale behind autonomous decisions. They propose a prospective architecture that synchronizes sample, instrument, and decision histories, arguing this is needed to make autonomous experimentation reproducible, auditable, and transferable across labs.

## 2. Expectations and Practices around AI Disclosure in CS Research [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Arati Mohapatra, Danish Pruthi
**Date:** August 25, 2026 (v2; originally posted August 24, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23271
**Summary:** The authors examine how well AI-use disclosure policies at top computer science venues match what researchers actually think should be disclosed, combining a policy audit with a survey of 109 CS researchers and an analysis of nearly 14,000 disclosure statements from EMNLP 2025 and ICLR 2026. They find existing policies are vague, that researchers see disclosure as most important for research-design tasks and low human oversight, and that actual disclosures diverge sharply from these expectations — writing assistance is disclosed often despite being seen as less necessary. The paper proposes categorizing research tasks by disclosure necessity and a standard template to close this gap between policy intent and practice.

## 3. Why AI Detection Fails for Academic Integrity [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Jonathan A. Karr, Grigorii Khvatskii, Ting Hua, Nitesh V. Chawla
**Date:** August 20, 2026 (updated; originally posted August 6, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11256
**Summary:** Using published English-language abstracts from before and after the rise of LLMs, the authors test how commercial AI detectors handle light AI-assisted editing versus fully AI-generated text versus deliberately "humanized" AI text. They find detectors flag guideline-compliant light editing at alarmingly high rates (38–80%), misclassify a meaningful share of pre-LLM human writing (especially outside STEM), and almost completely fail to catch AI text run through humanization tools (under 4% flagged). The results suggest institutions relying on detector scores as standalone misconduct evidence are punishing honest disclosure while letting deliberate evasion through, and call for policy reform.

## 4. The Credibility Revolution in Political Science [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Carolina Torreblanca, William Dinneen, Guy Grossman, Yiqing Xu
**Date:** August 20, 2026 (updated; originally posted December 2, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11542
**Summary:** Using large language models to classify research design and credibility-enhancing practices across 91,632 political science articles published between 2003 and 2023, the authors track how far the field's turn toward causal, design-based inference has actually spread. They find design-based studies leveraging plausibly exogenous variation have risen steadily while model-based approaches have declined, but this shift is concentrated in top journals and elite institutions and is driven mainly by growth in survey experiments. Other credibility practices like placebo tests and power calculations remain rare, leading the authors to characterize the discipline's change as a partial, unevenly distributed reform rather than a full revolution.

## 5. Trajectories and Comparative Analysis of Global Countries Dominating AI Publications, 2000-2025 [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Jason Hung
**Date:** August 25, 2026 (updated; originally posted September 29, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25298
**Summary:** Using OpenAlex data and fractional counting to avoid double-crediting co-authored papers, the author tracks how national shares of AI research output shifted from 2000 to 2025. He finds the US and EU's combined share fell from over 57% to under 25% while China's share rose from under 5% to nearly 36%, with India also rising to help form a multipolar Asian research base. Looking beyond raw volume, the analysis shows China has also recently overtaken Western countries in high-impact AI publications, challenging assumptions that Western institutions still lead in AI research quality.
