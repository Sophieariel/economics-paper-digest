# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-28

## 1. Normative boundaries of AI in scientific work: Evidence from PhD researchers [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Francesco Angelini, Johan Lyrvall
**Date:** August 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25678
**Summary:** Angelini and Lyrvall analyze survey responses from 3,785 PhD students in STEM and health sciences (Nature's 2025 Graduate Survey) to see which research tasks they find acceptable to delegate to AI. Using latent class analysis, they identify four attitudinal profiles, the largest showing a "division of labor" pattern where AI is welcomed for literature search and synthesis but resisted for writing, data analysis, and experimental design. The findings suggest doctoral training, disclosure norms, and research evaluation should be tailored task by task rather than treating AI acceptance as a single yes/no question.

## 2. Triadic Novelty: A Structural Typology of Science Innovation [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Jin Ai, Richard S. Steinberg, Chao Guo, Filipi Nascimento Silva
**Date:** August 25, 2026 (v3; originally posted June 21, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.17851
**Summary:** Ai, Steinberg, Guo, and Silva build a network-based typology that splits scientific novelty into three structural types — Pioneers who open new topics, Mavericks who recombine distant fields, and Vanguards who reinforce emerging but still-weak connections — rather than treating novelty as one score. Applying it to the philanthropic and nonprofit studies literature, they find these types are rewarded very differently: Pioneer work is often overlooked unless later adopted, Maverick work is reliably recognized, and Vanguard work sees diminishing returns as its area matures. The framework offers funders and evaluators a citation-independent way to assess research risk-taking and could inform how funding and journals reward different kinds of innovation.

## 3. Hybrid Panels: Toward Human-AI Collaboration in Survey Research [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Julia Romberg, Tobias Gummer, Gabriella Lapesa, Tanja Kunz, Claudia Wagner
**Date:** August 23, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22582
**Summary:** Romberg, Gummer, Lapesa, Kunz, and Wagner propose "hybrid panels," a survey infrastructure that mixes real respondents with LLM-simulated ones, using mismatches between the two to iteratively recalibrate which questions go to whom in future survey waves. The idea targets persistent problems in large-scale social-science surveys — falling response rates, rising costs, and long lags between collection and delivery — without sacrificing data quality. A pilot study demonstrates the framework end to end and surfaces open challenges in validating when LLM-simulated responses can substitute for human ones.

## 4. ExploraTwin, a Non-Profit Research Platform for Digital Twin Simulations [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Naveen Venkat, Yuchen Qiu, Tianyi Peng, George Gui, Olivier Toubia
**Date:** August 24, 2026 (v2; originally posted August 20, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20539
**Summary:** Venkat, Qiu, Peng, Gui, and Toubia introduce ExploraTwin, a free platform letting researchers run surveys and focus-group-style conversations against LLM-based "digital twins" of survey respondents instead of, or alongside, real participants. The platform includes a standardized data format (CroissantTwin) for sharing twin samples and was validated by replicating 19 published digital-twin experiments, with 99.6% of roughly 197,000 simulated answers returning a structurally valid response on the first try. By lowering the barrier to testing digital-twin methodology, the authors aim to help the field build more evidence before such simulations are deployed in real studies.
