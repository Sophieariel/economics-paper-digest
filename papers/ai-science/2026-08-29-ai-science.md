# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-29

## 1. Research Design Tracking and Assessment for the Social Sciences [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Marco Rovera, Sergiu Burlacu, Dominique Cappelletti, Alessio Tomelleri, Sonia Marzadro, Martina Bazzoli, Annalisa Tassi, Jessica Gagete-Miranda
**Date:** August 27, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27049
**Summary:** The authors build ARDTrA, a task and expert-annotated dataset for automatically detecting which causal research design a social-science paper uses and assessing how well it is implemented, then test a multi-turn retrieval-augmented pipeline across several LLMs, retrieval strategies, and embedding models. They find that how much text the system retrieves is the single biggest driver of performance, accounting for over half the variance in accuracy, and that the research designs machines struggle with are not the same ones that split human expert annotators. The results suggest automating research-design quality assessment faces distinct challenges from human review, with implications for how funders and journals might one day use AI to triage methodological rigor at scale.

## 2. Gender and the Production of Research Impact [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Sanger Wagner, Charles Rahal, Melinda C. Mills
**Date:** August 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26409
**Summary:** Combining records from the UK's Research Excellence Framework with bibliometric data, the authors examine who produces the case studies used to document scientific research's impact beyond academia, and how this differs by gender. Women make up 38% of impact-case contributors, a higher share than their 34% share of research-paper authorship, but this apparent parity masks a domain split: women are better represented in impact tied to education, health, and cultural outcomes, while men still dominate commercialization pathways like patenting. The findings matter for research-funding allocation, since impact assessment increasingly determines how national science budgets are distributed, and show that gender equity gains at one production stage don't automatically carry through to others.

## 3. Normative Boundaries of AI in Scientific Work: Evidence from PhD Researchers [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Francesco Angelini, Johan Lyrvall
**Date:** August 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25678
**Summary:** Drawing on Nature's 2025 Graduate Survey of 3,785 PhD students, the authors use latent class analysis to map how comfortable early-career researchers are with AI across five research tasks: writing, data collection/analysis, experiment design, literature tracking, and summarizing. They identify a dominant "division of labour" profile in which AI is welcomed for literature-related chores but resisted for tasks seen as core intellectual contribution, alongside smaller "status quo," "all-purpose," and "undecided" groups. The pattern suggests doctoral training, authorship norms, and disclosure policy should be tailored task by task rather than treating AI acceptance as a single up-or-down question.

## 4. From Hallucination to Reliability: Generative Modeling and the Structure of Scientific Inference [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Charles Rathkopf
**Date:** August 26, 2026 (updated; originally posted April 11, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08526
**Summary:** The author develops a philosophical account of how generative AI models can still produce reliable scientific knowledge despite being inherently prone to hallucination, reframing hallucinations as misrepresentations generated by the model itself rather than errors inherited from training data. Using AlphaFold and the SEEDS weather-generation model as case studies, he argues scientists' existing background knowledge lets workflows filter or qualify hallucinatory outputs before they contaminate downstream conclusions. The paper's key move is treating the whole scientific workflow, not the model in isolation, as the proper unit for judging whether AI-assisted science is epistemically trustworthy.

## 5. Triadic Novelty: A Structural Typology of Science Innovation [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Jin Ai, Richard S. Steinberg, Chao Guo, Filipi Nascimento Silva
**Date:** August 25, 2026 (updated; originally posted June 21, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.17851
**Summary:** The authors propose a network-based typology that splits scientific novelty into three structural types: Pioneers who open new topics, Mavericks who recombine distant fields, and Vanguards who reinforce weak, emerging connections, then apply it to citation data in philanthropic and nonprofit studies. They find recognition is distributed unevenly across these types: Pioneer work is often ignored unless later adopted, Maverick work is reliably rewarded, and Vanguard work sees diminishing returns as its ideas become mainstream. Because the typology doesn't rely on citation counts alone, the authors argue it offers funders and journals a better tool for identifying genuinely novel work that current evaluation systems undervalue.
