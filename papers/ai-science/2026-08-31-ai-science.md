# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-08-31

## 1. Normative Boundaries of AI in Scientific Work: Evidence from PhD Researchers [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Francesco Angelini, Johan Lyrvall
**Date:** August 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25678
**Summary:** Drawing on Nature's Graduate Survey 2025, the authors analyze how 3,785 PhD students in STEM and medical/health sciences feel about using AI across five research tasks, from literature tracking to experimental design. A latent class analysis finds four distinct attitudinal profiles, the largest reflecting a "division of labour" in which AI is welcomed for literature-related work but resisted for tasks tied to intellectual contribution, such as writing, data analysis, and study design. The findings suggest AI governance and doctoral training should target specific tasks rather than treat AI acceptance as a single up-or-down question.

## 2. Gender and the Production of Research Impact [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Sanger Wagner, Charles Rahal, Melinda C. Mills
**Date:** August 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26409
**Summary:** Combining UK Research Excellence Framework records with large-scale bibliometric data, the authors measure the gender composition of researchers behind documented "impact" case studies used in national research assessment and funding allocation. They find women contribute to 38% of impact case studies, more than their 34% share of research-output authorship overall, but remain concentrated in education, health, and cultural impact while commercialisation pathways like patenting stay male-dominated. As impact becomes a bigger factor in funding decisions, this uneven distribution across impact types could shape whose contributions get institutionally rewarded.

## 3. Research Design Tracking and Assessment for the Social Sciences [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Marco Rovera, Sergiu Burlacu, Dominique Cappelletti, Alessio Tomelleri, Sonia Marzadro, Martina Bazzoli, Annalisa Tassi, Jessica Gagete-Miranda
**Date:** August 27, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27049
**Summary:** The authors introduce ARDTrA, a new task and expert-annotated dataset for automatically detecting which causal research design a social science paper uses and judging how well it was applied, tested with a multi-turn retrieval-augmented pipeline across several LLMs and embedding models. They find that retrieved-passage length explains most of the variation in system performance, and that the designs hardest for the AI system are not the ones human experts disagree about most, pointing to two separate sources of difficulty. This is an early step toward AI-assisted screening of causal evidence quality, useful for evidence-based policy work but still short of replacing manual expert review.

## 4. Triadic Novelty: A Structural Typology of Science Innovation [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Jin Ai, Richard S. Steinberg, Chao Guo, Filipi Nascimento Silva
**Date:** August 25, 2026 (updated; originally posted June 21, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.17851
**Summary:** Using network-science methods applied to the philanthropic and nonprofit studies literature, the authors classify novel research contributions into three structural types: "pioneers" that open new topics, "mavericks" that recombine distant fields, and "vanguards" that strengthen weak, emerging connections. They find recognition is distributed unevenly across types — pioneering work is often overlooked until later picked up, maverick recombination is consistently rewarded, and vanguard contributions see diminishing returns as a topic matures. The typology offers a citation-independent lens on how novelty gets valued, with implications for research evaluation and funding design that current metrics may underweight genuinely paradigm-challenging work.

## 5. From Hallucination to Reliability: Generative Modeling and the Structure of Scientific Inference [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Charles Rathkopf
**Date:** August 26, 2026 (updated; originally posted April 11, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08526
**Summary:** This paper develops a philosophical account of how generative AI tools used in science, such as AlphaFold, can still yield reliable scientific knowledge despite being inherently prone to hallucination. The author argues these hallucinations arise from the model's own generative activity rather than from training data, and shows through case studies that surrounding scientific workflows catch and filter such errors before they reach downstream conclusions. The upshot is that reliability should be evaluated at the level of the whole research workflow rather than the model in isolation, a distinction relevant to how AI-for-science tools get vetted and trusted.
