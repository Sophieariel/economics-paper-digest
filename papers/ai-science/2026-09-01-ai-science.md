# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-09-01

## 1. Research Design Tracking and Assessment for the Social Sciences [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Marco Rovera, Sergiu Burlacu, Dominique Cappelletti, Alessio Tomelleri, Sonia Marzadro, Martina Bazzoli, Annalisa Tassi, Jessica Gagete-Miranda
**Date:** August 27, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27049
**Summary:** The authors introduce Automated Research Design Tracking and Assessment (ARDTrA), a task for using LLMs to detect which causal research design a social-science paper uses and judge how well it was applied, built on an expert-annotated dataset spanning six families of counterfactual designs. Testing a multi-turn RAG pipeline across four retrieval strategies, four LLMs, and six embedding models, they find that passage length — not model or retrieval choice — explains most of the variance in performance (52-66%). They also find the research designs that trip up the automated system are not the ones human experts disagree on most, suggesting machine and human difficulty stem from different sources.

## 2. Gender and the Production of Research Impact [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Sanger Wagner, Charles Rahal, Melinda C. Mills
**Date:** August 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26409
**Summary:** Combining records from the UK's Research Excellence Framework with large-scale bibliometric data, the authors measure how gender shapes who produces the case studies used to document research's impact beyond academia. Women make up 38% of impact-case contributors — still underrepresented, but a higher share than their 34% of research-output authorship — and are concentrated in education, health, and cultural-impact cases while men dominate commercialization pathways such as patenting. The findings give funders and evaluators concrete evidence of gendered inequality in how research impact is produced and credited, not just how research itself is authored.

## 3. Normative boundaries of AI in scientific work: Evidence from PhD researchers [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Francesco Angelini, Johan Lyrvall
**Date:** August 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25678
**Summary:** Drawing on Nature's 2025 Graduate Survey of 3,785 PhD students in STEM and health sciences, the authors use latent class analysis to map how comfortable researchers are with AI across tasks like writing, data analysis, experiment design, and literature review. Most fall into a "division of labour" profile that welcomes AI for literature-related tasks but resists it for work seen as core intellectual contribution, alongside smaller groups that are uniformly comfortable, uniformly uncomfortable, or undecided. The results suggest AI governance, disclosure rules, and doctoral training should target specific tasks rather than treat AI acceptance as a single acceptance-or-rejection dimension.

## 4. From Hallucination to Reliability: Generative Modeling and the Structure of Scientific Inference [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Charles Rathkopf
**Date:** August 26, 2026 (v3; originally posted April 11, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08526
**Summary:** Rathkopf argues that generative AI's tendency to hallucinate need not block it from producing genuine scientific knowledge, reframing hallucinations as non-strategic misrepresentations introduced by a model's generative process rather than defects inherited from training data. Using AlphaFold and the SEEDS weather model as case studies, he shows how scientific workflows draw on existing domain knowledge to filter or qualify AI outputs before they feed further inference, preventing hallucinated content from propagating downstream. The paper's key move is treating the whole workflow, not the model in isolation, as the proper unit for judging whether AI-assisted science is reliable.

## 5. Triadic Novelty: A Structural Typology of Science Innovation [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Jin Ai, Richard S. Steinberg, Chao Guo, Filipi Nascimento Silva
**Date:** August 25, 2026 (v3; originally posted June 21, 2025)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.17851
**Summary:** The authors propose that scientific novelty is better understood as a structural process than a single score, introducing a typology of three roles: Pioneers who open new topics, Mavericks who recombine distant fields, and Vanguards who strengthen weak, emerging connections. Applying the framework to the philanthropic and nonprofit-studies literature, they find these roles are rewarded very differently — Pioneer work is often overlooked unless later adopted, Maverick work is consistently recognized, and Vanguard work sees diminishing returns as its connections become mainstream. The results imply that citation-based evaluation systems can systematically under-reward the earliest, most unfamiliar contributions, with consequences for funding design and innovation policy.
