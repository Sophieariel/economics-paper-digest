# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-09-02

## 1. Filling holes in science draws collective attention, but most higher-order holes remain unexplored [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Jiajie Luo, James A. Evans
**Date:** August 28, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28822
**Summary:** The authors represent scientific knowledge as high-dimensional concept embeddings and use persistent homology to detect "holes" — gaps between ideas ranging from simple disconnections to complex higher-order cavities — then track which papers fill them. They find that filling holes the field already anticipates draws outsized citations and attention, especially in empirical fields, but as knowledge accumulates the number of higher-order holes grows explosively while the share science actually fills shrinks. The authors argue this leaves a widening frontier of unexplored higher-order combinations that current AI tools might help address, since humans struggle to search such high-dimensional spaces.

## 2. Tracing high-profile attention to questionable research as a case for funder due diligence [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Federica Silvi, Leslie D. McIntosh
**Date:** August 31, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30613
**Summary:** Using a known 2022 authorship-for-sale network as a case study, the authors trace how nearly 2,000 associated publications went on to be cited in policy documents, clinical guidelines, and patents, and whether the authors involved kept publishing and winning grants after the scheme was exposed. They find over 90% of the 278 traced authors continued publishing and 23% secured further grant funding, with almost a quarter of the flagged publications tied to research grants. The paper argues funders should incorporate authorship and network-structure checks, not just citation metrics, into due diligence to avoid continuing to fund researchers implicated in research misconduct.

## 3. Gender and the Production of Research Impact [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Sanger Wagner, Charles Rahal, Melinda C. Mills
**Date:** August 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26409
**Summary:** Combining records from the UK's Research Excellence Framework with bibliometric data, the authors examine who produces the case studies used to document research's real-world "impact" for national funding assessments, and how this differs by gender. They find women make up 38% of impact-case contributors — a smaller share than in the research population overall, but a larger share than among authors of standard research outputs — with strong gendering by domain: women are better represented in education, health, and cultural impact case studies, while men dominate commercialization pathways like patenting. The findings point to structural inequalities in how research impact is credited and rewarded, with implications for how funders and institutions design more equitable impact-assessment systems.

## 4. Normative boundaries of AI in scientific work: Evidence from PhD researchers [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Francesco Angelini, Johan Lyrvall
**Date:** August 26, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25678
**Summary:** Drawing on 3,785 STEM and health-science PhD students from Nature's 2025 Graduate Survey, the authors use latent class analysis to identify distinct patterns in how comfortable doctoral researchers are with AI across different research tasks. The dominant pattern is a "division of labour": PhD researchers broadly accept AI for literature search and summarization but resist it for tasks tied to intellectual contribution, like writing, data analysis, and experimental design, alongside smaller groups that are uniformly comfortable, uniformly uncomfortable, or undecided. The authors argue these task-specific boundaries — not a simple accept/reject split — should guide how doctoral training, disclosure rules, and research-evaluation policy treat AI going forward.

## 5. Research Design Tracking and Assessment for the Social Sciences [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Marco Rovera, Sergiu Burlacu, Dominique Cappelletti, Alessio Tomelleri, Sonia Marzadro, Martina Bazzoli, Annalisa Tassi, Jessica Gagete-Miranda
**Date:** August 27, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27049
**Summary:** The authors introduce ARDTrA, a new task for automatically detecting which causal research design a social-science paper uses and assessing how well it is applied, built on an expert-annotated dataset spanning six families of counterfactual designs. Testing a multi-turn retrieval-augmented pipeline across four retrieval strategies, four LLMs, and six embedding models, they find that the length of retrieved passages — not model or retrieval choice — explains most (52-66%) of the variance in performance. They also find that the research designs hardest for the automated system are not the ones human experts disagree about most, suggesting machine and human assessment struggle with different things — a caution for anyone hoping to automate research-design vetting in peer review.
