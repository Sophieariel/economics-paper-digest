# AI & Economics of Science Papers — 2026-09-03

## 1. Citing Less Critically: LLMs Reshape the Rhetoric and Reach of Scientific Citation [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Yixuan Liu, Lin Chen, Zhuoqi Liu, Jianglin Lu, Dakota Murray
**Date:** September 1, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01432
**Summary:** Using a masked-citation task across six LLMs and over 1,700 top NLP conference papers, the authors compare how models versus humans cite prior work in terms of rhetorical stance, popularity of cited papers, and social distance between authors. They find LLMs cite far less critically than humans, systematically over-cite older and already-popular papers even when contrasting findings, and draw on more socially distant authors rather than a scholar's close collaborators. The results suggest AI-assisted writing could broaden citation reach while simultaneously dulling critical engagement and amplifying existing visibility biases in the literature.

## 2. Tracing high-profile attention to questionable research as a case for funder due diligence [Science Policy]

**Authors:** Federica Silvi, Leslie D. McIntosh
**Date:** August 31, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30613
**Summary:** The authors trace nearly 2,000 publications linked to a known paper-mill/authorship-for-sale network exposed in 2022, tracking how far this questionable research penetrated into policy documents, clinical guidelines, and patents, and what happened to its authors afterward. They find dozens of these papers were cited in policy and clinical guidance and hundreds in patents, roughly a quarter were tied to research grants, and more than 90% of the 278 traced authors kept publishing (with nearly a quarter still securing funding) well after public exposure. The paper argues funders should treat authorship and network structure as a due-diligence signal alongside citation counts when deciding who to fund.

## 3. Filling holes in science draws collective attention, but most higher-order holes remain unexplored [Econ of Science]

**Authors:** Jiajie Luo, James A. Evans
**Date:** August 28, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28822
**Summary:** Representing scientific knowledge as concept embeddings and applying persistent homology, the authors identify "holes" — gaps and higher-order cavities between ideas — and the papers that fill them, across a large corpus of research works. They find that researchers who fill anticipated, lower-order holes attract outsized collective attention and novelty credit, especially in empirical fields, but as knowledge accumulates the number of unexplored higher-order holes grows much faster than science's capacity to fill them. The authors argue this widening gap marks a frontier where AI tools could help identify and pursue the higher-dimensional combinations human researchers are structurally unlikely to find on their own.

## 4. Do Large Language Models Favour Any Research Topics? [AI for Science]

**Authors:** Mike Thelwall
**Date:** August 31, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00323
**Summary:** Testing two LLMs (GPT-OSS-120B and Gemma 3 27B) as automated quality scorers on 73,489 health and life sciences articles, the author compares which topics and article types receive systematically higher or lower AI-assigned quality scores. He finds each model favors certain topics — for example GPT-OSS-120B rates virus, gene, and cell research higher while disfavoring survey- and patient-based studies, and the two models disagree with each other on topics like machine learning — with scores also shifting depending on whether full text or just title/abstract is given. The findings show at least one of the models exhibits genuine topic bias, warning against using LLMs for research evaluation without accounting for these systematic distortions.

## 5. Research Design Tracking and Assessment for the Social Sciences [Peer Review & Publication]

**Authors:** Marco Rovera, Sergiu Burlacu, Dominique Cappelletti, Alessio Tomelleri, Sonia Marzadro, Martina Bazzoli, Annalisa Tassi, Jessica Gagete-Miranda
**Date:** August 27, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27049
**Summary:** The authors build an expert-annotated dataset spanning six families of causal research designs and introduce a task, automated research design tracking and assessment, for detecting a paper's research design and judging how well it was applied. Testing a multi-turn retrieval-augmented pipeline across four retrieval strategies, four LLMs, and six embedding models, they find passage length — not model or retrieval choice — explains most of the variance in performance (52–66%), and that the designs hardest for the system are not the ones human experts disagree about most. This points to two distinct sources of difficulty in automating research-design assessment, with implications for using LLMs to support peer review of causal social-science research.
