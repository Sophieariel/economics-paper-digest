# China Economics Papers — 2026-05-01

## 1. China's Global Ownership
**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, Hong Ru
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106 (NBER Working Paper No. 35106)
**Abstract:** We study the global footprint and real effects of Chinese overseas corporate ownership. By assembling a comprehensive micro-level dataset of 161,773 firms across 159 countries (2012–2021), we independently reconstruct multi-layered ownership chains to trace capital through offshore tax havens to its ultimate origin. This approach reveals a global footprint substantially broader than official FDI statistics. Chinese-controlled foreign assets expanded at 20% annually, reaching $2.1 trillion or roughly 3% of global corporate assets by 2021. Chinese investors—particularly state-owned enterprises (SOEs)—strategically target R&D-intensive and supply-chain-linked firms. Following acquisition, target firms increase capital stock and R&D expenditures, yet these inputs fail to generate higher patent output and are accompanied by a significant decline in profitability. We document a novel "innovation spillback" mechanism: while target innovation remains stagnant, Chinese parent firms experience a sharp acceleration in granted patents following their first developed-economy acquisition. Furthermore, a greater Chinese presence crowds out R&D at non-target peer firms, though aggregate industry-level innovation remains unchanged. China thus represents a distinctively state-driven model of global ownership that accepts weaker near-term performance to internalize technological capacity at home.

## 2. Homemade Foreign Trading
**Authors:** Zhiguo He, Yuehan Wang, Xiaoquan Zhu
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35095 (NBER Working Paper No. 35095)
**Abstract:** Using cross-border holding data from all custodians in China's Stock Connect, we provide evidence that Chinese mainland insiders tend to evade see-through surveillance by round-tripping via the Stock Connect program. After the regulatory reform of Northbound Investor Identification in 2018, the correlation between insider trading and northbound flows decays, and so does the return predictability of northbound flows. The reduction of return predictability is especially pronounced among less prestigious foreign custodians and cross-operating mainland custodians, behind which mainland insiders are more likely to hide. We further show that the 2018 reform weakens price informativeness for stocks with greater exposure to such "homemade" foreign investors, shedding light on the role of regulatory cooperation over capital market integration.

## 3. When Do Financial Frictions Matter for Misallocation?
**Authors:** Yan Bai, Dan Lu, Xu Tian, Yajie Wang
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34930 (NBER Working Paper No. 34930)
**Abstract:** We revisit the role of financial frictions in shaping capital misallocation by developing a model disciplined by firm-level borrowing costs and average revenue product of capital (ARPK). Using Chinese manufacturing data, we document substantial dispersion in ARPK and a strong positive correlation between ARPK and borrowing costs—features absent in U.S. data. We build a heterogeneous-firm model with endogenous firm-specific borrowing costs and find that eliminating financial frictions raises total factor productivity (TFP) by 25 percentage points. In contrast, in an environment without other capital distortions, removing financial frictions raises TFP by less than 2 percentage points. The stark difference arises from the interaction between financial frictions and permanent firm-level distortions, which generate endogenous financial heterogeneity and selection, making the most productive firms the most constrained. Our findings imply that financial frictions can be highly distortionary when other sources of misallocation are present.

## 4. Where Has All the Dynamism Gone? Productivity Growth in China's Manufacturing Sector, 1998–2013
**Authors:** Loren Brandt, Johannes Van Biesebroeck, Luhang Wang, Yifan Zhang
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 181)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304387826000039 (DOI: 10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103720)
**Abstract:** We examine productivity dynamics in China's manufacturing sector by addressing data quality concerns in the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) survey of "above-scale" firms. To overcome these issues, we exploit the annual State Taxation Administration (STA) firm-level survey, which provides a reliable alternative for 2007–2013. We recover the sampling scheme of the STA survey and use it to simulate samples comparable to earlier years, allowing us to construct consistent productivity estimates over a longer horizon. Our results reveal a marked slowdown in revenue-based total factor productivity growth that cuts across all industries, ownership types, and regions. The loss of dynamism in the private sector and the reduced contribution of firm entry to aggregate productivity growth are especially prominent. The productivity level of newly entered firms falls significantly relative to that of incumbents, and data from the Business Registry reveal a sharp drop in the rate of new firm entry over this period, especially by foreign-invested enterprises. These findings highlight a fundamental shift in the sources of growth in Chinese manufacturing.

## 5. English's significance: Exam performance by subject and future income in China
**Authors:** Ruixue Jia, Hongbin Li, Yu Qin, Qiuyi Wang, Jing Wu
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 181)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304387826000398 (DOI: 10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103756)
**Abstract:** We examine how subject-specific test scores from China's National College Entrance Exam (Gaokao) predict future income, using a sample of approximately 190,000 individuals born between 1980 and 1985. While Chinese, Math, and English scores are all positively associated with later earnings, English emerges as the most influential predictor of income, with effects that persist over the life cycle. The English premium is especially pronounced for individuals working in skill-intensive sectors and in cities more integrated into the global economy. Our findings underscore the lasting importance of English proficiency as a form of human capital tied to China's integration into international markets, and they speak to ongoing debates about language requirements in China's education system.
