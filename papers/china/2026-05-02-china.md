# China Economics Papers — 2026-05-02

## 1. A Tale of Two Countries – The Real Estate Crises in 1990s Japan and Contemporary China
**Authors:** Kenneth S. Rogoff, Yuanchen Yang
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35054
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35054
**Abstract:** This paper examines how China's real estate sector has shifted from driving growth to becoming an economic drag since 2018. The analysis documents that housing comprises nearly 70 percent of household wealth, and the sector accounts for roughly one-third of aggregate demand when including related industries. The study reveals striking parallels in investment dynamics and consumption responses between China's current downturn and Japan's 1990s real estate collapse, despite their institutional differences. The authors emphasize how real-side economic channels—beyond just banking effects—generate sustained output losses when real estate markets deteriorate significantly.

## 2. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes
**Authors:** Chang Ma, Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35056
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056
**Abstract:** This paper examines China's persistent current account surplus and its underlying causes. While trade and industrial policies contribute to sector-specific imbalances, structural factors such as an unbalanced sex ratio and uneven access to financing by state-owned and non-state firms are more important determinants of the current account imbalance. The authors conclude that addressing the economy-wide surplus requires structural reforms beyond short-term macroeconomic interventions.

## 3. AI Patents in the United States and China: Measurement, Organization, and Knowledge Flows
**Authors:** Hanming Fang, Xian Gu, Hanyin Yan, Wu Zhu
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35022
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35022
**Abstract:** The researchers developed an advanced classifier to identify AI patents, testing it against both U.S. patents (1976–2023) and Chinese patents (2010–2023). Their findings show rapid growth in AI patenting in both countries, with China surpassing the United States in recent annual patent counts. Innovation organization differs significantly: American AI patenting concentrates among large corporations and established tech hubs, while Chinese patenting is more geographically spread with greater university and state-owned enterprise involvement. The study also reveals that Chinese AI inventors rely more heavily on U.S. frontier knowledge, indicating continued technological interdependence rather than complete decoupling between the two countries.

## 4. Homemade Foreign Trading
**Authors:** Zhiguo He, Yuehan Wang, Xiaoquan Zhu
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35095
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35095
**Abstract:** Using cross-border holding data from all custodians in China's Stock Connect, this paper provides evidence that Chinese mainland insiders evade see-through surveillance by round-tripping via the program. The research shows that following a 2018 regulatory reform on investor identification, the predictive power of northbound flows diminished, along with their association to insider trading activities. Effects were most noticeable among less established foreign custodians. The findings demonstrate the role of regulatory cooperation in capital market integration.

## 5. China's Global Ownership
**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, Hong Ru
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35106
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This paper examines Chinese firms' international corporate ownership patterns using data covering 161,773 firms across 159 countries (2012–2021). The researchers reconstructed ownership networks to uncover the full scope of Chinese capital flows, which expanded at 20% annually, reaching $2.1 trillion or roughly 3% of global corporate assets by 2021. Chinese investors—particularly state-owned enterprises—deliberately acquire companies emphasizing research and development. While target companies boost capital investment and R&D spending post-acquisition, these investments do not yield proportional innovation gains and coincide with profitability declines. Notably, Chinese parent companies significantly accelerate patent acquisitions following their initial developed-market purchases—a mechanism termed "innovation spillback." The study concludes that China represents a "state-driven model of global ownership that accepts weaker near-term performance to internalize technological capacity at home."
