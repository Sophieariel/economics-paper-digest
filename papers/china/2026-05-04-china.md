## 1. AI Patents in the United States and China: Measurement, Organization, and Knowledge Flows

**Authors:** Hanming Fang, Xian Gu, Hanyin Yan, Wu Zhu  
**Date:** April 2026  
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35022  
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35022  
**Topics:** Industrial policy, innovation, technology  

**Abstract:** The researchers created a refined classification tool for identifying AI patents by enhancing PatentSBERTa using hand-labeled USPTO data. Their approach achieved 97.0% precision, 91.3% recall, and a 94.0% F1 score, and successfully transferred to Chinese patent databases. Analyzing U.S. patents (1976–2023) and Chinese patents (2010–2023), they found both nations experienced significant AI patent growth with converging field distributions, though China recently surpassed the U.S. in annual filings. However, innovation structures diverged markedly: American AI patents concentrated among established corporations and regional clusters, while Chinese AI patents showed greater geographic distribution with stronger participation from universities and state enterprises. Listed companies in both countries derived substantial market-value benefits from AI patents. Citation patterns revealed sustained technological interdependence rather than isolation, with Chinese inventors drawing more heavily on American frontier innovations.

## 2. A Tale of Two Countries – The Real Estate Crises in 1990s Japan and Contemporary China

**Authors:** Kenneth S. Rogoff, Yuanchen Yang  
**Date:** April 2026  
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35054  
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35054  
**Topics:** Financial markets, real estate, macroeconomics  

**Abstract:** Real estate has been fundamental to China's economic expansion, but since 2018 the sector's contribution has diminished substantially, shifting from an engine of growth to a significant economic burden. Policy tightening may have initiated the downturn, yet it reflects underlying imbalances in a sector representing roughly one-third of total demand when including related industries and infrastructure. Housing comprises approximately 70 percent of household wealth, so the ongoing price decline has generated considerable negative wealth effects, intensifying the contraction via reduced consumption, investment, and confidence. The paper examines the macroeconomic propagation of China's real estate downturn and evaluates dangers of extended weakness if deterioration persists. It contrasts China's situation with Japan's real estate collapse in the 1990s, revealing notable similarities in investment behavior and consumption patterns despite major institutional variations. The research underscores the significance of real-economy channels, including non-banking amplification mechanisms, in producing sustained output declines after real estate collapses.

## 3. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes

**Authors:** Chang Ma, Shang-Jin Wei  
**Date:** April 2026  
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35056  
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056  
**Topics:** Trade, financial markets, macroeconomics  

**Abstract:** The research examines why China maintains a substantial current account surplus and its implications for trading partners. Industrial and trade policies often lead to sector-level imbalances but play a relatively limited role in the economy-wide surplus. Instead, the paper identifies structural factors—particularly an unbalanced sex ratio and unequal access to financing between state-owned and non-state firms—as more significant contributors to the overall imbalance. The authors conclude that while short-term macroeconomic stimulus can temporarily reduce the surplus by boosting imports, addressing the underlying structural issues requires fundamental reforms for any sustained resolution.

## 4. Homemade Foreign Trading

**Authors:** Zhiguo He, Yuehan Wang, Xiaoquan Zhu  
**Date:** April 2026  
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35095  
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35095  
**Topics:** Financial markets, capital market regulation  

**Abstract:** Using cross-border holding data from China's Stock Connect custodians, the researchers document evidence that mainland insiders circumvent regulatory oversight by conducting round-trip transactions through the program. Following a 2018 reform requiring investor identification for northbound flows, the predictive power of these flows diminished, along with their correlation to insider trading activities. This weakening was most evident among less reputable foreign custodians and dual-operating mainland custodians—venues where insiders could more easily conceal their activities. The reform additionally reduced price informativeness, particularly in stocks with high exposure to homemade foreign investors. The study emphasizes how regulatory cooperation strengthens capital market integration.

## 5. China's Global Ownership

**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, Hong Ru  
**Date:** April 2026  
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35106  
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106  
**Topics:** Firm dynamics, trade, industrial policy, FDI  

**Abstract:** By assembling a comprehensive micro-level dataset of 161,773 firms across 159 countries (2012–2021), the authors independently reconstruct multi-layered ownership chains to trace capital through offshore tax havens to its ultimate origin. This approach reveals a global footprint substantially broader than official FDI statistics. Chinese-controlled foreign assets expanded at 20% annually, reaching $2.1 trillion or roughly 3% of global corporate assets by 2021. Chinese investors—particularly state-owned enterprises (SOEs)—strategically target R&D-intensive and supply-chain-linked firms. Following acquisition, target firms increase capital stock and R&D expenditures, yet these inputs fail to generate higher patent output and are accompanied by a significant decline in profitability. The paper documents a novel "innovation spillback" mechanism: while target innovation remains stagnant, Chinese parent firms experience a sharp acceleration in granted patents following their first developed-economy acquisition. Furthermore, a greater Chinese presence crowds out R&D at non-target peer firms, though aggregate industry-level innovation remains unchanged. China thus represents a distinctively state-driven model of global ownership that accepts weaker near-term performance to internalize technological capacity at home.
