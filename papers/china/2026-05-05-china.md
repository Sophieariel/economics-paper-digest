# China Economics Papers — 2026-05-05

## 1. AI Patents in the United States and China: Measurement, Organization, and Knowledge Flows

**Authors:** Hanming Fang, Xian Gu, Hanyin Yan, and Wu Zhu
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35022
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35022
**Abstract:** The authors develop a high-precision machine learning classifier (97.0% precision, 91.3% recall, 94.0% F1) to identify AI patents in U.S. data from 1976–2023 and Chinese data from 2010–2023. They document rapid growth in AI patenting in both countries and broad convergence in AI patenting intensity and composition, even as China surpasses the U.S. in recent annual patent counts. Organizational structure differs sharply: U.S. AI innovation concentrates among large private incumbents and regional hubs, while Chinese AI patenting is more geographically diffuse with larger roles for universities and state-owned enterprises. Cross-border patent citations reveal continued technological interdependence rather than decoupling, with Chinese AI inventors relying more heavily on U.S. frontier knowledge than vice versa.

## 2. A Tale of Two Countries – The Real Estate Crises in 1990s Japan and Contemporary China

**Authors:** Kenneth S. Rogoff and Yuanchen Yang
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35054
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35054
**Abstract:** This paper compares China's recent real estate downturn with Japan's 1990s collapse. The authors document how China's housing sector, which represents roughly one-third of aggregate demand, has shifted from a key engine of expansion into a major drag on economic activity since 2018. With housing comprising nearly 70% of household wealth, price corrections have triggered substantial negative wealth effects dampening consumption and investment. The paper identifies striking parallels between the two countries' investment dynamics and consumption responses despite institutional differences, and emphasizes real-side transmission channels—beyond banking mechanisms—in explaining prolonged output losses following real estate busts.

## 3. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes

**Authors:** Chang Ma and Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35056
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056
**Abstract:** China's substantial trade surplus has created friction with trading partners. This paper examines the structural drivers of this imbalance. While sector-specific industrial and trade policies generate localized imbalances, they have limited impact on the overall surplus. The research identifies structural causes—including demographic sex ratio imbalances and unequal access to financing between state-owned enterprises and private firms—as the primary drivers. Short-term macroeconomic stimulus may increase imports temporarily, but resolving the underlying surplus requires addressing these foundational structural issues through comprehensive reform.

## 4. Consumer Tobacco Product Choices in China: The Impact of a Ban on Flavored E-cigarettes

**Authors:** Hua Wang, Yuhan Deng, Donald S. Kenkel, Alan D. Mathios, and Sen Zeng
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35048
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35048
**Abstract:** This study examines China's 2022 ban on flavored e-cigarettes using discrete choice experiments conducted with approximately 600 adult smokers in 2021 and 2023. The authors find that the ban decreases stated preferences for e-cigarettes but has the unintended consequence of increasing stated preferences for conventional cigarettes. The analysis predicts that the choice share of flavored e-cigarettes when illegal but loosely enforced is 53% of the predicted share when legal, suggesting substantial continued illegal consumption—a finding aligned with post-ban market observations and raising questions about the efficacy of product bans under weak enforcement.

## 5. China's Global Ownership

**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, and Hong Ru
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35106
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** The authors examine Chinese corporate ownership across 161,773 firms in 159 countries from 2012–2021, tracing ownership chains through offshore entities. They find that Chinese-controlled foreign assets reached $2.1 trillion by 2021, growing at roughly 20% annually. Chinese investors—particularly state-owned enterprises—strategically target R&D-intensive and supply-chain-linked firms. While target firms boost capital spending and R&D investment following acquisition, profitability declines and patent output stagnates at the target. Notably, Chinese parent companies experience accelerated patent growth after acquiring developed-economy firms, documenting an "innovation spillback" mechanism that concentrates technological gains domestically rather than at acquisition targets.
