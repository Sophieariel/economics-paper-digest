## 1. China's Global Ownership

**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, Hong Ru
**Date:** April 28, 2026 (revised May 4, 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This research examines Chinese corporate investments abroad by analyzing 161,773 firms across 159 nations from 2012–2021. The authors reconstruct ownership chains to identify ultimate Chinese control, revealing that Chinese-controlled foreign assets expanded at 20% annually, reaching $2.1 trillion or roughly 3% of global corporate assets by 2021. The study finds that state-owned enterprises strategically acquire research-focused and supply-chain-connected companies. While target firms increase capital spending and R&D investment post-acquisition, they fail to generate stronger patent outputs and experience profitability declines. Notably, Chinese parent companies show accelerated patent growth following their initial developed-economy acquisition—a phenomenon the authors term "innovation spillback." Additionally, Chinese ownership crowds out R&D at competing peer firms, though overall industry innovation levels remain stable. The research concludes that China exemplifies a "state-driven model of global ownership" prioritizing long-term technological capacity accumulation domestically over immediate financial returns.

## 2. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes

**Authors:** Chang Ma, Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056
**Abstract:** This paper examines China's persistent current account surplus and its underlying causes. The authors find that while "industrial and trade policies often lead to sector-level imbalances, they play a relatively limited role" in the overall economic surplus. Instead, structural factors—particularly demographic imbalances in sex ratios and disparities in financing access between state-owned and non-state enterprises—emerge as more significant drivers of the imbalance. The research suggests that while "macroeconomic stimulus can boost imports and reduce the surplus in the short run," addressing the current account problem requires "reforms aiming at addressing the structural problems."

## 3. When Do Financial Frictions Matter for Misallocation?

**Authors:** Yan Bai, Dan Lu, Xu Tian, Yajie Wang
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34930
**Abstract:** This research examines how financial frictions contribute to capital misallocation using a model calibrated to firm-level borrowing costs and capital productivity measures. The study analyzes Chinese manufacturing data and reveals substantial differences in capital returns across firms, with a notable correlation between higher returns and elevated borrowing expenses—a pattern not observed in U.S. data. The authors construct a theoretical framework incorporating firm-specific borrowing costs and additional capital distortions. Their findings indicate that eliminating financial frictions alone produces a 25 percentage point productivity increase when other distortions persist, but generates less than a 2 percentage point gain without such distortions. The research concludes that financial constraints become particularly consequential when interacting with other misallocation sources that concentrate constraints on the most productive firms.

## 4. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy

**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893
**Abstract:** The researchers investigate whether industrial policies promoting clean energy can boost growth and welfare while reducing carbon emissions. They examine Chinese solar subsidies implemented across city-regions from 2004–2020, constructing new datasets on policies, patents, and output. Using synthetic-difference-in-differences methodology, they found that "production and innovation subsidies were more effective than demand-side (installation) subsidies in generating large and persistent increases in local innovation, net entry, production and exports." Demand policies did reduce local pollution. Through structural modeling accounting for business stealing and knowledge spillovers, counterfactual analysis revealed local effects explain 40%–50% of aggregate solar changes, social benefits exceeded subsidy costs by 65% (doubling when environmental gains are included), and innovation subsidies proved most cost-effective.

## 5. Currency Pegs, Trade Imbalances and Unemployment: A Reevaluation of the China Shock

**Authors:** Bumsoo Kim, Marc De la Barrera, Masao Fukui
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34823
**Abstract:** The researchers develop a quantitative model examining how China's currency peg influenced U.S. economic outcomes during China's rapid growth period. Their findings indicate that "the peg temporarily boosts China's export growth by preventing an appreciation of the Chinese currency, thereby amplifying the US labor-market consequences of the China shock." The analysis reveals the peg contributed significantly to manufacturing decline, trade deficit expansion, and employment fluctuations. Notably, they conclude "the overall welfare impact of the China shock remains significant and positive," highlighting that exchange-rate dynamics play a crucial role in understanding trade-related economic disruptions.
