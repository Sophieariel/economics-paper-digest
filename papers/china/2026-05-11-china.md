# China Economics Papers — 2026-05-11

## 1. China's Global Ownership
**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, Hong Ru
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106 (NBER Working Paper No. 35106)
**Abstract:** We study the global footprint and real effects of Chinese overseas corporate ownership. By assembling a comprehensive micro-level dataset of 161,773 firms across 159 countries (2012–2021), we independently reconstruct multi-layered ownership chains to trace capital through offshore tax havens to its ultimate origin. This approach reveals a global footprint substantially broader than official FDI statistics. Chinese-controlled foreign assets expanded at 20% annually, reaching $2.1 trillion or roughly 3% of global corporate assets by 2021. Chinese investors—particularly state-owned enterprises (SOEs)—strategically target R&D-intensive and supply-chain-linked firms. Following acquisition, target firms increase capital stock and R&D expenditures, yet these inputs fail to generate higher patent output and are accompanied by a significant decline in profitability. We document a novel "innovation spillback" mechanism: while target innovation remains stagnant, Chinese parent firms experience a sharp acceleration in granted patents following their first developed-economy acquisition. Furthermore, a greater Chinese presence crowds out R&D at non-target peer firms, though aggregate industry-level innovation remains unchanged. China thus represents a distinctively state-driven model of global ownership that accepts weaker near-term performance to internalize technological capacity at home.

## 2. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy
**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893 (NBER Working Paper No. 34893)
**Abstract:** Can industrial policies that promote clean energy increase a country's growth and welfare while reducing carbon emissions? We study the impact of Chinese solar subsidies whose implementation by city-regions went alongside massive expansion of the sector and a dramatic fall in global solar prices. Using detailed city and firm panel data (2004–2020) and exploiting the idiosyncratic timing of subsidy adoption across Chinese city-regions via synthetic difference-in-differences, we find that production and innovation subsidies were more effective than demand-side (installation) subsidies in generating large and persistent increases in local innovation, net entry, production, and exports, though demand policies reduced local pollution. A spatial economic model with endogenous innovation shows that local effects remain substantial at the macro level, explaining 40–50% of aggregate changes in solar innovation, prices, and revenues. Social benefits to Chinese citizens exceed subsidy costs by 65% (and roughly double when environmental benefits are included). Innovation subsidies are the most cost-effective policy instrument examined.

## 3. A Few Bad Apples? Academic Dishonesty, Political Selection, and Institutional Performance in China
**Authors:** Zhuang Liu, Wenwei Peng, Shaoda Wang
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34747 (NBER Working Paper No. 34747)
**Abstract:** We apply plagiarism-detection algorithms to approximately 500,000 publicly available graduate dissertations in China, and link plagiarism records to rich administrative data. We document four main findings. First, plagiarism is pervasive and predicts adverse political selection: plagiarists are more likely to enter and advance in the public sector. Second, once in power, plagiarists perform worse: judges with plagiarism histories issue more preferential rulings and attract a greater number of appeals. Third, dishonesty spills over across judges and from judges to lawyers. Fourth, exploiting the staggered adoption of plagiarism-detection tools across dissertation committees, we find that enforcing academic integrity leads to modest improvements in future professional conduct. Together, our findings document a mechanism through which weak academic integrity norms contribute to institutional underperformance in China's public sector.

## 4. Raising Grievances to the State: The Political Economic Effects of Anti-Corruption Crackdowns on Labor Activism in China
**Authors:** Huiyi Chen
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 181, article 103758)
**Source:** https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/deveco/v181y2026ics0304387826000416.html (DOI: 10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103758)
**Abstract:** This paper examines how government anti-corruption inspections influenced workers' willingness to engage in collective labor action in China between 2011 and 2020. Using city-level data on strike incidents from China Labour Bulletin, the author finds that strike incidents rise sharply after a city's first high-profile anti-corruption inspection, driven largely by wage arrears from private firms concentrated in construction and manufacturing. The mechanism is a weakening of firm–government connections: inspections disrupted patronage networks, redirecting official attention in ways that improved workers' prospects for successful grievance resolution through state channels. Strike activity also propagated geographically through network effects, with neighboring and subsequently inspected cities responding to enforcement activity elsewhere. The findings speak to how anti-corruption campaigns shape the political economy of labor relations in developmental states.

## 5. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes
**Authors:** Chang Ma, Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056 (NBER Working Paper No. 35056)
**Abstract:** China runs a sizable current account surplus that has generated frictions with its trading partners. While industrial and trade policies often lead to sector-level imbalances, they play a relatively limited role in the economy-wide surplus. Instead, structural factors—particularly an imbalanced sex ratio and unequal access to financing between state-owned and non-state enterprises—appear to be the more important drivers of China's macroeconomic imbalance. Short-term macroeconomic stimulus may temporarily increase imports and reduce the surplus, but any long-term solution would need to involve reforms aiming at addressing the structural problems. The paper documents patterns in China's current account dynamics and offers a framework for distinguishing between cyclical, policy-driven, and structural explanations.
