## 1. China's Global Ownership
**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, and Hong Ru
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This paper examines how Chinese companies have expanded their ownership stakes globally. Using data from over 161,000 firms across 159 countries (2012–2021), the authors trace ownership through complex international structures to identify ultimate Chinese control. They find that Chinese-controlled foreign assets expanded at 20% annually, reaching $2.1 trillion or roughly 3% of global corporate assets by 2021. State-owned Chinese enterprises strategically acquire firms with strong research capabilities. While target firms boost capital and R&D spending post-acquisition, these investments do not translate to increased patent output and coincide with reduced profitability. Notably, Chinese parent companies experience rapid patent growth following their first developed-economy acquisition — a phenomenon the authors term an "innovation spillback" mechanism — suggesting China's model prioritizes transferring technology home over immediate financial returns.

## 2. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes
**Authors:** Chang Ma and Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056
**Abstract:** This paper examines China's persistent current account surplus and its causes. The authors find that industrial and trade policies often lead to sector-level imbalances but have limited impact on the economy-wide surplus. Instead, structural factors — including demographic imbalances and differential financing access between state-owned and non-state enterprises — are identified as primary drivers of the aggregate surplus. The research suggests that while short-term macroeconomic stimulus may reduce the surplus through increased imports, addressing the underlying structural issues would be necessary for sustained long-term solutions.

## 3. When Do Financial Frictions Matter for Misallocation?
**Authors:** Yan Bai, Dan Lu, Xu Tian, and Yajie Wang
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34930
**Abstract:** This paper examines how financial frictions contribute to capital misallocation using a model informed by firm-level borrowing costs and capital productivity metrics. Analyzing Chinese manufacturing firm data, the researchers find substantial variation in firms' capital productivity alongside a noteworthy correlation between productivity and borrowing costs — a pattern absent in U.S. data. Their heterogeneous-firm model incorporates endogenous borrowing costs and additional capital distortions. Key findings indicate that eliminating financial frictions alone raises total factor productivity by 25 percentage points when other distortions persist. However, removing only financial frictions without addressing other capital distortions increases productivity by less than 2 percentage points. The research demonstrates that financial constraints become significantly more damaging when combined with permanent firm-level distortions, which create selection effects that disproportionately constrain the most productive firms.

## 4. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy
**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, and Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether industrial policies promoting clean energy can boost economic growth and welfare while reducing emissions. The authors examine Chinese solar subsidies implemented across city-regions between 2004 and 2020, utilizing new data on solar policies, patents, and output. They find that production and innovation subsidies were more effective than demand-side (installation) subsidies in generating large and persistent increases in local innovation, net entry, production, and exports. Using a structural quantitative model, counterfactual analysis reveals that subsidy effects explain 40–50% of aggregate changes in solar innovation, prices, and revenues. The research concludes that social benefits to Chinese citizens exceeded subsidy costs by 65%, doubling when environmental gains are included, with innovation subsidies proving the most cost-effective policy instrument.

## 5. A Few Bad Apples? Academic Dishonesty, Political Selection, and Institutional Performance in China
**Authors:** Zhuang Liu, Wenwei Peng, and Shaoda Wang
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34747
**Abstract:** This study examines how academic misconduct correlates with career outcomes and professional conduct in China. Using plagiarism-detection algorithms applied to approximately 500,000 graduate dissertations, the researchers uncover hidden dishonesty and connect it to administrative records. Key findings include: plagiarists disproportionately enter public sector roles and advance within them; judges with plagiarism histories make more biased rulings and face increased appeals; plagiarizing judges negatively influence colleagues and legal professionals; and enforcing academic integrity standards produces measurable improvements in future professional behavior, though the gains remain modest. The paper provides novel evidence on how political selection processes shape institutional quality in China.
