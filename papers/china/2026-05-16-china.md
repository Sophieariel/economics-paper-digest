# China Economics Papers — 2026-05-16

## 1. China's Global Ownership
**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, and Hong Ru
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This study assembles a dataset of 161,773 firms across 159 countries from 2012–2021, reconstructing ownership structures through offshore entities to identify ultimate Chinese ownership—revealing holdings substantially larger than official foreign direct investment figures suggest. Chinese-controlled foreign assets reached approximately $2.1 trillion by 2021, representing roughly 3% of global corporate assets. Chinese investors, particularly state-owned enterprises, deliberately acquire R&D-intensive and supply-chain-connected companies. Following acquisitions, target firms increase capital investments and R&D spending, yet fail to boost patent outputs and experience declining profitability. An "innovation spillback" occurs where Chinese parent companies accelerate patent generation following developed-economy acquisitions, while target firm innovation stagnates. Increased Chinese ownership also reduces R&D activities at competing non-target firms, though overall industry innovation levels remain stable. The study characterizes Chinese global ownership as a "state-driven model" accepting near-term performance trade-offs to accumulate technological capabilities domestically.

## 2. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes
**Authors:** Chang Ma and Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056
**Abstract:** This paper examines China's substantial current account surplus and its effects on trading partners. The authors find that while industrial and trade policies create sector-specific imbalances, they play a "relatively limited role in the economy-wide surplus." Instead, structural factors—particularly an unbalanced sex ratio and differential access to financing between state-owned and non-state enterprises—emerge as more significant drivers. The research suggests macroeconomic stimulus can temporarily boost imports and reduce the surplus, though a "long-term solution would need to involve reforms" targeting these underlying structural issues.

## 3. When Do Financial Frictions Matter for Misallocation?
**Authors:** Yan Bai, Dan Lu, Xu Tian, and Yajie Wang
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34930
**Abstract:** This paper reassesses how financial frictions contribute to capital misallocation using a model informed by firm-level borrowing costs and average revenue product of capital. Analysis of Chinese manufacturing firms reveals substantial variations in capital productivity alongside a strong correlation between productivity and borrowing expenses—findings not observed in comparable U.S. data. The authors construct a heterogeneous-firm framework incorporating endogenous firm-specific borrowing costs and additional capital distortions modeled as exogenous wedges. Results indicate that removing financial frictions would boost total factor productivity by 25 percentage points when other capital distortions persist, versus less than 2 percentage points without such distortions. The research concludes that financial frictions generate particularly severe economic damage when combined with other misallocation sources.

## 4. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy
**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, and Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether industrial policies promoting clean energy can boost growth and welfare while reducing emissions. The authors analyze Chinese solar subsidies implemented across city-regions from 2004–2020, using synthetic-difference-in-differences methodology with newly constructed panel data. Production and innovation subsidies proved more effective than demand-side installation subsidies for driving innovation, firm entry, production, and exports locally. Installation subsidies did reduce pollution. A structural quantitative spatial model reveals that "local effects remain substantial at the macro level explaining 40%–50% of the aggregate changes in solar innovation, prices and revenues." Social benefits to Chinese citizens exceeded subsidy costs by 65% (doubling when environmental gains are included), with innovation subsidies proving most cost-effective overall.

## 5. A Few Bad Apples? Academic Dishonesty, Political Selection, and Institutional Performance in China
**Authors:** Zhuang Liu, Wenwei Peng, and Shaoda Wang
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34747
**Abstract:** This research investigates academic misconduct and its consequences in China's political system. The scholars applied plagiarism-detection algorithms to approximately 500,000 graduate dissertations and linked the findings to administrative records. Key findings: plagiarists disproportionately enter government positions; judges with plagiarism histories issue preferential rulings and receive more appeals; plagiarizing officials create negative spillover effects on colleagues; and enforcement of academic integrity standards yields modest improvements in future professional behavior. The study suggests that hidden dishonesty affects institutional performance and political selection, with some mitigation possible through increased transparency mechanisms such as trial livestreaming.
