# China Economics Papers — 2026-05-17

## 1. Product Quality, the Scope of Export Destinations and Skill Demand for Sustainable Development: An Investigation for China
**Authors:** Zhuangxiong Yu, Yang Yang, Jiemiao Dong, Pundarik Mukhopadhaya
**Date:** April 8, 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, available online)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304387826000775 (DOI: 10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103794)
**Abstract:** This paper incorporates firms' export-destination breadth into the skill-selection framework to show that product quality shapes skill demand through a non-linear mechanism mediated by export scope dynamics. Using data from China's Industrial Firm Census and China Customs Trade database for Guangdong Province (2013–2015), the authors find that as firms upgrade product quality, the range of export destinations first expands—because gains in consumer satisfaction outweigh the additional costs of entering more markets—and then contracts as marginal quality benefits diminish relative to the growing cost of serving a wide set of destinations. Skill demand mirrors this trajectory: at lower quality levels two opposing forces operate simultaneously—a skill-enhancing task-specialization effect and a skill-reducing export-scope-expansion effect—that together can produce an insignificant or even negative net effect on skilled labor demand. Once product quality crosses a threshold, the task-specialization effect dominates and the simultaneous contraction of export scope further amplifies the upward trend, producing a robustly positive skill premium. The results connect product quality upgrading, trade patterns, and labor-market polarization in a large developing economy and have implications for sustainable industrial policy.

## 2. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy
**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893 (NBER Working Paper No. 34893)
**Abstract:** This paper asks whether industrial policies promoting clean energy can simultaneously boost growth and welfare while reducing emissions. The authors assemble new city- and firm-level panel datasets on Chinese solar subsidies, patents, and output covering 2004–2020 and apply synthetic-difference-in-differences methods. Production and innovation subsidies are found to be far more effective than demand-side installation subsidies in generating large and persistent increases in local innovation, net firm entry, production, and exports; demand subsidies nonetheless reduced local pollution. A quantitative spatial model with endogenous innovation shows that local policy effects account for 40–50 percent of aggregate changes in solar-sector innovation, prices, and revenues—a substantial macroeconomic footprint. Social benefits to Chinese citizens exceed subsidy costs by 65 percent, and this ratio roughly doubles once environmental benefits are included. Among subsidy types, innovation subsidies prove the most cost-effective, though all types raise aggregate welfare. The findings provide the first large-scale causal evidence on the returns to green industrial policy and suggest that the design of subsidies—not just their scale—is decisive.

## 3. Currency Pegs, Trade Imbalances and Unemployment: A Reevaluation of the China Shock
**Authors:** Bumsoo Kim, Marc De la Barrea, Masao Fukui
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34823 (NBER Working Paper No. 34823)
**Abstract:** This paper develops a dynamic quantitative model to examine how China's currency peg interacted with the country's rapid productivity growth to shape the U.S. labor market—the core of the "China shock" debate. The peg prevented the renminbi from appreciating as China's export capacity expanded, temporarily amplifying China's export surge and magnifying the resulting dislocations in U.S. manufacturing employment and wages. The peg also suppressed domestic Chinese consumption relative to saving, enlarging the bilateral trade deficit and contributing to the broader pattern of global imbalances. Calibrating the model to trade and employment flow statistics, the authors show that exchange-rate adjustment—or the lack thereof—is quantitatively important for understanding the size and persistence of the China shock on U.S. labor markets. Despite these employment costs, the overall welfare impact of the China shock on the U.S. remains significantly positive in the model, with consumers gaining substantially from lower import prices. The results underscore exchange-rate regimes as a first-order determinant of how trade shocks propagate through labor markets.

## 4. A Few Bad Apples? Academic Dishonesty, Political Selection, and Institutional Performance in China
**Authors:** Zhuang Liu, Wenwei Peng, Shaoda Wang
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34747 (NBER Working Paper No. 34747)
**Abstract:** This paper applies advanced plagiarism-detection algorithms to approximately 500,000 publicly available graduate dissertations in China to study academic misconduct and its downstream consequences. Linking dissertation records to administrative career and judicial data, the authors document four main findings. First, plagiarism is pervasive and strongly predicts adverse political selection: plagiarists are significantly more likely to enter and advance in the public sector. Second, exploiting quasi-random case assignments in the judiciary, plagiarist judges are found to issue more preferential rulings and attract a greater number of appeals; trial livestreaming partially mitigates these effects. Third, dishonesty exhibits spillovers—corrupt behavior propagates across co-assigned judges and between judges and lawyers they interact with repeatedly. Fourth, exploiting the staggered adoption of anti-plagiarism detection tools, the authors find that enforcing academic integrity produces modest but statistically significant improvements in subsequent professional conduct. The paper provides rare causal evidence on the link between educational misconduct, bureaucratic selection, and the quality of public institutions in a major developing economy.

## 5. Rural-Urban Migration and Market Integration
**Authors:** Dennis Egger, Benjamin Faber, Ming Li, Wei Lin
**Date:** August 2025
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34098 (NBER Working Paper No. 34098)
**Abstract:** This paper studies how reductions in rural-urban migration barriers affect flows of trade and investment between cities and the countryside, using Chinese microdata and the staggered expansion of urban Hukou (residence-permit) eligibility as a natural policy experiment. Increases in a rural county's share of workers eligible for urban registration raise rural-urban exports, imports, capital inflows and outflows, and the number of active buyer-seller matches between rural and urban firms. The authors develop a spatial equilibrium model in which migrants endogenously reduce matching frictions between rural and urban markets. Quantifying the model, they find that a ten-percent increase in a rural county's migration market access raises its trade market access by 1.5 percent and its investment market access by 2 percent on average. China's Hukou reforms generated larger knock-on effects on market integration for urban destinations than for rural origins, creating a reinforcing dynamic that further encourages rural-to-urban migration. The results show that labor mobility is a first-order determinant of goods and capital market integration and that migration policy has aggregate efficiency consequences well beyond the direct reallocation of workers.
