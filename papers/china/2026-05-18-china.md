# China Economics Papers — 2026-05-18

## 1. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy
**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893 (NBER Working Paper No. 34893)
**Abstract:** We study whether industrial policies that promote clean energy can foster economic growth and welfare while also reducing emissions. We analyze Chinese solar subsidies whose implementation by city-regions went alongside massive expansion of the sector and a dramatic fall in global solar prices, using newly constructed datasets on policies, patents, and production from 2004–2020. Our synthetic-difference-in-differences analysis reveals that production and innovation subsidies were more effective than demand-side (installation) subsidies in generating large and persistent increases in local innovation, net entry, production, and exports. Demand policies did, however, reduce local pollution. We build a structural spatial model that incorporates innovation and firm productivity heterogeneity and find that local effects explain 40–50% of the aggregate changes in solar innovation, prices, and revenues. Counterfactual analysis shows that social benefits to Chinese citizens exceed subsidy costs by 65% (and double this when environmental benefits are included), and that innovation subsidies are the most cost-effective instrument.

## 2. A Few Bad Apples? Academic Dishonesty, Political Selection, and Institutional Performance in China
**Authors:** Zhuang Liu, Wenwei Peng, Shaoda Wang
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34747 (NBER Working Paper No. 34747)
**Abstract:** We apply plagiarism-detection algorithms to approximately 500,000 publicly available graduate dissertations in China and link detected misconduct to administrative career records. We document four main findings. First, plagiarism is pervasive and predicts adverse political selection: plagiarists are significantly more likely to enter and advance in the public sector. Second, plagiarists perform worse when holding power: exploiting quasi-random case assignments in the judiciary, we find that judges with plagiarism histories issue more preferential rulings and attract a greater number of appeals. Third, dishonesty spills over across judges and between judges and lawyers. Fourth, enforcing academic integrity—through the adoption of detection tools by graduate schools—leads to modest but measurable improvements in future professional conduct. These negative patterns disappear in cases subject to public oversight mechanisms such as livestreamed court proceedings, suggesting that the behavior reflects conscious strategic choice rather than lower competence.

## 3. Currency Pegs, Trade Imbalances and Unemployment: A Reevaluation of the China Shock
**Authors:** Bumsoo Kim, Marc De la Barrea, Masao Fukui
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34823 (NBER Working Paper No. 34823)
**Abstract:** We develop a dynamic quantitative model of trade and labor adjustment, incorporating nominal wage rigidity and consumption–saving decisions, to study how China's currency peg interacted with its rapid productivity growth in shaping the U.S. economy from 1995 to 2010. We show that the peg temporarily boosted China's export growth by preventing an appreciation of the renminbi, thereby amplifying the U.S. labor-market consequences of the China shock. The temporary export surge also prompted increased Chinese saving, worsening the U.S. current account imbalance. We find that the peg accounts for roughly one-third of the total China shock effect on U.S. manufacturing employment. Despite these disruptive labor-market effects, the overall welfare impact of the China shock remains positive in our model, because productivity gains dominate distributional losses.

## 4. Culture, Risk-Taking, and Public Leadership: Evidence from Chinese Villages
**Authors:** Justin Jihao Hong
**Date:** 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304387826000762 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 181, DOI: 10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103762)
**Abstract:** This paper studies how traditional culture shapes public leadership and local governance in China. Exploiting the 12-year Chinese zodiac cycle—which creates exogenous within-individual variation in culturally perceived risk appetite based on birth year—I identify the causal effect of risk-taking on village heads' governance behavior. I find that village heads in their risk-avoidant zodiac years adhere more closely to governance processes and improve responsiveness to constituents, leading to increased public-good spending and a comparable decline in administrative spending prone to misuse. However, risk-averse leaders are less likely to promote policy innovation. The results suggest that risk avoidance can improve leader performance when incentives for accountability are limited, while revealing a potential trade-off between accountability and public entrepreneurship.

## 5. The Ins & Outs of Chinese Monetary Policy Transmission
**Authors:** Silvia Miranda-Agrippino, Tsvetelina Nenova, Hélène Rey
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34626 (NBER Working Paper No. 34626)
**Abstract:** We construct a novel high-frequency indicator of the People's Bank of China's monetary policy stance and estimate a policy rule that accounts for its dual mandates in inflation and exchange rates. Using a structural VAR identified with external instruments, we show that domestic transmission of PBoC monetary policy follows textbook patterns—tightening reduces output and prices—with two notable exceptions driven by the active management of the renminbi and China's partially closed financial account: exchange rate responses differ from those predicted by uncovered interest parity, and capital flows respond asymmetrically to policy shocks. Internationally, PBoC monetary shocks generate sizable spillovers to global commodity prices and trade volumes, with considerable pass-through to U.S. import prices, underscoring China's growing influence on the global monetary environment.
