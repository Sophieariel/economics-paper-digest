# China Economics Papers — 2026-05-19

## 1. The Geography of Market Power: Rethinking Consolidation in China's Steel Industry
**Authors:** Loren Brandt, Feitao Jiang, Yao Luo, Yingjun Su
**Date:** May 6, 2026
**Source:** https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/public/workingPapers/tecipa-820.pdf (University of Toronto Department of Economics Working Paper No. 820; VoxChina: https://voxchina.org/show-3-456.html)
**Abstract:** This study examines how mergers and consolidation in China's steel industry affect market power and consumer welfare, with a focus on geographic factors often overlooked in policy analysis. Because steel is heavy and costly to transport, markets are largely regional, meaning national concentration ratios can be misleading about actual market power distribution. Using firm-level data on China's steel producers and a structural model of regional competition, the authors find that regionally-focused mergers led by efficient producers yield the largest welfare gains (24% improvement in consumer welfare), while consolidation driven purely by size tends to raise prices and harm consumers. The actual 2018–2024 merger wave, dominated by state-owned enterprises such as Baowu Steel, captured less than one-quarter of potential efficiency gains, suggesting that policy prioritized producer interests and national-champion objectives over consumer benefits. The findings have implications for evaluating China's broader industrial consolidation drive across heavy industries.

## 2. Sex and the City: Demographics, Spatial Sorting, and the Marriage Market
**Authors:** Min Fang, Zibin Huang, Yushi Wang, Yu Alan Yang
**Date:** April 28, 2026
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5327630 (SSRN Working Paper No. 5327630; VoxChina: https://voxchina.org/show-3-451.html)
**Abstract:** This research examines China's declining marriage rate through the lens of spatial economics and demographic change. The authors document how highly educated women increasingly concentrate in wealthy, service-sector cities, while less educated men remain in poorer regions. Persistent cultural expectations around hypergamy—where women marry men of equal or higher socioeconomic status—create local marriage market mismatches across space. Combining prefecture-level panel data with a spatial equilibrium model incorporating multi-sector production, migration, and local marriage markets, the study finds that gender differences in education and spatial sorting account for roughly 30% of rising female singlehood nationwide. Financial interventions such as marriage subsidies prove ineffective because they do not address the underlying geographic mismatch in available partners. Without policy intervention, projections suggest the national female singles rate could rise by nearly 60% by 2030, with college-educated women in major cities facing the largest increases.

## 3. Contract Design and Insurance Demand
**Authors:** Jing Cai
**Date:** February 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34797); highlighted on VoxChina April 22, 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34797 (NBER Working Paper No. 34797)
**Abstract:** This paper examines how insurance contract structure influences take-up among agricultural households in China. Through a field experiment with rice farmers in rural China, the study compares offering a single standardized insurance contract against providing a menu of options with varying premiums and coverage levels. The key finding is that expanding the contract menu substantially increases overall insurance adoption—from approximately 30% to 50%—primarily through higher take-up of the basic, lowest-cost contract. Counterintuitively, offering additional higher-priced contracts increases demand for the cheapest option by making it more attractive through relative price comparisons. The research identifies that menu expansion effects operate through comparison effects rather than information provision, demonstrating that supply-side contract design choices are an effective and cost-effective lever for broadening financial inclusion in developing countries, complementing demand-side interventions such as education and subsidies.

## 4. Strategic Emissions in the Rain: Evasion of Water Pollution Regulations in China
**Authors:** Runhao Zhao, Ye Yuan
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 181, Article 103731; VoxChina: April 15, 2026)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103731 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 181)
**Abstract:** This paper documents how Chinese industrial firms strategically discharge untreated wastewater during heavy rainfall to exploit natural dilution effects and evade threshold-based water quality regulations. Rather than releasing effluent when monitoring is absent, firms employ a "store-then-discharge" strategy: they stockpile polluted wastewater and release it when rain raises river flows enough to dilute concentrations below regulatory thresholds. Using monitoring data from 147 river stations across China during 2014–2019, the authors estimate that approximately 75,000 tons of annual chemical oxygen demand (COD) emissions occur on rain days—a figure that offsets 43% of China's stated industrial COD reduction targets. The findings reveal a significant and systematic gap between apparent regulatory compliance and actual environmental outcomes, with important implications for how threshold-based environmental monitoring systems should be redesigned to close this strategic evasion window.

## 5. Can Foreign Direct Investment Boost Social Mobility? Evidence from China
**Authors:** Jianpeng Deng, Zibin Huang, Qing Shi, Xin Zhao
**Date:** April 1, 2026
**Source:** https://dx.doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5331276 (SSRN Working Paper No. 5331276; VoxChina: https://voxchina.org/show-3-452.html)
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether foreign direct investment promotes intergenerational occupational mobility, leveraging China's phased FDI liberalization following WTO accession as a natural experiment. Using linked data from National Population Census waves in 2000, 2005, and 2010, the Annual Survey of Industrial Firms, and the Catalogue for Guidance of Foreign Investment Industries, the study finds that greater local exposure to FDI significantly increases upward occupational mobility, particularly for individuals from disadvantaged family backgrounds. A one standard deviation increase in a prefecture's FDI exposure raises upward occupational mobility by approximately 2.7% of a standard deviation. The analysis identifies three complementary mechanisms: FDI raises local demand for high-skilled labor, induces greater household educational investment in the next generation, and accelerates structural transformation away from agriculture toward modern manufacturing and services. The results suggest that trade and investment openness can be an important—and underappreciated—driver of intergenerational economic mobility in developing countries.
