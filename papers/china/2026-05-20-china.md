# China Economics Papers — 2026-05-20

## 1. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy
**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893 (NBER Working Paper No. 34893)
**Abstract:** We examine whether industrial policies promoting clean energy can boost growth and welfare while also reducing emissions. We study the Chinese solar sector, the largest in the world, using new data on production, patents, prices, and firm entry constructed from multiple administrative and survey sources. Cities were exposed to different national subsidy programs across time, allowing us to use synthetic-difference-in-differences designs to identify causal effects. We find that production and innovation subsidies were more effective than demand-side (installation) subsidies in generating large and persistent increases in local innovation, net entry, production and exports. A structural spatial model with endogenous innovation allows us to recover aggregate consequences from these local estimates and show that local effects remain large at the macro level, explaining 40%-50% of the aggregate changes in solar innovation, prices and revenues. Social benefits to Chinese citizens exceed subsidy costs by 65% (and double this when environmental benefits are included), with innovation subsidies being the most cost-effective. While all subsidy types increased welfare, the gains were primarily driven by production and innovation subsidies through their effects on technology diffusion and firm entry rather than through direct displacement of fossil-fuel-based power generation.

## 2. Currency Pegs, Trade Imbalances and Unemployment: A Reevaluation of the China Shock
**Authors:** Bumsoo Kim, Marc De la Barrea, Masao Fukui
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34823 (NBER Working Paper No. 34823)
**Abstract:** We develop a dynamic quantitative model of trade and labor adjustment to study how China's currency peg interacted with its rapid growth in shaping the US economy. We show that the peg temporarily accelerated Chinese export growth by preventing currency appreciation, amplifying labor market disruptions in the US. China's temporary export acceleration also elevated savings, contributing to a larger US trade deficit. Calibrating the model to match key features of trade and labor market data, we find that the currency peg significantly amplified the adverse effects of the China shock on US manufacturing employment, trade balances, and unemployment dynamics. Despite these distributional consequences, the overall welfare impact of the China shock remains significant and positive, with the peg increasing the welfare gains from trade by facilitating a faster transition to a new long-run equilibrium.

## 3. A Few Bad Apples? Academic Dishonesty, Political Selection, and Institutional Performance in China
**Authors:** Zhuang Liu, Wenwei Peng, Shaoda Wang
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34747 (NBER Working Paper No. 34747)
**Abstract:** We deploy plagiarism-detection algorithms on approximately 500,000 Chinese graduate dissertations to study the consequences of academic dishonesty on political selection and institutional performance. We find that plagiarism is pervasive and predicts adverse political selection: plagiarists are more likely to enter and advance in the public sector. When plagiarists hold judicial positions, they issue more preferential rulings and attract a greater number of appeals, though livestreaming trials partially mitigates these effects. We further document spillover consequences affecting co-workers—honest judges matched with plagiarist colleagues exhibit changes in ruling behavior—and downstream effects on lawyers and litigants. Finally, we show that enforcing academic integrity standards produces modest improvements in future professional behavior among those who barely avoid being flagged as plagiarists. Our findings suggest that academic misconduct can serve as an early signal of the type of behavior that undermines institutional quality, and that strengthening academic integrity has downstream effects on the performance of public institutions.

## 4. The Ins & Outs of Chinese Monetary Policy Transmission
**Authors:** Silvia Miranda-Agrippino, Tsvetelina Nenova, Hélène Rey
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34626 (NBER Working Paper No. 34626)
**Abstract:** We construct a novel measure of the monetary policy stance of the People's Bank of China (PBoC) that captures its dual mandate of domestic price stability and exchange rate management, and reflects changes in its operational framework over time. We identify monetary policy shocks and trace their domestic and international transmission using a battery of local projection and VAR models estimated on high-frequency and lower-frequency data. Domestically, monetary policy transmission is consistent with standard theory, albeit modified by the management of the renminbi and capital account dynamics. Internationally, Chinese monetary policy spillovers are significant, affecting commodity sectors, manufacturing, and trade. Pass-through to foreign—and especially US—price levels is substantial. Financial spillovers are secondary and emerge mainly as consequences of the trade-related transmission.

## 5. Rural-Urban Migration and Market Integration
**Authors:** Dennis Egger, Benjamin Faber, Ming Li, Wei Lin
**Date:** August 2025
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34098 (NBER Working Paper No. 34098)
**Abstract:** This paper studies how reducing barriers to rural-urban migration affects spatial market integration. We use microdata and a natural policy experiment that generated plausibly exogenous variation in the eligibility of rural workers to obtain urban residence registration (Hukou) in Chinese cities. We find that higher migration eligibility expands rural-urban trade and capital flows. A spatial equilibrium model recovers the implied structural elasticities and allows us to quantify the effects of migration on market access. A 10% increase in a rural county's migration market access raises its trade market access by 1.5% and its investment market access by 2%. Knock-on effects on market integration were on average larger among the urban destinations compared to the rural origins, suggesting that migration reforms have reinforced urbanization incentives. Our results imply that migration barriers are a quantitatively important source of misallocation in developing economies, and that migration liberalization can generate broad market integration gains beyond labor markets.
