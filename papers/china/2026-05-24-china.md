# China Economics Papers — 2026-05-24

> **Note:** Search of NBER, SSRN, and top journals found no qualifying new China empirical papers posted in the last 30 days (April 24–May 24, 2026) that have not already appeared in prior digests. The five papers below are the most recent empirical China papers from the NBER working paper series not yet featured; they were issued January–February 2026.

## 1. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy
**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893 (NBER Working Paper No. 34893)
**Abstract:** We examine whether industrial policies for clean energy can simultaneously boost growth and welfare while reducing emissions. Using new city-region and firm panel data for China over 2004–2020, we analyze solar subsidies implemented across localities with a synthetic-difference-in-differences strategy. Production and innovation subsidies were more effective than demand-side (installation) subsidies in generating large and persistent increases in local innovation, net entry, production, and exports. We build a structural spatial model with endogenous innovation and find that local effects account for 40–50% of aggregate solar-sector changes. Social benefits to Chinese citizens exceed subsidy costs by 65% (and double this when environmental benefits are included), with innovation-focused subsidies proving the most cost-effective policy instrument.

## 2. Currency Pegs, Trade Imbalances and Unemployment: A Reevaluation of the China Shock
**Authors:** Bumsoo Kim, Marc De la Barrea, Masao Fukui
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34823 (NBER Working Paper No. 34823)
**Abstract:** We develop a dynamic quantitative model of trade and labor adjustment to study how China's currency peg interacted with its rapid growth in shaping the U.S. economy. We show that the peg temporarily boosts China's export growth by preventing an appreciation of the Chinese currency, thereby amplifying the U.S. labor-market consequences of the China shock. The peg also contributes to a larger U.S. trade deficit by raising Chinese savings. Using our empirically calibrated model, we find the exchange-rate policy played a quantitatively important role in U.S. manufacturing decline, the widening trade deficit, and unemployment dynamics during the adjustment period. Notably, despite these labor-market dislocations, the overall welfare impact of the China shock remains significant and positive.

## 3. A Few Bad Apples? Academic Dishonesty, Political Selection, and Institutional Performance in China
**Authors:** Zhuang Liu, Wenwei Peng, Shaoda Wang
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34747 (NBER Working Paper No. 34747)
**Abstract:** We apply plagiarism-detection algorithms to approximately 500,000 Chinese graduate dissertations to study hidden academic misconduct and its broader societal consequences. We document that plagiarism is pervasive and predicts adverse political selection: plagiarists disproportionately enter public-sector roles and advance politically. Examining judicial performance, we find that plagiarizing judges issue more preferential rulings and attract a greater number of appeals—though trial livestreaming partially mitigates these effects. We further document negative spillovers onto colleague judges and legal professionals and show that enforcing academic integrity, when detection tools are adopted, leads to modest improvements in future professional conduct. Our findings highlight how undetected dishonesty in educational gatekeeping can compound into systemic institutional dysfunction.

## 4. The Ins & Outs of Chinese Monetary Policy Transmission
**Authors:** Silvia Miranda-Agrippino, Tsvetelina Nenova, Hélène Rey
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34626 (NBER Working Paper No. 34626)
**Abstract:** We develop a novel indicator of People's Bank of China monetary policy stance and construct a policy rule that reflects the dual nature of its price stability mandate—encompassing domestic inflation and the exchange rate. Using this indicator, we document two key findings. First, domestic transmission mechanisms follow conventional economic patterns, though with notable variations stemming from renminbi management and financial account controls. Second, international spillovers are substantial: Chinese monetary policy transmits meaningfully to commodity markets, global production chains, and trade flows, with measurable pass-through to U.S. prices. Financial spillovers rank as secondary effects, largely flowing from trade-related impacts. Our results underscore China's systemic importance for global monetary conditions.

## 5. Rural-Urban Migration and Market Integration
**Authors:** Dennis Egger, Benjamin Faber, Ming Li, Wei Lin
**Date:** August 2025
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34098 (NBER Working Paper No. 34098)
**Abstract:** We study how reductions in rural-urban migration barriers affect trade and investment integration between cities and rural areas in China. Using microdata on firm-to-firm transactions and a natural policy experiment that expanded worker eligibility for urban residence registration (Hukou), we find that increased migration market access boosts rural-urban exports, imports, capital inflows, and outflows—both in terms of bilateral transaction values and the number of unique buyer-seller matches. We embed these findings in a spatial equilibrium model and estimate that a 10% increase in a rural county's migration market access produces a 1.5% increase in trade market access and a 2% increase in investment market access. Our findings suggest that China's recent Hukou reforms generated stronger knock-on effects for urban destinations than rural origins, reinforcing the structural incentives driving rural-to-urban migration.
