# China Economics Papers — 2026-05-25

## 1. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy
**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893 (NBER Working Paper No. 34893)
**Abstract:** We study whether clean energy industrial policies can generate economic growth and welfare improvements while reducing carbon emissions. Our empirical setting is China's solar subsidies, which were implemented by city-regions from 2004–2020 alongside a massive expansion of the solar sector and a dramatic fall in global solar prices. Using synthetic-difference-in-differences, we find that production and innovation subsidies were more effective than demand-side (installation) subsidies in generating large and persistent increases in local innovation, net entry, production, and exports. A structural model reveals that local policy effects scale substantially to the macro level, explaining around 40–50% of aggregate solar sector changes. The local industrial policies explain about half of the global price decline since 2004—a decline of over 90%—which has become a major factor driving the diffusion of solar energy worldwide. Each $1 of solar subsidy generated approximately $1.65 in welfare benefits for Chinese citizens through lower energy prices and export growth, with social benefits exceeding subsidy costs by 65% (and more than doubling when environmental benefits are included). Our findings suggest that targeted industrial policies supporting emerging high-tech sectors can simultaneously boost growth and help fight climate change.

## 2. A Few Bad Apples? Academic Dishonesty, Political Selection, and Institutional Performance in China
**Authors:** Zhuang Liu, Wenwei Peng, Shaoda Wang
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34747 (NBER Working Paper No. 34747)
**Abstract:** Honesty is perceived as fundamental to societal functioning, yet much dishonesty remains hidden and difficult to measure. We develop novel measures of hidden misconduct using plagiarism-detection algorithms applied to approximately 500,000 Chinese graduate dissertations. We document three main findings. First, individuals with plagiarism histories are more likely to enter and advance within the public sector, consistent with an adverse selection mechanism. Second, in the judiciary, judges with plagiarism records issue more preferential rulings and attract a greater number of appeals; these effects are attenuated by the introduction of livestreaming court proceedings, which increases transparency and public accountability. Third, dishonest judges exert negative peer effects on co-workers, suggesting institutional spillovers from individual misconduct. Finally, we show that enforcing academic integrity through sequentially rolled-out detection tools leads to modest improvements in future professional conduct. Together, our results highlight how hidden academic misconduct shapes political selection and institutional performance, while also documenting the potential—and limits—of enforcement as a corrective mechanism.

## 3. The Ins & Outs of Chinese Monetary Policy Transmission
**Authors:** Silvia Miranda-Agrippino, Tsvetelina Nenova, Hélène Rey
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34626 (NBER Working Paper No. 34626)
**Abstract:** We construct a novel indicator of the People's Bank of China's monetary policy stance and estimate a policy rule that accounts for its dual mandate—targeting domestic inflation and exchange rate stability—as well as changes in operational frameworks over time. We find that domestic transmission of Chinese monetary policy shocks follows textbook patterns, with notable exceptions arising from the active management of the renminbi and the financial account. Internationally, spillovers are substantial: Chinese monetary policy affects commodity markets, global production and trade, and generates considerable pass-through to U.S. prices. Financial spillovers are comparatively secondary and largely arise as a byproduct of the trade channel rather than through direct financial linkages. Our results highlight the growing importance of Chinese monetary policy for global economic conditions.

## 4. Rural-Urban Migration and Market Integration
**Authors:** Dennis Egger, Benjamin Faber, Ming Li, Wei Lin
**Date:** August 2025
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34098 (NBER Working Paper No. 34098)
**Abstract:** We combine microdata from China with a natural policy experiment to investigate how reductions in rural-urban migration barriers affect trade and investment flows. Using staggered changes in eligibility for urban residence registration (Hukou) that increased worker mobility across Chinese counties, we find that greater migration market access raises rural-urban exports, imports, capital inflows, and capital outflows—both in terms of bilateral transaction values and the number of unique buyer-seller matches. A spatial equilibrium model reveals that a 10% increase in a rural county's migration market access leads on average to a 1.5% increase in the county's trade market access and a 2% increase in investment market access. The integration effects are notably stronger in urban destinations than in rural origins, reinforcing incentives for rural-to-urban migration within China's ongoing Hukou reform process and highlighting the complementarity between factor market liberalization and product and capital market integration.

## 5. Industrial Policy Implementation: Empirical Evidence from China's Shipbuilding Industry
**Authors:** Panle Jia Barwick, Myrto Kalouptsidi, Nahim Bin Zahur
**Date:** February 2025 (advance publication)
**Source:** https://academic.oup.com/restud/advance-article-abstract/doi/10.1093/restud/rdaf011/8099437 (The Review of Economic Studies)
**Abstract:** We empirically assess China's industrial policy in the shipbuilding sector using firm-level data from 1998–2014 and a dynamic model of firm entry, exit, investment, and production. We find that the policy boosted China's domestic investment, entry, and international market share dramatically, but delivered low returns and led to fragmentation, idle capacity, and depressed world ship prices. We evaluate the long-term performance of different policy instruments—production subsidies, investment subsidies, entry subsidies, and consolidation policies—and find that production and investment subsidies can be justified by market-share rationales, while entry subsidies are wasteful. Counter-cyclical policies, firm targeting, and shorter intervention horizons can substantially reduce distortions. We also explore alternative justifications, finding support for nonclassical considerations such as reducing freight rates to boost Chinese trade. Our results underscore how firm heterogeneity, economic cycles, and cost structures shape the optimal design of industrial policy.
