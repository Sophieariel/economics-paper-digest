## 1. China's Global Ownership

**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, and Hong Ru
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This paper studies the global footprint and real effects of Chinese overseas corporate ownership by assembling a comprehensive micro-level dataset of 161,773 firms across 159 countries between 2012 and 2021. By tracing capital through offshore structures to its ultimate origin, the authors find that Chinese-controlled foreign assets expanded at 20% annually, reaching approximately $2.1 trillion—or roughly 3% of global corporate assets—by 2021. Chinese investors, particularly state-owned enterprises, strategically target R&D-intensive and supply-chain-linked firms. Following acquisition, target firms increase capital stock and R&D expenditures, yet these inputs fail to generate higher patent output and are accompanied by a significant decline in profitability. Chinese parent companies, however, experience accelerated patent growth following developed-economy acquisitions, documenting an "innovation spillback" mechanism whereby Chinese firms internalize technological capacity domestically, accepting weaker short-term performance at foreign subsidiaries as a trade-off.

## 2. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes

**Authors:** Chang Ma and Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056
**Abstract:** This paper examines China's persistent current account surplus and its underlying structural causes. The authors document that while trade and industrial policies create sector-specific imbalances, they have limited impact on the overall economy-wide surplus. Instead, structural factors—particularly an imbalanced sex ratio and unequal financing access between state-owned and non-state enterprises—are identified as the primary drivers of the surplus. The paper shows that macroeconomic stimulus can temporarily reduce the surplus by boosting imports, but that addressing the structural issues is essential for long-term resolution. The analysis draws on firm-level and macro data for China and employs a range of empirical strategies to identify causal relationships between institutional features and external imbalances.

## 3. From Free Rider to Innovator: The Rise of China's Drug Development

**Authors:** Panle Jia Barwick, Hongyuan Xia, and Tianli Xia
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34977
**Abstract:** This paper examines how China transformed from a pharmaceutical "free rider"—relying on generic copies of foreign drugs—to an emerging global innovator over the past decade, surpassing the United States in annual registered clinical trial volume by 2020. The authors compile a comprehensive database tracking scientific publications, clinical trials, drug development milestones, and sales data across China, the U.S., and Europe, alongside detailed government policy records. Using the National Reimbursement Drug List (NRDL) reform as a quasi-natural experiment, the study finds that the reform dramatically expanded the effective market size for innovative drugs, resulting in an 86% increase in drug trials concentrated in reform-exposed disease categories and domestic firms. The NRDL reform accounts for 43% of the growth in oncology trial activity, substantially exceeding contributions from knowledge accumulation and talent flows. The authors conclude that dynamic gains from induced innovation exceed the reform's static gains in consumer access to innovative drugs by threefold, highlighting the importance of considering long-term innovation incentives alongside immediate affordability when evaluating pharmaceutical industrial policy.

## 4. When Do Financial Frictions Matter for Misallocation?

**Authors:** Yan Bai, Dan Lu, Xu Tian, and Yajie Wang
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34930
**Abstract:** This paper examines when and how financial frictions generate capital misallocation using a model calibrated with firm-level borrowing costs and capital productivity metrics from Chinese manufacturing data. The authors document significant variation in capital returns across firms in China, with a notable positive correlation between productivity and borrowing costs—a pattern not observed in U.S. firm data—suggesting that more productive Chinese firms face disproportionately higher financing costs, consistent with state-ownership advantages in credit access. The paper constructs a model incorporating both variable firm-specific borrowing costs and additional capital distortions. The key finding is that removing financial frictions alone yields modest productivity gains of under 2%, but eliminating them alongside other distortions produces substantial improvements of approximately 25 percentage points. The research demonstrates that financial frictions can be highly distortionary when other sources of misallocation are simultaneously present, with important implications for understanding China's aggregate productivity gap.

## 5. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy

**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, and Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether clean energy subsidies can simultaneously boost economic growth and welfare while reducing carbon emissions, using China's solar energy industrial policies as a large-scale natural experiment. The authors analyze Chinese solar policies implemented across city-regions from 2004 to 2020, exploiting variation in the timing and targeting of production subsidies, innovation subsidies, and demand-side (installation) subsidies. They find that production and innovation subsidies were substantially more effective than demand-side subsidies in generating large and persistent increases in local innovation, net firm entry, production, and exports. A structural model reveals that local effects explain 40–50% of aggregate solar sector changes. Social benefits of the policies exceed subsidy costs by 65%, or approximately double when environmental gains from emissions reduction are included. Innovation-focused subsidies proved the most cost-effective overall. The results provide rare causal evidence on the real effects of industrial policy in clean energy, and inform debates about the role of the Chinese state in driving the global solar cost decline.
