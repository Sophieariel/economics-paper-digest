# China Economics Papers — 2026-05-30

## 1. Earnings Management and Price Informativeness
**Authors:** Zhiguo He, Wenxi Jiang, Wei Xiong
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35178 (NBER Working Paper No. 35178)
**Abstract:** We investigate the relationship between stock prices and future earnings in China's A-share market. In the cross-section, firms with higher market valuations report higher earnings over the subsequent one to five years, suggesting that stock prices contain information about future fundamentals. In the time series, however, we find evidence of earnings reversal: higher stock prices predict weaker earnings growth three to five years ahead, consistent with short-term earnings management induced by stock-price pressures. We further document such earnings management through non-recurring gains and losses (NRGLs) and use the 2020 delisting rule reform as a natural experiment to examine these mechanisms.

## 2. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy
**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893 (NBER Working Paper No. 34893)
**Abstract:** Can industrial policies promoting clean energy drive growth and welfare improvements while reducing carbon emissions? We study Chinese solar subsidies implemented across city-regions from 2004–2020, constructing new datasets on policies, patents, and output. Using synthetic difference-in-differences, we find that production and innovation subsidies were more effective than demand-side (installation) subsidies in generating large and persistent increases in local innovation, net entry, production, and exports. A quantitative spatial model with endogenous innovation reveals that local effects explain 40–50% of aggregate changes in solar innovation, pricing, and revenues. Social benefits to Chinese citizens exceeded subsidy costs by 65%, doubling when environmental gains are included. Innovation subsidies proved the most cost-effective among the subsidy types studied.

## 3. Currency Pegs, Trade Imbalances and Unemployment: A Reevaluation of the China Shock
**Authors:** Bumsoo Kim, Marc De la Barrea, Masao Fukui
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34823 (NBER Working Paper No. 34823)
**Abstract:** We develop a dynamic quantitative model to study how China's currency peg interacted with its rapid growth in shaping the US economy. The peg temporarily enhanced China's export growth by preventing currency appreciation, thereby intensifying labor-market disruptions in the US. The currency intervention also expanded China's savings, widening the US trade deficit. The peg played a quantitatively important role in the US manufacturing decline, the widening US trade deficit, and unemployment dynamics. Despite these negative labor-market effects, the overall welfare impact of the China shock remained significantly positive.

## 4. A Few Bad Apples? Academic Dishonesty, Political Selection, and Institutional Performance in China
**Authors:** Zhuang Liu, Wenwei Peng, Shaoda Wang
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34747 (NBER Working Paper No. 34747)
**Abstract:** We apply plagiarism-detection algorithms to approximately 500,000 graduate dissertations in China, validate the findings against incentivized honesty measures, and link plagiarism records to administrative data on career outcomes. Plagiarism is pervasive and predicts adverse political selection: plagiarists are more likely to enter and advance in the public sector. Plagiarizing judges issue more preferential rulings and attract a greater number of appeals, with effects partially mitigated by trial livestreaming. We also document spillover effects onto other judges and lawyers, and show that enforcing academic integrity standards produces modest improvements in future professional conduct.

## 5. The Ins & Outs of Chinese Monetary Policy Transmission
**Authors:** Silvia Miranda-Agrippino, Tsvetelina Nenova, Hélène Rey
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34626 (NBER Working Paper No. 34626)
**Abstract:** Using a novel indicator for the People's Bank of China monetary policy stance, we estimate a policy rule that accounts for the dual nature of its price stability mandate—encompassing domestic inflation and the exchange rate—and for the evolution of its operational framework. The "Ins": the domestic transmission follows textbook patterns, with exceptions due to the active management of the renminbi and the financial account. The "Outs": international spillovers are powerful and affect commodity markets, global production and trade. The pass-through to foreign (US) prices is substantial. Financial spillovers are second-order, and mostly derivative from trade spillovers.
