# China Economics Papers — 2026-05-31

## 1. China's Global Ownership
**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, and Hong Ru
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This paper analyzes Chinese overseas corporate ownership across 161,773 firms in 159 countries from 2012–2021. By tracing ownership chains through offshore entities, the authors find that Chinese-controlled foreign assets reached $2.1 trillion by 2021—substantially larger than official FDI statistics suggest. State-owned enterprises strategically targeted R&D-intensive and supply-chain-linked firms. While acquired companies increased capital stock and R&D expenditures following acquisition, these inputs failed to generate higher patent output, and were accompanied by a significant decline in profitability. Notably, Chinese parent companies accelerated patent grants following developed-economy acquisitions, yet peer firms' research declined without changing aggregate industry innovation. The paper characterizes this as "a distinctively state-driven model of global ownership that accepts weaker near-term performance to internalize technological capacity at home."

## 2. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes
**Authors:** Chang Ma and Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056
**Abstract:** This paper examines why China maintains a substantial current account surplus that creates friction with trading partners. The authors find that while sector-specific trade and industrial policies contribute to imbalances in particular industries, they account for relatively modest portions of the overall economy-wide surplus. Instead, the paper identifies deeper structural drivers as more significant factors: specifically, the demographic effects of China's skewed sex ratio and disparities in financing access between state-owned enterprises and private firms. The study concludes that although short-term macroeconomic stimulus measures can increase imports and temporarily reduce surpluses, addressing the underlying imbalances would require fundamental structural reforms targeting these root causes.

## 3. Currency Pegs, Trade Imbalances and Unemployment: A Reevaluation of the China Shock
**Authors:** Bumsoo Kim, Marc De la Barrea, and Masao Fukui
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34823
**Abstract:** This paper develops a dynamic quantitative model of trade and labor adjustment to study how China's currency peg interacted with its rapid growth in shaping the U.S. economy. The analysis reveals that the peg temporarily accelerated China's export growth by preventing currency appreciation, which intensified negative labor-market impacts in the United States. The peg also spurred increased Chinese savings, contributing to a larger U.S. trade deficit. When calibrated against actual trade and labor-market data, the model demonstrates that the currency peg played a "quantitatively important role in the US manufacturing decline, the widening US trade deficit, and unemployment dynamics." The findings highlight exchange-rate dynamics' significance in understanding trade shocks. Notably, the overall welfare effect of the China shock remains substantially positive despite these distributional consequences.

## 4. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy
**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, and Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether industrial policies supporting clean energy can foster growth and welfare while reducing emissions. The authors analyze Chinese solar subsidies implemented across city-regions from 2004–2020, using synthetic-difference-in-differences methodology. They find that "production and innovation subsidies were more effective than demand-side (installation) subsidies in generating large and persistent increases in local innovation, net entry, production and exports." A quantitative spatial model with endogenous innovation reveals that local effects explain 40–50% of aggregate solar sector changes, social benefits exceed subsidy costs by 65% (doubling when environmental gains are included), and innovation-focused subsidies prove most cost-effective overall.

## 5. When Do Financial Frictions Matter for Misallocation?
**Authors:** Yan Bai, Dan Lu, Xu Tian, and Yajie Wang
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34930
**Abstract:** This paper reassesses the role of financial frictions in capital misallocation using a model grounded in firm-level borrowing costs and capital productivity metrics. Using Chinese manufacturing data, the authors document substantial dispersion in the average return to physical capital (ARPK), alongside a strong positive relationship between ARPK and borrowing costs that does not appear in U.S. data. The model shows that eliminating financial frictions raises TFP by 25 percentage points when other distortions exist, but less than 2 percentage points without them. The findings indicate that financial frictions become particularly distortionary when interacting with permanent firm-level misallocations, highlighting the importance of accounting for the broader institutional environment when evaluating the quantitative impact of credit market imperfections.
