## 1. China's Global Ownership
**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, Hong Ru
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This research examines Chinese corporate ownership worldwide using data from 161,773 firms across 159 countries between 2012–2021. The authors reconstructed ownership chains through offshore tax havens to identify ultimate origins, revealing a footprint significantly larger than official FDI figures. Chinese-controlled foreign assets grew at 20% annually, totaling $2.1 trillion or approximately 3% of worldwide corporate assets by 2021. The study finds that Chinese investors, particularly state-owned enterprises, deliberately acquire "R&D-intensive and supply-chain-linked firms." While target companies expanded capital investments and research spending post-acquisition, these increases "fail to generate higher patent output and are accompanied by a significant decline in profitability." A key discovery is an "innovation spillback" effect: Chinese parent companies accelerated patent grants following initial developed-economy acquisitions, though target firm innovation stagnated. Additionally, increased Chinese presence reduced R&D at competing peer firms, though overall industry innovation remained stable. The authors conclude that China pursues a state-driven ownership model accepting short-term performance losses to build technological capacity domestically.

## 2. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes
**Authors:** Chang Ma, Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056
**Abstract:** China maintains a substantial current account surplus that has created friction with trading partners. While sector-specific industrial and trade policies generate imbalances in particular industries, they contribute minimally to the overall economy-wide surplus. The research identifies structural factors as primary drivers—specifically an imbalanced sex ratio and disparities in financing access between state-owned enterprises and non-state firms. Short-term macroeconomic stimulus can increase imports and temporarily reduce the surplus, yet addressing the persistent imbalance requires "reforms aiming at addressing the structural problems."

## 3. From Free Rider to Innovator: The Rise of China's Drug Development
**Authors:** Panle Jia Barwick, Hongyuan Xia, Tianli Xia
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34977
**Abstract:** This research examines how China transformed from a pharmaceutical "free rider" to a global innovation leader over the past decade. The authors document that China's share of global clinical trials grew from under 8% in 2010 to surpassing the U.S. in annual trial volume by 2020. Using a comprehensive database covering publications, clinical trials, drug development milestones, and sales data across China, the U.S., and Europe, they identify the National Reimbursement Drug List (NRDL) reform as the primary driver of this transition. The reform significantly expanded the market for innovative drugs, yielding an 86% increase in trial quantity and improved drug novelty. The analysis reveals that the NRDL reform accounts for 43% of oncology trial growth, while the long-term innovation gains from the reform triple its immediate consumer access benefits.

## 4. When Do Financial Frictions Matter for Misallocation?
**Authors:** Yan Bai, Dan Lu, Xu Tian, Yajie Wang
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34930
**Abstract:** This research reassesses how financial frictions contribute to capital misallocation using a model informed by firm-level borrowing costs and the average revenue product of capital (ARPK). Analysis of Chinese manufacturing data reveals significant ARPK dispersion and a strong positive correlation between ARPK and borrowing costs—patterns not observed in U.S. data. The authors constructed a heterogeneous-firm model incorporating endogenous firm-specific borrowing costs and additional capital distortions as exogenous wedges. Key findings indicate that "eliminating financial frictions raises total factor productivity (TFP) by 25 percentage points." However, when other capital distortions are absent, removing financial frictions increases TFP by less than 2 percentage points. The research demonstrates that interactions between financial frictions and permanent firm-level distortions create endogenous financial heterogeneity and selection effects, making productive firms face the greatest constraints. The conclusion suggests financial frictions prove considerably more distortionary when combined with other misallocation sources.

## 5. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy
**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893
**Abstract:** This research investigates whether industrial policies supporting clean energy generation advance economic growth and welfare while reducing emissions. The authors examine Chinese solar subsidies implemented across city-regions between 2004–2020, during a period of significant sector expansion and declining global solar prices. Using synthetic-difference-in-differences methodology and new city and firm panel data, they found that "production and innovation subsidies were more effective than demand-side (installation) subsidies in generating large and persistent increases in local innovation, net entry, production and exports." Installation policies did reduce local pollution. The researchers developed a quantitative spatial model with endogenous innovation to assess broader impacts. Key findings include: subsidies account for 40–50% of aggregate solar industry changes; social benefits surpassed subsidy costs by 65% (doubling when environmental gains are included); and innovation-focused subsidies proved most cost-effective overall.
