## 1. Price and Volume Divergence in China's Real Estate Markets: The Role of Local Governments
**Authors:** Jeffery (Jinfan) Chang, Yuheng Wang, Wei Xiong
**Date:** September 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 34303); published in *Review of Financial Studies*, Vol. 39(2), 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34303
**Abstract:** During China's Covid-19 pandemic period (2020–2022), a puzzling divergence emerged in residential land and housing markets: prices surged while transaction volumes collapsed. This paper documents and explains that divergence using comprehensive data from Chinese cities. The authors attribute the phenomenon to active price management by local governments, operating through three channels: supply restrictions on new land auctions, land purchases by Local Government Financing Vehicles (LGFVs) at above-market prices, and administrative caps on new home sales permits. Cities with greater pre-pandemic fiscal dependence on land sales revenue and larger outstanding land-backed debt experienced more pronounced price increases and more severe price-volume gaps. The empirical analysis shows that LGFVs systematically outbid competing private buyers in land auctions, functioning as a price-support mechanism rather than a commercial buyer. While these interventions succeeded in sustaining municipal fiscal revenues and preventing headline price declines, they imposed significant unintended costs: real estate developers, deprived of transaction volume and saddled with illiquid land holdings, faced mounting financial distress. The paper provides causal evidence on how local government fiscal incentives shape real estate market dynamics in China and contributes to understanding the political economy of China's housing sector and the systemic risks created by local government dependence on land finance.

## 2. Equity Financing and Exports: Evidence from IPO Approvals in China
**Authors:** Robin Kaiji Gong, Yao Amber Li, Stephen Teng Sun, Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** February 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34906); conditionally accepted at *Journal of International Economics*
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34906
**Abstract:** This paper provides the first empirical analysis of how access to equity financing shapes firm-level export performance. The identification strategy exploits China's historically stringent IPO approval process, in which firms that satisfied all eligibility requirements still faced uncertain approval at final regulatory review meetings. Comparing firms that received approval against those rejected at the final stage in a difference-in-differences framework, the authors document that IPO approval generates a significant annualized increase of more than 6% in firm exports, with the effect sustained over a six-year post-approval window. Unlike debt financing studied in prior work, equity financing expands primarily the extensive margin of exports: IPO firms enter additional destination markets and product categories rather than simply scaling up existing relationships. The authors identify the operative mechanism as equity financing's unique features—no fixed interest obligations and tolerance for strategic risk-taking—which allow firms to incur the fixed costs of intangible investments and market entry experiments that bank credit constrains. The results highlight the role of capital market development in driving export growth, with implications for understanding China's trade expansion and for evaluating financial market reforms in emerging economies.

## 3. Subsidising the Supply Chain: How China's Industrial Policy Shapes Export Competitiveness
**Authors:** Wenyin Cheng, David Liang, Bo Meng, Hongyong Zhang
**Date:** RIETI Discussion Paper 25-E-119 (2025); featured on VoxEU, March 25, 2026
**Source:** https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/subsidising-supply-chain-how-chinas-industrial-policy-shapes-export-competitiveness
**Abstract:** This paper examines how Chinese government subsidies affect export competitiveness, with particular emphasis on value-chain spillovers that conventional firm-level evaluations miss. The analysis combines three datasets: firm-level production data covering 1998–2013, customs transaction records, and input-output tables that map domestic supply linkages. The authors construct novel measures of both direct subsidies received by a given firm and upstream subsidies flowing to that firm's domestic input suppliers. The empirical results show that a one standard deviation increase in direct subsidies raises export value by approximately 10%, with larger effects on intensive rather than extensive margins. Critically, upstream subsidies to supplier firms generate significant spillover effects for downstream exporters: receiving subsidised inputs lowers effective production costs and improves product quality. The quality-upgrading channel is central—subsidised firms and their downstream customers achieve higher quality-adjusted competitiveness rather than engaging in simple price dumping, with quality-adjusted export prices declining while nominal prices remain broadly stable. The paper's key policy contribution is demonstrating that industrial subsidy evaluations must account for network effects propagating through domestic value chains: direct-recipient effects alone substantially understate the aggregate export impact of China's industrial policy, and the mechanisms—operating through R&D investment and input quality upgrading—differ from the standard marginal-cost-reduction narrative.

## 4. The Impact of Public-Private Partnership Health Plans on Private Insurance
**Authors:** Hui Ding, Xintong Wang, Xian Xu
**Date:** 2026; *Journal of Development Economics*, Vol. 182, Article 103780
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304387826000635
**Abstract:** This paper evaluates the market-level consequences of China's City-Customized Supplemental Medical Insurance (CCSMI), a government-endorsed supplemental health insurance scheme that expanded rapidly from 2020 and enrolled hundreds of millions of individuals at very low premiums. Exploiting the staggered rollout of CCSMI programs across cities as a quasi-natural experiment, and using a novel transaction-level dataset of private health insurance purchases, the authors estimate how CCSMI entry affects private insurance demand. The findings reveal significant crowd-out effects on both the extensive and intensive margins: CCSMI introduction substantially reduces private insurance enrollment and compresses average coverage amounts purchased. Decomposing the coverage decline, approximately two-thirds reflects consumers substituting narrower private plans for more comprehensive ones after the public option fills baseline risk, while the remainder reflects insurer pricing responses to the changed risk pool. Aggregating these effects, the decline in private insurance purchases offsets at least 25% of CCSMI enrollment gains, implying that headline enrollment figures substantially overstate the net expansion of meaningful health coverage. The results speak to a central tension in health insurance design: government-endorsed plans that leverage pooling and low prices can crowd out private markets in ways that reduce product diversity and risk coverage depth even as they raise nominal enrollment rates.

## 5. Trading Suspensions, Liquidity, and Information Acquisition in the Chinese Stock Market
**Authors:** Clemens Sialm, David X. Xu
**Date:** Forthcoming 2026 (working paper); featured on VoxChina, March 18, 2026
**Source:** https://voxchina.org/show-3-449.html
**Abstract:** China's stock exchanges permit listed firms to suspend their shares for extended periods, creating a distinctive institutional environment for studying asset pricing under illiquidity. This paper documents that between 2004 and 2020, Chinese exchanges recorded approximately 16,958 multiday suspensions, with an average of 4.6% of all listed stocks suspended at any given time. The authors study how suspensions affect mutual fund valuations and investor behavior. Because fund NAVs are computed using stale last-traded prices for suspended holdings, suspensions generate predictable mispricing between fund NAV and the market value of underlying assets. Sophisticated investors exploit this mispricing through what the authors term "arbitrage via mutual funds," reallocating capital into (out of) funds whose suspended holdings are likely undervalued (overvalued) on NAV: a 1% NAV mispricing attracts quarterly net flows equal to 3% of fund assets. Crucially, the authors identify a positive externality of this arbitrage channel: the prospect of profitable reallocation incentivizes investors to acquire information about suspended stocks during the suspension window, evidenced by elevated rates of corporate site visits and internet search activity for suspended firms. This information acquisition leads to enhanced price discovery at trading resumption, with post-suspension price movements more accurately reflecting subsequent earnings surprises for stocks held in mutual funds. The paper illuminates how institutional design in Chinese equity markets—specifically the interaction between suspension rules, fund structure, and investor incentives—shapes information production and market efficiency.
