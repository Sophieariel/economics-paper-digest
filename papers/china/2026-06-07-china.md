## 1. Equity Financing and Exports: Evidence from IPO Approvals in China
**Authors:** Robin Kaiji Gong, Yao Amber Li, Stephen Teng Sun, Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** February 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34906); featured on VoxChina June 3, 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34906
**Abstract:** While finance theory distinguishes the roles of equity and debt in supporting firm growth, their differential impacts on international trade remain underexplored. This study provides the first empirical analysis of how access to equity financing affects firm exports. The authors leverage the unique institutional setting in China, where initial public offerings (IPOs) require stringent regulatory approval, ensuring that only qualified firms advance to the final review stage. The empirical strategy compares the export performance of successful IPO applicants with that of "near misses"—applicants rejected at the final review meetings. To sharpen identification, the authors utilize meeting records to exclude rejections citing concerns about future revenue growth or profitability risks, as these may entail unobserved shocks to export performance. A cohort-based difference-in-differences analysis reveals that IPO approval leads to a significant annualized increase of more than 6% in firm exports over the subsequent six years. Distinct from previous findings on debt financing, IPO approval primarily affects the extensive margin, enabling firms to expand into more destination-product markets. Mechanism tests suggest that IPOs enhance exports by financing intangible investments and fostering risk-taking activities.

## 2. The Impact of Public-Private Partnership Health Plans on Private Insurance
**Authors:** Hui Ding, Xintong Wang, Xian Xu
**Date:** 2026; featured on VoxChina May 26, 2026
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103780 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 182, Article 103780)
**Abstract:** This paper examines the market-wide effects of China's city-customized supplemental medical insurance (CCSMI), a government-endorsed public-private partnership insurance product that expanded coverage to over 300 million people by 2023. While CCSMI is widely credited with expanding access to healthcare financing, the authors use the program's staggered rollout across cities and a novel transaction-level dataset of private health insurance purchases to identify crowd-out effects. They find that the introduction of CCSMI led to a 13.7% decline in private insurance purchases and a 13.6% reduction in average coverage amounts among those who retained private insurance. Approximately two-thirds of the decline in premium payments is driven by consumers opting for narrower coverage, with the remainder attributable to insurer pricing adjustments. A back-of-the-envelope calculation suggests the decline in private insurance purchases offsets at least 25% of CCSMI enrollment gains. The authors conclude that headline enrollment growth overstates the true gains in risk protection, and recommend improved coordination between public and private programs as well as better consumer information about product differences to minimize unintended substitution.

## 3. Industrial Subsidies along Domestic Value Chains and Their Impacts on China's Exports
**Authors:** Wenyin Cheng, Tao Liang, Bo Meng, Hongyong Zhang
**Date:** December 2025 (RIETI Discussion Paper 25-E-119); featured on VoxEU/VoxChina May 20, 2026
**Source:** https://www.rieti.go.jp/en/publications/summary/25120013.html
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether and how government subsidies explain China's dominant export position despite substantially lower prices than international competitors. Using firm-level subsidy data matched to inter-provincial input-output tables, the authors measure both direct subsidies received by exporting firms and indirect subsidies transmitted through upstream domestic suppliers. Direct subsidies significantly increase both the probability of exporting (extensive margin) and export volumes (intensive margin), with stronger effects on the latter. Indirect subsidies from first-tier upstream industries also generate meaningful export spillovers, though the effects of further-removed upstream subsidies are smaller and vary by firm ownership type. A key finding that challenges simple price-dumping narratives is that subsidies correlate with higher rather than lower export prices, driven by quality upgrading. The underlying mechanisms show that export growth and quality improvements are achieved through (i) direct subsidies raising R&D investment and imported input use, and (ii) indirect subsidies improving the quality of domestic intermediate inputs. The authors conclude that industrial policy effects cannot be adequately assessed at the firm level alone: subsidies propagate along domestic value chains in ways that amplify their aggregate impact on export competitiveness.

## 4. The Geography of Market Power: Evidence from the Chinese Steel Industry
**Authors:** Loren Brandt, Feitao Jiang, Yao Luo, Yingjun Su
**Date:** May 6, 2026 (University of Toronto Department of Economics Working Paper No. 820)
**Source:** https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/public/workingPapers/tecipa-820.pdf
**Abstract:** This paper examines consolidation in China's steel sector through a spatial economics lens, exploiting the fact that steel markets are inherently regional due to high transportation costs while production and demand are geographically mismatched—Hebei province produces approximately one quarter of national output while the Yangtze River Delta consumes far more than it generates locally. Using detailed data on inter-regional steel flows, the authors build a structural model incorporating buyer location preferences and firm productivity heterogeneity. They find that regional mergers led by efficient producers deliver substantially larger welfare gains (24% improvement) than nationally-coordinated consolidation centered on incumbent state-owned giants. The actual 2018–2024 merger wave, dominated by state-owned enterprise Baowu Steel, achieved less than one-quarter of potential welfare gains, suggesting that industrial policy prioritized producer interests and political objectives over consumer welfare, in part by expanding the market power of favored incumbents. The paper contributes a general framework for evaluating consolidation policies in industries where geographic frictions determine the scope of competition.

## 5. Rural-Urban Migration and Market Integration
**Authors:** Dennis Egger, Benjamin Faber, Ming Li, Wei Lin
**Date:** August 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 34098); presented at NBER Spring 2026 Chinese Economy Working Group Meeting
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34098
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how reducing barriers to rural-urban migration affects trade and investment flows between cities and rural areas in China. Using a natural policy experiment involving changes to Hukou (urban residence registration) eligibility, the authors identify the causal effect of increased worker migration access on rural-urban economic linkages. They find that greater migration access leads to higher rural-urban exports, imports, and capital flows between origin counties and destination cities. A quantitative spatial equilibrium model is used to interpret these findings and perform counterfactual policy analysis. The model reveals that a 10% increase in a rural county's migration market access leads on average to a 1.5% increase in trade market access and a 2% increase in investment market access for that county. The findings suggest that migration-driven reductions in buyer-seller matching frictions generate significant spillover effects on broader market integration, with urban destinations experiencing larger aggregate benefits than rural origin communities. The paper provides causal evidence that internal migration policy shapes not just labor market outcomes but the extent of goods and capital market integration across China's urban-rural divide.
