## 1. Intergenerational Mobility in Late Qing Dynasty: Evidence from Northeast China
**Authors:** Kristina Butaeva, Steven N. Durlauf, Alexander Shapoval
**Date:** June 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35313)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35313
**Abstract:** This paper examines intergenerational class mobility across generations during China's late Qing era using a remarkable historical dataset from Liaoning province. The authors identify two distinct epochs with qualitatively different mobility dynamics. Before 1850, the social class system exhibited stability with mobility patterns converging toward equilibrium. After 1850, intergenerational class dynamics became unstable and remained so until the dynasty's collapse. The transition between epochs coincides with the Opium Wars and the Taiping Rebellion, illustrating how major political crises reshaped social mobility. Strikingly, while overall mobility increased in the post-1850 period, this aggregate rise masked a concerning compositional shift: upward mobility declined while downward mobility rose, patterns consistent with theories linking social disruption to regressive mobility dynamics. The findings contribute to a growing literature on long-run inequality and the political economy of state capacity by providing rare quantitative evidence on mobility processes in pre-modern China during a period of institutional collapse.

## 2. College Affirmative Action and Students' Outcomes: A Matter of the Dose
**Authors:** Yi Chen, Chao Fu, Hongbin Li, Teng Li, Siyan Tang
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35281)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35281
**Abstract:** This paper examines how the intensity of affirmative action policies affects the outcomes of intended beneficiaries, using China's ethnic minority bonus-point system in college admissions as a laboratory. The bonus-point system awards additional points to ethnic minority applicants on the national college entrance exam (gaokao), with the size of the bonus varying across provinces and over time. Exploiting this variation, the authors find strongly non-monotonic effects of affirmative action intensity on recipient outcomes. Higher affirmative action doses increase recipients' admissions to colleges and elite colleges but lower their academic rankings within their cohort and reduce long-term earnings. Conversely, lower doses produce positive consumption gains of approximately 19%. The analysis reveals non-monotonic effects across expenditure categories and suggests that careful calibration of affirmative action policies is crucial for achieving their intended goals of improving welfare for disadvantaged groups rather than inadvertently harming them through excessive preferential treatment.

## 3. Earnings Management and Price Informativeness
**Authors:** Zhiguo He, Wenxi Jiang, Wei Xiong
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35178)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35178
**Abstract:** This paper investigates the relationship between stock prices and future earnings in China's A-share market, documenting an informational disconnect driven by earnings management. In the cross-section, companies with higher market prices tend to report stronger earnings over the following one to five years, indicating that stock prices do incorporate forward-looking information about fundamentals. However, in time series, elevated stock prices predict diminished earnings growth three to five years later, suggesting that firms engage in short-term earnings manipulation when facing stock-price pressures. The authors identify such manipulation via non-recurring gains and losses—a category of accounting entries that Chinese listed firms can use to manage reported earnings—and use China's 2020 delisting regulation, which introduced new performance-based delisting thresholds, as a natural experiment to test these mechanisms. The regulation significantly changed managerial incentives, allowing the authors to establish that the observed time-series reversal is causally linked to earnings management rather than underlying economic fundamentals. The findings carry implications for the informativeness of prices in emerging markets where accounting quality and regulatory enforcement are uneven.

## 4. China's Global Ownership
**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, Hong Ru
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35106)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This paper assembles a comprehensive micro-level dataset of 161,773 firms across 159 countries from 2012 to 2021, tracing ownership chains through offshore structures to identify the ultimate Chinese ownership origin of foreign-held firms. Chinese-controlled foreign corporate assets grew at 20% annually, reaching $2.1 trillion—roughly 3% of global corporate assets—by 2021. State-owned enterprises strategically targeted R&D-intensive and supply-chain-connected firms for acquisition. While acquisitions boosted capital expenditure and R&D spending at target firms, these investments did not generate measurable increases in patent output and coincided with declines in profitability. Notably, the study documents an "innovation spillback" effect: though target firm innovation stagnated, Chinese parent companies accelerated their own patent grants after acquiring developed-economy firms. Additionally, greater Chinese ownership presence reduced R&D investment among competitor firms, though aggregate industry-level innovation remained broadly stable. The findings characterize China's global ownership model as state-driven, prioritizing long-term domestic technological capacity accumulation over near-term financial performance at acquired entities abroad.

## 5. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes
**Authors:** Chang Ma, Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35056)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056
**Abstract:** This paper examines the origins and persistence of China's substantial current account surplus, one of the most consequential macroeconomic imbalances in the global economy. The authors document the historical patterns of the surplus and evaluate a range of candidate explanations. Industrial and trade policies often lead to sector-level imbalances but play a relatively limited role in driving the economy-wide surplus. Instead, two structural factors emerge as more important quantitatively: an unbalanced sex ratio—which raises household saving rates as families compete for marriage partners—and unequal access to financing between state-owned enterprises and private non-state firms, which generates inefficient capital allocation and suppresses consumption. Short-term macroeconomic stimulus can reduce the surplus by boosting imports, but the authors conclude that any durable resolution requires addressing the underlying structural distortions. The paper contributes to ongoing policy debates about the sources of global imbalances and provides a framework for distinguishing between proximate and fundamental drivers of China's external position.
