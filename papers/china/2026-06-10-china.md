## 1. China's Financial Spillovers to Emerging Markets
**Authors:** Rodolfo G. Campos, Ana-Simona Manu, Luis Molina, Marta Suárez-Varela
**Date:** February 4, 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261560626000239 (Journal of International Money and Finance, 2026); SSRN: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4994133
**Abstract:** This paper quantifies the spillovers of shocks originating in China on the financial markets of emerging economies, distinguishing between monetary policy disturbances and macroeconomic developments. Using a high-frequency identification strategy based on sign, relative magnitude, and narrative restrictions imposed on a Bayesian VAR, the authors identify five structural shocks from China and trace their dynamic effects on equity markets, sovereign and corporate bond spreads, and exchange rates across emerging economies in East Asia, Eastern Europe, and Latin America. Macroeconomic shocks from China generate significant and persistent effects on emerging-economy equity markets, while monetary policy shocks exhibit more modest and short-lived spillovers. The key transmission mechanism runs through the effect of Chinese macroeconomic shocks on international commodity prices: spillovers are strongest for Latin American equity markets (approximately 0.26% same-day increase), reflecting greater commodity export dependence, compared to East Asia and Eastern Europe (approximately 0.15%). Effects extend beyond equities to sovereign and corporate spreads and currency values, with implications for central bank modeling and financial stability assessments. The results document a growing systemic role for China in driving financial conditions across emerging markets.

## 2. High-Speed Rail and Local Agricultural Development
**Authors:** Xiaoguang Chen, Binlei Gong, Zhilong Qin, Xiaoli Wang
**Date:** January 7, 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304387825001981 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 179, DOI: 10.1016/j.jdeveco.2025.103647); VoxChina summary: https://voxchina.org/show-3-440.html
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether China's large-scale high-speed rail (HSR) expansion undermines food security by drawing labor and land away from agriculture. Using staggered HSR station openings as a quasi-natural experiment with county-level and household-level agricultural data, the authors employ difference-in-differences methods to estimate causal effects of HSR connectivity on local agricultural development. While HSR access facilitates the outflow of labor and cropland from agriculture—consistent with structural transformation—total agricultural output remains stable owing to productivity gains: agricultural total factor productivity increases by approximately 4.9–7.8% following HSR connectivity, fully offsetting the decline in traditional inputs. The authors identify multiple reinforcing channels: HSR increases local government fiscal capacity (via higher GDP and tax revenue), which is channeled into agricultural infrastructure investment and rural road construction; it facilitates farmer access to technical training; and it induces new agribusiness firm entry and a shift toward higher-value crops. Household survey data confirm that farmers' revenues and profits increase, strengthening production incentives. The findings indicate that major transport infrastructure investments can coexist with sustained agricultural productivity when embedded in supportive policy environments.

## 3. Pollution Reduction Effects of New Transregional Power Transmission Systems: Evidence from Ultra-High-Voltage Projects of China
**Authors:** Shenggang Ren, Li Zhao, Peng Zhang, Caiquan Bai
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304387825001920 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 179, Article 103641)
**Abstract:** This paper studies the pollution reduction effects of China's ultra-high-voltage (UHV) transregional power transmission system, a flagship energy infrastructure investment under China's industrial policy. Using a comprehensive firm-level panel dataset covering pollution emissions, energy consumption, and economic information for 43,458 firms from 2007 to 2014—merged from the China Environment Statistical Database (CESD) and the China National Tax Survey Database (CNTSD)—the authors employ a staggered difference-in-differences design that compares firms in provinces receiving UHV transmission lines to those in provinces without such access. They find that UHV line operation reduces firms' total SO2 emissions by 16.8% on average and emission intensity by 17.1%, primarily by displacing coal-based local power generation with cleaner long-distance electricity in receiving provinces. The aggregate reduction amounts to approximately 1.46 million tons of SO2 annually, generating annual environmental benefits of roughly 29.2 billion CNY. Incorporating these environmental gains shortens the UHV investment payback period from 28 years to approximately 5 years at a 2.5% discount rate. The paper provides novel causal evidence on the environmental returns to large-scale clean energy infrastructure in a developing economy context.

## 4. Feeling the Same Heat? Urban–Rural Inequalities in Adapting to Extreme Temperatures via Electricity Consumption
**Authors:** Caiquan Bai, Jing Cao, Ming Huang, Chen Xi, Peng Zhang
**Date:** March 1, 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304387825002433 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 180, DOI: 10.1016/j.jdeveco.2025.103692)
**Abstract:** This paper examines urban–rural inequalities in Chinese households' adaptive responses to extreme temperatures, using electricity consumption as the primary margin of adjustment. Using household-level electricity consumption data matched with granular temperature records, the authors apply the Theil index to decompose within- and between-group inequality across urban, rural, and regional dimensions. They document substantial urban–rural gaps in temperature responsiveness: higher-income urban households are significantly better able to increase air conditioning and heating use during extreme temperature events, while rural and lower-income households face binding demand-side constraints rooted in income limitations and inferior appliance ownership. Although better urban infrastructure partially explains the adaptive gap, demand-side income constraints account for the dominant share of the urban–rural disparity. The authors show that standard aggregate welfare cost estimates for extreme weather events substantially understate the burden falling on disadvantaged households. The findings highlight a distributional challenge in climate adaptation—the households least able to adjust are also the most exposed—with policy implications for electricity tariff design and social protection programs targeting rural populations in China.

## 5. Higher Education Expansion and the Rural–Urban Gap in Secondary Schooling and Labor Outcomes: Evidence from China's 1999 Reform
**Authors:** Loren Brandt, Yifan Zhang
**Date:** January 17, 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304387826000040 (Journal of Development Economics, 2026)
**Abstract:** This paper exploits China's 1999 higher education expansion—which raised annual university enrollment from roughly one million to over 6.3 million within a decade—to estimate causal effects of tertiary expansion on upstream secondary schooling decisions and downstream labor market outcomes. Using cohort-level variation in exposure to the expansion across provinces, the authors find that the reform increased academic-track (rather than vocational) take-up among rural secondary students, narrowing the rural–urban gap in academic high school completion even as rural–urban gaps in college completion did not close commensurately. The authors identify a strong indirect channel: national tertiary expansion increased the perceived attainability of the academic track for rural cohorts, inducing upward adjustments in secondary school continuation decisions at the academic-versus-vocational margin. While rural students respond robustly to expanded higher education opportunities, the evidence also reveals complementary constraints: returns to the reform are attenuated in counties with weaker rural upper-secondary school capacity and thinner local non-farm labor markets. The results highlight both the promise and the limits of demand-side tertiary expansion as a tool for reducing rural–urban inequality in human capital accumulation and labor market earnings.
