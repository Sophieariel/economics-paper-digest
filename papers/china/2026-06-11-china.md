## 1. College Affirmative Action and Students' Outcomes: A Matter of the Dose
**Authors:** Yi Chen, Chao Fu, Hongbin Li, Teng Li, Siyan Tang
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35281)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35281
**Abstract:** This paper examines how varying intensities of affirmative action policies affect student outcomes using China's college admissions system, which grants explicit bonus points to ethnic minorities. The authors track cohorts of applicants through long-run card-transaction spending data observed 10–23 years post-admission, enabling a rare measure of downstream consumption and earnings. Key findings document a non-monotonic ("dose-response") relationship between affirmative action intensity and outcomes: modest AA bonuses increase admission to colleges and elite colleges and raise long-run consumption by approximately 19%, while large bonuses lower recipients' academic ranks, shift them toward lower-paying fields, and reduce consumption by roughly 25%. The negative effects of high-dose AA are partially offset for students with stronger pre-admission academic foundations. The paper rules out peer-effects channels and demonstrates that the mechanism operates through program-college match quality. The results imply that careful calibration of affirmative action intensity is crucial for achieving its intended redistributive goals, and provide the first causal evidence on the full dosage-outcome gradient of affirmative action in a large developing-country setting.

## 2. State Ownership, Asset Prices, and Monetary Policy Transmission: A Tale of Two Sectors
**Authors:** Frederico Belo, Dapeng Hao, Xiaoji Lin, Zhigang Qiu, Jincheng Tong
**Date:** February 2026 (SSRN Working Paper No. 3966723); conditionally accepted, Review of Financial Studies
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3966723
**Abstract:** This paper studies how heterogeneity in state ownership shapes monetary policy transmission in China. The authors develop and estimate a dynamic general-equilibrium model with two types of firms—state-owned enterprises (SOEs) and privately owned enterprises (POEs)—featuring monetary supply shocks and endogenous financial frictions. The model is disciplined with matched firm-level data on borrowing costs, asset prices, and productivity. A central finding is that SOEs and POEs respond to monetary shocks and risk premiums in substantially different ways, both within and across ownership categories. Counterfactual analysis shows that more severe debt-market frictions for POEs amplify capital misallocation during contractionary monetary episodes, generating sizable losses in aggregate total factor productivity and output. The results highlight how the dual-sector structure of China's economy—characterized by the privileged credit access of state firms alongside financial constraints for private firms—creates a distinct transmission channel with policy implications for the People's Bank of China's conduct of monetary policy.

## 3. Market Contraction and Innovation Divergence: The Impact of the US–China Trade War on Chinese Innovation
**Authors:** Xiao Ma, Yueyuan Ma, Hanyi Tao, Yiran Zhang
**Date:** January 2026 (SSRN Working Paper No. 5455894; presented NBER Spring 2026 Chinese Economy Working Group)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5455894
**Abstract:** This paper studies the impact of US import tariffs on the intensity and direction of Chinese innovation during the trade-war period (2018–2022). Using textual analysis of patent abstracts, the authors decompose patents into technical-term vectors and measure the directional distance between Chinese and US innovation frontiers. They find that greater exposure to US tariffs reduces Chinese patent filings and—crucially—reduces the cosine similarity between Chinese and contemporaneous US patents, particularly for the most recent US technology vintages. This innovation divergence is not a simple decline: the results indicate that Chinese firms reallocated innovation effort away from technology adjacent to the US frontier and toward domestic-market or state-directed priorities. The authors develop a heterogeneous-firm model in which tariff-induced contractions in export demand reduce the returns to frontier-mimicking innovation, and calibrate it to data. Quantitatively, tariff-driven innovation divergence accounts for approximately 27% of the observed decline in Chinese-US patent similarity, contributing to a 14% reduction in Chinese export growth by 2021 and a 3.5% decline in total exports.

## 4. United States Financial Sanctions and the Surge of Chinese Lending
**Authors:** Daniel Berkowitz, Yichen Sun, Zehao Wang, Sili Zhou
**Date:** December 2025 (circulated version); presented at AEA Annual Meeting, January 2026
**Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2026/program/paper/DkG46fSs
**Abstract:** This paper examines how US financial sanctions on third-country firms created an opening for Chinese banks to expand their international loan portfolios. Using a large dataset of Chinese-origin and US-origin syndicated loans, the authors document a sharp increase in Chinese-bank lending supply to borrowers in sanctioned countries following each round of US sanctions. The empirical design exploits staggered sanction events as shocks to borrowing firms' access to US credit. Chinese-origin loan contracts offered to these borrowers feature systematically shorter maturities, a greater number of covenants, and higher refinancing rates relative to comparable US-origin contracts, granting Chinese lenders stronger ongoing control rights over borrower cash flows and asset sales. Firms that became dependent on Chinese credit subsequently exhibit reduced public disclosures and elevated rates of delisting or acquisition, consistent with the transfer of effective corporate governance to Chinese banks. The results provide causal evidence that US financial sanctions unintentionally accelerated Chinese banks' international expansion and deepened borrower-country dependence on Chinese financial institutions, with implications for the geopolitical architecture of global capital markets.

## 5. Environmental Impacts of China's Overseas Development Projects
**Authors:** Douglas Almond, Xinming Du
**Date:** January 2026 (SSRN Working Paper No. 6084486; CEEP Working Paper No. 37)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6084486
**Abstract:** This paper provides the first large-scale causal assessment of the environmental consequences of China's overseas development finance. Using 6,303 geocoded Chinese finance projects spanning 2000–2023 in more than 140 countries, combined with high-resolution satellite imagery and global biodiversity records, the authors construct a project-level panel and exploit variation in project implementation timing within narrow geographic cells. Project implementation leads to an immediate and persistent decline in surrounding vegetation cover of 0.3%—equivalent to an average annual loss of approximately 4,755 km² of vegetated land globally. Air quality deteriorates significantly in project zones, with aerosol optical depth rising by 0.9%. Biodiversity losses are concentrated at higher trophic levels: bird species richness falls by 1.6% and mammalian richness by 0.1% within project catchment areas. These effects are larger for infrastructure and extractive-industry projects than for manufacturing or social-sector investments. The findings document a systematic environmental footprint of China's Belt and Road Initiative and related overseas development activities, and have implications for international development finance governance and the design of environmental conditionality in Chinese loan agreements.
