## 1. Laboratories of Autocracy: Landscape of Central–Local Dynamics in China's Policy Universe
**Authors:** Kaicheng Luo, Shaoda Wang, David Y. Yang
**Date:** September 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 34219); featured on VoxChina February 25, 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34219
**Abstract:** This paper analyzes 3.7 million Chinese policy documents spanning two decades to track 115,679 distinct policies and measure how the locus of policymaking innovation has shifted over time. The authors find that roughly 80% of new policy ideas originated locally before 2013, with approximately two-thirds of locally initiated policies spreading to other prefectures within three years, and that individual bureaucrats—rather than localities—account for most policy innovation. After 2013, a sharp turn toward centralization increased top-down policies by 30% and reduced local policy experimentation and adaptation. To quantify the costs of centralization, the authors exploit variation in the alignment between centrally mandated industrial policies and local comparative advantages. Centralized industrial policies prove 18–22% less well-suited to regional economic structures than locally initiated alternatives, and the authors estimate this misalignment results in approximately ¥400 billion in forgone annual output, ¥32 billion in lost exports, and roughly 750 fewer patents per year. While decentralization generates its own distortions through inter-jurisdictional competition, the paper concludes that the economic costs of post-2013 centralization have significantly outweighed its benefits.

## 2. Information Acquisition By Mutual Fund Investors: Evidence from Stock Trading Suspensions
**Authors:** Clemens Sialm, David X. Xu
**Date:** November 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 34520); featured on VoxChina March 18, 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34520
**Abstract:** This paper examines how mutual funds' role as liquidity providers shapes investor information acquisition and stock price discovery using China's frequent stock trading suspensions as a natural experiment. When a stock is suspended, mutual funds holding the suspended security cannot accurately value their portfolios, leading to systematic NAV mispricing. The authors show that investors respond by actively gathering information about suspended stocks held by funds, driving capital flows toward undervalued funds and away from overvalued ones. Upon trading resumption, this information—reflected in fund flows—is incorporated into stock prices, enhancing price discovery. The paper identifies a novel channel through which the financial intermediation structure of equity markets influences information production: mutual fund liquidity creation stimulates information acquisition about illiquid, information-sensitive assets, with downstream effects on market efficiency.

## 3. Robots as Guardians: Industrial Automation and Workplace Safety in China
**Authors:** Wei Luo, Lixin Tang, Yaxin Yang, Xianqiang Zou
**Date:** 2025 (Journal of Development Economics 172: 103381); featured on VoxChina February 3, 2026
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2024.103381
**Abstract:** This paper investigates the relationship between industrial robot adoption and workplace safety in China. Between 2013 and 2019, China's national robot stock grew by nearly 500%; over the same period, workplace accidents per thousand population fell from 0.218 to 0.040 and fatality rates were cut in half. Using city-level robot exposure measures constructed from industry-level robot penetration data across three independent administrative safety metrics, the authors estimate that robot adoption accounts for roughly two-thirds of the observed decline in workplace accidents and over 70% of the reduction in fatalities—far exceeding the contribution of other concurrent trends. The safety gains are largest for older and less-educated workers employed in physically demanding jobs. Mechanism analysis reveals that robots improve safety primarily by making existing tasks safer rather than by displacing workers from hazardous occupations into safer ones. The findings suggest that automation can complement worker welfare in developing countries through occupational risk reduction, in addition to its well-studied productivity and wage effects.

## 4. Productivity Gains from Trade: Bunching Estimates from Trading Rights in China
**Authors:** Yunong Li, Yi Lu, Jianguo Wang
**Date:** 2025 (Review of Economics and Statistics 107: 1275–1290); featured on VoxChina January 14, 2026
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1162/rest_a_01434
**Abstract:** This paper estimates the causal effect of access to direct international trade on firm total factor productivity using China's trading rights system. Prior to WTO accession, China restricted the right to directly import and export goods to firms that met a minimum registered capital threshold, creating a sharp discontinuity in trade access. Using a bunching estimator that exploits the excess mass of firms registering just above this capital threshold, the authors identify firms that obtained trading rights specifically to access export and import markets. Firms gaining trading access experienced TFP increases of approximately 9.9%, with the gains accumulating gradually over time in a pattern consistent with "learning by trading" rather than one-time selection or investment effects; R&D expenditures and product mix changes do not account for the productivity gains. The benefits also extended to workers, with wages rising in firms that obtained trading access, and younger firms showed greater productivity responsiveness. The paper provides clean causal evidence that export market access builds productivity through learning mechanisms, contributing to debates about the productivity-trade nexus in developing countries.

## 5. High-Speed Rail and Local Agricultural Development
**Authors:** Xiaoguang Chen, Binlei Gong, Zhilong Qin, Xiaoli Wang
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics 179: 103647); featured on VoxChina January 7, 2026
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2025.103647
**Abstract:** This paper examines how China's rapid high-speed rail (HSR) expansion affects agricultural production in connected rural counties. Using a staggered difference-in-differences design that exploits the phased rollout of HSR stations across counties, the authors find that HSR access reduces agricultural labor inputs and cultivated cropland area, consistent with workers migrating to non-agricultural employment in better-connected urban areas. Yet agricultural output does not decline: total factor productivity in agriculture rises by 4.9–7.8% following HSR connectivity, fully offsetting the contraction in inputs. The paper identifies three mechanisms driving the productivity gains: (i) higher local incomes supported by HSR-induced growth raise government and household investment in agricultural infrastructure; (ii) reduced transportation costs enable rural farmers to participate in technical training programs and extension services offered in urban centers; and (iii) improved market access to high-value urban consumers incentivizes a shift toward higher-value crops and livestock. The findings suggest that transportation infrastructure investment can simultaneously accelerate structural transformation and sustain agricultural productivity, helping reconcile urbanization with food security in developing economies.
