## 1. Feeling the Same Heat? Urban–Rural Inequalities in Adapting to Extreme Temperatures via Electricity Consumption
**Authors:** Caiquan Bai, Jing Cao, Ming Huang, Chen Xi, Peng Zhang
**Date:** March 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 180, Article 103692)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2025.103692
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether urban and rural residents in China adapt unevenly to extreme temperature shocks, using novel high-frequency electricity consumption data. The authors construct a panel covering a large cross-section of Chinese localities and separate responses to short-run day-to-day heat events from longer-run climate warming trends. Both urban and rural groups increase electricity consumption in response to extreme heat, but urban residents exhibit significantly stronger adaptive responses. A key finding is that the urban–rural real income gap—tightly linked to adaptive capacity—accounts for nearly all of the observed adaptation inequality. Richer households can afford air conditioning and more energy-intensive cooling strategies, while rural households are constrained by income and infrastructure. The Theil index decomposition shows that high aggregate income inequality need not map directly onto electricity consumption inequality, as modernization and electrification have spread access even while private consumption diverges. The results highlight how differential adaptive capacity to climate change reflects deeper structural inequalities in China's economy, and suggest that policies narrowing the income gap could yield co-benefits in climate resilience.

## 2. Pollution Reduction Effects of New Transregional Power Transmission Systems: Evidence from Ultra-High-Voltage Projects of China
**Authors:** Shenggang Ren, Li Zhao, Peng Zhang, Caiquan Bai
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 179, Article 103641)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304387825001920
**Abstract:** This paper investigates the pollution reduction effects of China's ultra-high-voltage (UHV) electricity transmission projects, which transfer power from coal-rich western regions to high-demand eastern cities and thereby enable cleaner energy mixes at the point of consumption. The authors compile a comprehensive firm-level panel dataset covering pollution emissions, energy consumption, and economic data for 43,458 firms from 2007 to 2014, merging the China Environment Statistical Database (CESD) with the China National Tax Survey Database (CNTSD). A staggered difference-in-differences design compares firms in provinces that gained operational UHV lines to those that did not, separately identifying effects on power-exporting and power-importing provinces. The results show that UHV transmission projects reduce sulfur dioxide (SO₂) emissions by approximately 1.46 million tons per year, generating annual environmental benefits of roughly 29.2 billion CNY. The analysis distinguishes asymmetric effects between exporting and importing regions: importing provinces experience larger reductions in local industrial emissions, while exporting provinces see shifts in their energy and production mix. A welfare calculation that incorporates these environmental benefits shortens the payback period for UHV infrastructure investment from 28 years to approximately 5 years at a 2.5% discount rate, suggesting that standard cost-benefit analyses significantly understate the social return to energy infrastructure when environmental externalities are excluded.

## 3. Air Pollution as Comparative Disadvantage
**Authors:** Shi-Ting He, Liugang Sheng, Peng Zhang
**Date:** January 2026 (Journal of Public Economics, Vol. 254, Article 105560)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0047272725002592
**Abstract:** This paper provides the first systematic empirical analysis of how air pollution shapes exporters' comparative advantage in global markets and induces strategic firm-level responses. Using comprehensive firm-product export data from China spanning 2000 to 2007, the authors exploit exogenous variation in local air pollution concentrations induced by thermal inversions—meteorological episodes that trap pollutants near the surface—as an instrumental variable. The identification strategy isolates the causal effect of pollution on export performance, ruling out confounders such as simultaneous changes in local demand or regulatory enforcement. The results reveal a significant negative effect of air pollution on exports, operating through reduced labor productivity: higher pollution depresses worker output, raising unit costs and eroding competitive margins. The negative impacts are especially pronounced for labor-intensive products, since these goods depend more heavily on physical worker effort and are less able to substitute capital or technology for impaired labor. In response, affected firms strategically restructure their export product scope away from labor-intensive varieties. Larger firms exhibit greater resilience to these adverse effects, likely because of better access to abatement technologies and greater ability to diversify across product lines. Aggregate welfare calculations indicate that improving air quality in China would substantially enhance its comparative advantage in international trade while generating positive spillovers for the rest of the world through cheaper and more abundant Chinese goods.

## 4. Price and Volume Divergence in China's Real Estate Markets: The Role of Local Governments
**Authors:** Jeffery (Jinfan) Chang, Yuheng Wang, Wei Xiong
**Date:** September 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 34303)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34303
**Abstract:** During the COVID-19 pandemic period of 2020–2022, Chinese cities experienced a striking paradox: residential land and new house prices increased substantially while transaction volumes declined sharply. This paper attributes the price-volume divergence to active market management by local governments. Using a comprehensive dataset on land transactions, housing sales, Local Government Financing Vehicle (LGFV) balance sheets, and new home sales permit records, the authors identify three mechanisms through which local governments sustained prices: (1) restricting housing supply to limit downward price pressure; (2) directing LGFVs to purchase land at above-market prices, providing artificial price support; and (3) controlling the volume of new home sales permits to manage market pace. Cities that were more reliant on land sales revenue and land-backed debt prior to the pandemic exhibited larger price increases and more pronounced price-volume divergence, with LGFVs in these cities paying systematically higher prices for land than other market participants. While these interventions temporarily sustained local government fiscal revenues, they inadvertently worsened financial stress for property developers by reducing cash flows even as land costs remained high. The paper provides causal evidence that China's real estate market dynamics during this period were shaped as much by government-managed price formation as by underlying supply and demand fundamentals.

## 5. Taming Cycles: China's Growth Targets and Macroeconomic Management
**Authors:** Jeffery (Jinfan) Chang, Yuheng Wang, Wei Xiong
**Date:** April 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 33718)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33718
**Abstract:** This paper examines how China's hybrid economy uses annual growth targets set by local governments to guide macroeconomic management, and documents their unintended fiscal and allocative consequences. Using a rich panel of provincial and city-level data, the authors show that local governments systematically set overly ambitious targets during economic expansions and reduce them sluggishly during downturns—a pattern of asymmetric optimism. When regions fall behind their targets, they respond by ramping up infrastructure spending, accelerating land sales, and accumulating debt. Empirical estimates indicate that during the relatively stable period of 2011–2019, overly optimistic targets alone contributed an additional 14.0% of GDP to local government debt accumulation. While these countercyclical interventions moderated GDP deceleration and helped smooth business cycle volatility, they came at a significant cost: they weakened the informativeness of GDP as an economic indicator by decoupling it from corporate revenues, household consumption, and total factor productivity. The findings highlight a fundamental tension in China's growth model—using administratively set targets as a coordination device for economic management generates political incentives for fiscal activism that undermine long-run resource allocation and debt sustainability.
