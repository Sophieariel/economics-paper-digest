## 1. How Do Workers Adjust to Robots? Evidence from China
**Authors:** Osea Giuntella, Yi Lu, Tianyi Wang
**Date:** 2025 (*The Economic Journal*, Vol. 135, Issue 666, pp. 637–652); featured on VoxChina August 6, 2025
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/ueae090
**Abstract:** This paper estimates the causal effect of industrial robot adoption on Chinese workers using data from the International Federation of Robotics matched to the China Family Panel Studies (2010–2016). The authors construct industry-level robot exposure measures and exploit cross-industry variation in robot penetration to identify effects on individual labor market outcomes. A one standard deviation increase in robot exposure reduces employment probabilities by approximately 5 percentage points, decreases hourly wages by 8%, and raises labor force exit rates by 12.6%. The negative impacts fall disproportionately on low-skilled workers, male workers, and older workers, who are less able to adapt through reskilling. The paper identifies two distinct adjustment channels: younger workers (aged 16–44) respond by significantly increasing enrollment in technical and vocational training programs, while older workers are pushed toward early retirement. These divergent responses suggest that automation creates a two-tier labor market adjustment process in a developing economy, with younger cohorts acquiring new skills to remain competitive but older workers facing permanent displacement. The findings are especially policy-relevant for China, which became the world's largest industrial robot market after 2014, and contribute to a growing literature on automation's distributional effects in emerging economies where labor market institutions differ substantially from those in advanced countries.

## 2. Money or Monitoring: Evidence on Improving Worker Effort
**Authors:** Jing Cai, Sai Luo, Shing-Yi Wang
**Date:** June 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 33977); featured on VoxChina September 17, 2025
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33977
**Abstract:** This paper uses a randomized field experiment conducted at a Chinese automobile manufacturing firm to compare the effectiveness of two canonical management interventions—financial incentives and monitoring—in improving worker effort among newly hired employees. Workers were randomly assigned to receive signing bonuses (16% of three-month compensation), enhanced monitoring intensity (weekly supervisor visits), both, or neither. The experiment yields three main findings. First, signing bonuses significantly increase job acceptance rates, extend working hours, and reduce voluntary turnover, but do not improve the quality of work as assessed by supervisors. Second, enhanced monitoring substantially raises supervisor-assessed performance quality and lowers rejection rates, but increases voluntary turnover by making the job less attractive. Third, because replacement costs are high, the net effect of monitoring on firm profitability is negative despite its performance benefits, while financial bonuses deliver a positive net return. The paper formalizes these trade-offs in a simple principal-agent model that incorporates effort on both quantity and quality margins alongside workers' outside options. The results inform optimal labor contract design in Chinese manufacturing—a sector that employs hundreds of millions of workers—and highlight how the two instruments generate fundamentally different behavioral responses that managers must weigh against each other.

## 3. Why Are So Many Young Women Moving to China's Big Cities?
**Authors:** Yumi Koh, Jing Li, Yifan Wu, Junjian Yi, Hanzhe Zhang
**Date:** 2025 (*Journal of Development Economics*, Vol. 172, Article 103378); featured on VoxChina December 3, 2025
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2024.103378
**Abstract:** This paper documents a striking and largely overlooked feature of China's urbanization: among migrants aged 16–25, women substantially outnumber men in Chinese cities, and the gender gap in urban migration is larger in bigger cities. The authors use China's staggered rollout of special economic zones (SEZs) in the late 1990s as a natural experiment to establish the causal effect of urban economic opportunities on gender-specific migration. SEZ designation increases young female migration by approximately 6 percentage points relative to males; this gender asymmetry disappears among married cohorts, providing strong evidence against labor-demand or educational-opportunity explanations. The paper instead identifies two marriage-market mechanisms: a hypergamy norm (women are expected to "marry up" socially) and the patrilocal residence norm (couples settle near the husband's family), both of which create stronger incentives for single women to migrate to cities with richer pools of potential partners. The authors rule out alternative explanations including gender-specific sectoral demand, amenity preferences, and educational access. Using census data, they confirm that "migrate to marry" is the second-most cited reason for female migration. The findings reveal that marriage market incentives are a quantitatively important driver of urbanization in China, with implications for understanding urban-rural inequality, gender differentials in economic outcomes, and the long-run consequences of the one-child policy's sex-ratio distortions for internal migration.

## 4. Firm Selection and Growth in Carbon Offset Markets: Evidence from the Clean Development Mechanism
**Authors:** Qiaoyi Chen, Nicholas Ryan, Daniel Xu
**Date:** April 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 33636); featured on VoxChina October 29, 2025
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33636
**Abstract:** This paper examines the performance of the Clean Development Mechanism (CDM)—the world's largest carbon offset program—among Chinese manufacturing firms. Using firm-level panel data matched to CDM project records, the authors find that offset-selling firms increase emissions by 49% over four years following CDM project approval, directly contrary to the program's projected 20% emissions reductions. The authors decompose this counterintuitive result into two mechanisms. First, a selection effect accounts for approximately 35 percentage points: CDM approval requirements favor high-growth firms with strong profitability trajectories, so approved firms would have expanded substantially even without the program. Second, a scale effect contributes approximately 14 percentage points: CDM projects fund efficiency-enhancing capital investments that lower marginal costs and thereby stimulate output expansion, raising absolute emissions even as emissions intensity falls. A welfare analysis finds that damages from increased emissions are roughly four times larger than the private gains accruing to Chinese manufacturers and European carbon credit buyers, implying net global welfare losses of approximately $65 billion from Chinese participation in the CDM. The paper contributes to the industrial policy and environmental economics literatures by providing the first systematic causal evidence on how voluntary, project-based carbon offset mechanisms interact with firm-level growth incentives, and by identifying adverse selection and moral hazard as fundamental barriers to credible emissions abatement through offset markets.

## 5. A Trade-off Between Monetary Policy Transmission and Systemic Risk in China
**Authors:** Kaiji Chen, Yiqing Xiao, Tao Zha
**Date:** July 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 34056); featured on VoxChina September 24, 2025
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34056
**Abstract:** This paper investigates a fundamental tension in China's monetary policy framework: the same channel that makes interest-rate tools effective also generates systemic risk. Using quarterly bank-level data and an estimated policy rule for the 7-day repo rate, the authors document that non-state banks' access to wholesale interbank funding amplifies the transmission of monetary policy easing to lending—a "liquidity reallocation channel" in which funds flow from state banks with deposit surpluses to non-state banks with credit demand. This channel improves resource allocation toward more productive private firms. However, it also increases non-state banks' vulnerability to systemic risk during economic downturns: since 2018, non-state banks dependent on wholesale funding have experienced markedly larger increases in expected capital shortfalls (SRISK). The authors develop and estimate a structural model incorporating China's dual-track interest rate system, segmented deposit markets, and heterogeneous bank types. Counterfactual analysis reveals a sharp macroprudential dilemma: stricter regulation of wholesale funding that reduces the banking crisis probability by roughly half simultaneously reduces the output gains from monetary policy easing by nearly half. The paper has direct implications for the People's Bank of China's design of macroprudential tools and for understanding how the ownership structure of China's banking system shapes macroeconomic policy effectiveness.
