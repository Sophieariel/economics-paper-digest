## 1. How Credible Is Hong Kong's Currency Peg?
**Authors:** Urban Jermann, Bin Wei, Vivian Yue
**Date:** September 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 34300); featured on VoxChina April 8, 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34300
**Abstract:** This paper develops an asset-pricing framework to measure the time-varying credibility of Hong Kong's Linked Exchange Rate System (LERS), which has pegged the Hong Kong Dollar to the U.S. dollar since 1983. The authors depart from standard fixed-rate models by allowing for the possibility of a regime break, deriving closed-form solutions for exchange rates and option prices under imperfect peg credibility. Using daily Hong Kong Dollar spot rates and derivatives prices, they recover market-implied probabilities of peg survival and the fundamental value of the HKD under an alternative regime. The empirical estimates reveal that peg credibility is not constant: it weakens when U.S. interest rates rise sharply, when local interbank liquidity tightens, and when renminbi movements signal stress in Mainland China's financial system. The authors show that their option-pricing approach yields substantially more realistic assessments of peg sustainability than conventional Black–Scholes models applied to currency options, because it properly accounts for the possibility—however small—that the peg could be abandoned. The results carry implications for understanding the interaction between Hong Kong's financial system, U.S. monetary policy, and China's capital account, and for pricing hedges against a potential depegging event.

## 2. From Rural Schools to City Factories: Assessing the Quality of Chinese Rural Schools
**Authors:** Eric A. Hanushek, Le Kang, Xueying Li, Lei Zhang
**Date:** July 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 34005); featured on VoxChina January 21, 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34005
**Abstract:** This paper develops a novel strategy for measuring the quality of rural education across Chinese provinces by exploiting the earnings of rural-to-urban migrants working in the same urban labor market. The core identification idea is that interprovincial migrants employed in identical city jobs who differ only in their province of basic schooling provide a direct estimate of provincial school quality: variation in the wage returns to an additional year of schooling across provinces of origin—holding urban destination fixed—isolates the human capital contribution of origin-province schooling. Applying this strategy to large-scale survey data, the authors find that annual returns to schooling range from 1.6 to 3.1 percentage points across Chinese provinces, a difference that is economically large and robust to controls for worker selection and destination fixed effects. Provincial quality estimates correlate closely with independently collected cognitive test scores, validating the methodology. A striking finding is that younger cohorts exhibit both higher average quality and reduced cross-provincial dispersion, suggesting that recent central government policies targeting rural school improvement have had measurable success. However, conventional school-resource measures—teacher-student ratios and per-pupil expenditure—are essentially uncorrelated with estimated school quality, cautioning against using these inputs as proxies for educational attainment in China's rural sector.

## 3. Integrating Jurisdictions, Dividing Workers: Consolidation, Labor Markets, and the Migrant-Local Wage Gap in China
**Authors:** Shiyu Bo, Yi Wang
**Date:** 2025 (Journal of Urban Economics, Vol. 149, Article 103788); featured on VoxChina December 24, 2025
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jue.2025.103788
**Abstract:** This paper examines how China's city-county merger reforms—which absorbed surrounding rural counties into adjacent prefectural-level cities—reshaped local labor markets. The authors exploit the staggered rollout of consolidation policies across city-regions in a difference-in-differences design, using individual-level survey data to track labor market outcomes for both local residents and rural migrants in affected areas. They find that jurisdictional consolidation raised aggregate wages through two channels: it expanded the effective scale of local goods and factor markets, and it improved governance capacity by concentrating administrative resources. However, the wage gains are distributed unevenly. Local urban residents—who hold urban hukou and have full access to public services and job protections—captured significantly larger earnings gains than migrant workers, who remain subject to residence permit restrictions even after jurisdictional unification. The migrant-local wage gap actually widened in post-consolidation periods, driven partly by selective in-migration of higher-skill workers attracted by the improved urban environment and partly by continued institutional barriers that prevent migrants from fully participating in the expanded local labor market. The findings illustrate how political consolidation can promote aggregate economic growth while simultaneously sharpening distributional fault lines along the migrant-local dimension, with implications for China's ongoing hukou reform debates.

## 4. Barriers to Entry and Regional Economic Growth in China
**Authors:** Loren Brandt, Gueorgui Kambourov, Kjetil Storesletten
**Date:** January 2026 (Review of Economic Studies, Vol. 93, No. 1, pp. 286–326); featured on VoxChina December 9, 2025
**Source:** https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-abstract/93/1/286/8159677
**Abstract:** This paper quantifies how barriers to entrepreneurial entry shape regional economic disparities and aggregate growth in China. The authors combine comprehensive firm-level data from China's Industrial Census with a quantitative macroeconomic model that features heterogeneous firms and three distinct entry-related distortions: capital market frictions that raise the cost of financing new ventures, output market frictions arising from state-sector competition, and direct administrative barriers to operating a new private business (often linked to the presence of large state-owned enterprises). Estimating these distortions separately for each Chinese province and time period, the authors find that barriers to starting new firms are a first-order driver of regional economic differences. In counterfactual exercises, eliminating entry barriers alone explains 15% of total factor productivity growth and 35% of real wage growth observed across provinces from 1995 to 2008. The link between entry barriers and SOE dominance is particularly strong: provinces with larger state sectors impose higher costs on private entrepreneurs, generating a reinforcing cycle of SOE persistence and weak private-sector dynamism. The paper thus provides a unified account of China's regional divergence, the slowdown in private-sector growth in high-SOE provinces, and the aggregate productivity gains that accompanied China's WTO-era reforms, which reduced some of these barriers in export-oriented sectors.

## 5. China's Successful Securities Regulation to Protect Investors
**Authors:** Thomas Bourveau, Xingchao Gao, Rongchen Li, Frank S. Zhou
**Date:** 2025 (Journal of Accounting and Economics, forthcoming; SSRN Working Paper No. 4436460); featured on VoxChina November 12, 2025
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4436460
**Abstract:** This paper studies whether flexible "comply-or-explain" regulation can effectively protect minority shareholders in an emerging market without sacrificing corporate investment and growth. The setting is a 2012 Shanghai Stock Exchange directive requiring all listed firms with positive annual profits to either distribute at least 30% of net income as cash dividends or publicly disclose, in their annual reports, an explanation for withholding distributions. Using a difference-in-differences strategy that exploits cross-firm variation in profitability around the regulation's effective threshold, the authors find that the policy achieved near-universal compliance: 98% of eligible firms satisfied the requirement within two years. More substantively, the regulation reduced corporate tunneling—measured by related-party transactions that siphon cash from minority shareholders to controlling stockholders—by approximately 38%. Crucially, this investor protection gain did not come at the cost of real investment: compliant firms did not reduce capital expenditures or R&D relative to control firms. The paper interprets these findings as evidence that regulatory design matters: mandating a hard dividend floor would have induced distortions, whereas the comply-or-explain approach imposed reputational costs on tunneling firms without binding the investment decisions of genuinely constrained ones. The results offer lessons for securities regulation design in other emerging markets where controlling-shareholder expropriation is a first-order governance problem.
