## 1. China's Mercantilist Squeeze on Developing Countries
**Authors:** Shoumitro Chatterjee, Arvind Subramanian
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.piie.com/publications/working-papers/2026/chinas-mercantilist-squeeze-developing-countries (PIIE Working Paper 26-7)
**Abstract:** This paper examines what the authors term the "China Squeeze"—three simultaneous competitive pressures weighing on low- and middle-income countries: intense competition in global export markets, rising Chinese import penetration in their own domestic markets, and limited access to China's consumer market for labor-intensive goods. Using CEPII-BACI bilateral trade data, the authors show that despite China's technological ascent, it continues to dominate sectors such as apparel, footwear, and light manufacturing that historically served as entry points for labor-surplus developing economies. The scale of the squeeze is, by the authors' assessment, "historically unprecedented and may represent hundreds of billions of dollars in lost exports and forgone jobs in labor-intensive manufacturing." Macroeconomic indicators on wages, productivity, and exchange-rate policy suggest that distortions—especially a persistently undervalued renminbi—may have amplified China's export surge, though the authors note this is not the sole explanation. The paper concludes that China's sustained dominance in low-skill-intensive sectors may be "closing off the traditional manufacturing-led development path for low- and middle-income countries," posing a structural challenge that goes beyond cyclical trade-policy disputes.

## 2. Desired Fertility, Realized Fertility, and the Effects of China's Universal Two-Child Policy
**Authors:** Hanming Fang, Chang Liu
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 182, Article 103796)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304387826000799
**Abstract:** This paper studies the impact of China's 2016 Universal Two-Child (UTC) policy, which lifted birth restrictions for all married couples. Drawing on the 2017 China Fertility Survey—sampling 249,946 women aged 15–60 across 2,737 counties in all 31 provinces—the authors compare women who had one child in late 2015 and became newly eligible under the UTC against those who had already been eligible for a second birth. The study documents a markedly compressed distribution of fertility desires: 29.6% of women prefer one child, 58.1% prefer two, and only 11.4% prefer three or more. Despite this, the UTC raised births by only approximately 0.025 children per newly eligible woman—roughly 744,000 additional births per year, just 4.1% of China's annual total—releasing only 16.6% of China's fertility potential. Childrearing costs, proxied by local housing prices, significantly dampen the policy's effectiveness: a one-standard-deviation increase in log housing prices reduces policy-induced births by 0.007. The findings indicate that China's fertility shortfall is driven primarily by what women want rather than by government restrictions, and that relaxing birth limits without addressing the economic costs of child-rearing—particularly housing—will have limited demographic impact.

## 3. The Stalled Quiet Revolution: Population Control, Skewed Sex Ratios, and the Widening Gender Gap in Labor Force Participation
**Authors:** Zihao Chen, Yawen Ding, Xu Tian
**Date:** June 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 182, Article 103798)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103798
**Abstract:** China's female labor force participation (FLFP) has declined over recent decades, bucking the global "quiet revolution" of rising women's economic engagement. This paper attributes the widening gender gap to the long-run unintended consequences of China's One-Child Policy (OCP), which skewed local sex ratios toward males. Exploiting cross-county variation in sex-ratio distortions generated by the OCP as the primary identification strategy, the authors find that the policy reduced women's labor force participation by 12 percentage points relative to male counterparts. Mechanism analysis reveals that women exposed to more male-skewed sex ratios exhibit higher marriage and fertility rates, improved spousal income, greater intra-household bargaining power, and more leisure time—all factors that reduce labor market attachment. These household-level responses, rather than labor demand effects, account for most of the FLFP decline. The paper connects China's demographic interventions directly to its labor market outcomes, arguing that the stalling of China's gender gap closure is a structural consequence of birth-control policy rather than cultural preferences, with implications for how fertility policy reforms interact with female labor supply.

## 4. Restricting Video Games in China: Effects on Time Use, Educational Achievement, and Health
**Authors:** Zhejian Wang
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 182, Article 103812)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103812
**Abstract:** China's August 2021 Online Gaming Regulation virtually eliminated weekday online gaming for minors under 18, capping play at three hours per week on weekends and holidays only. Using nationally representative survey data and a difference-in-differences design that compares affected minors to unaffected adults and to pre-regulation cohorts, this paper estimates the regulation's short-run effects on time use, academic outcomes, and health. The study finds sharp and statistically robust declines in gaming engagement and overall Internet use among the targeted age group, consistent with meaningful regulatory compliance. However, the paper finds no robust short-run improvements in academic performance—as measured by self-reported homework time, academic satisfaction, and exam preparation—or in health outcomes including physical activity and sleep duration. The results suggest that while the regulation successfully compressed screen time, the freed time was not systematically redirected into productive educational or health-improving activities in the short run, raising questions about the efficacy of device-restriction policies without complementary interventions that channel time toward beneficial alternatives.

## 5. The Art of Not Being Chocked: Environmental Awareness, Residents' Responses, and Land Transactions in China
**Authors:** Wei Zou, Pinghan Liang
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 182)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304387826000404
**Abstract:** This paper studies how a sudden increase in public environmental awareness alters residential location choices and, through the Tiebout mechanism, depresses local government land revenue in polluted Chinese cities. The authors use the unexpected national broadcast of "Under the Dome" in February 2015—a widely viewed environmental documentary that dramatized China's air pollution crisis—as an exogenous shock to pollution awareness. Collecting all publicly available land-use-right transaction records from 2013 to 2017 and implementing a generalized difference-in-differences design that exploits cross-city variation in pre-existing pollution levels and documentary viewership, the paper shows that heightened environmental awareness substantially increases migration intentions and actual out-migration toward cleaner cities. This "voting with feet" behavior reduces residential demand in heavily polluted cities, materially depressing local government land transaction revenue—a critical fiscal resource for Chinese municipalities. The magnitude of the effect is economically significant: cities at the 75th percentile of pollution exposure see land revenue decline considerably relative to cleaner cities following the documentary release. The results show that environmental awareness changes not only individual behaviors but also the fiscal constraints facing local governments, with implications for understanding how pollution externalities are capitalized into local public finance and for the political economy of environmental regulation in China.
