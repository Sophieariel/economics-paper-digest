# China Economics Papers — 2026-06-21

## 1. China's Mercantilist Squeeze on Developing Countries
**Authors:** Shoumitro Chatterjee (Johns Hopkins University), Arvind Subramanian (Peterson Institute for International Economics)
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** PIIE Working Paper 26-7 — https://www.piie.com/publications/working-papers/2026/chinas-mercantilist-squeeze-developing-countries
**Abstract:** This paper documents a "China Squeeze": the compression of industrialization space available to low- and middle-income countries in labor-intensive manufacturing. China's economic dominance constrains growth opportunities for developing nations through three pathways: intense rivalry in global export sectors, heightened competition within their domestic markets, and restricted entry into China's consumer market for labor-intensive goods from poorer countries. Using gross trade flows, value-added exports, historical benchmarks, and labor-endowment comparisons, the authors show that China, despite becoming richer and moving up the technology value chain, continues to occupy a historically unprecedented share of global low-skill export markets. The scale of the squeeze may represent hundreds of billions of dollars in lost exports and forgone manufacturing jobs in developing economies. Evidence on wages, productivity, and exchange rate policy suggests that macroeconomic distortions—especially an undervalued renminbi—may have played a contributing role.

## 2. On the Negative Consequences of Low-Wage Offshoring for Innovation
**Authors:** Wulong Gu (Statistics Canada), Alla Lileeva (York University), Daniel Trefler (University of Toronto / NBER)
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper 35167 — https://www.nber.org/papers/w35167
**Abstract:** This paper challenges the assumption that outsourcing intermediate inputs to low-wage countries like China necessarily drives domestic innovation. The authors identify two competing mechanisms: offshoring reduces costs and boosts returns to innovation for large firms, but it also lowers production costs for older, mature products, discouraging investment in newer innovations. Using a sample of 6,024 Canadian manufacturing firms spanning 2002–2011, and exploiting quality improvements in Chinese intermediate inputs as an instrument, the authors find that greater offshoring to China and Mexico reduced aggregate R&D spending by 15% over the period. This reduction operated through two channels: reduced R&D expenditures at incumbent R&D-performing firms and decreased startup of new R&D projects. While rising Chinese import quality creates winners among the very largest offshorers, it suppresses innovation among smaller competitors, resulting in a net negative effect on aggregate innovation.

## 3. Price Effects of US–China Trade Wars
**Authors:** Thuy Hang Duong (Australian National University), Weifeng Larry Liu (Australian National University)
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** CAMA Working Paper 30/2026, Crawford School of Public Policy, ANU — https://crawford.anu.edu.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/30_2026_Duong_Liu_0.pdf
**Abstract:** This paper provides a comprehensive analysis of how US tariffs on Chinese goods have affected domestic prices across two major protectionist episodes: 2018–2019 and 2024–2025. The authors analyze US firms' sourcing patterns to highlight the role of production linkages with China in shaping price pass-through. Because US tariffs were introduced in multiple rounds at different points in time, the paper addresses cohort-specific exposure and potentially heterogeneous dynamic responses using a staggered difference-in-differences approach, which avoids the bias produced by conventional two-way fixed-effect event-study specifications when policy changes are staggered. The paper documents heterogeneous tariff effects across protectionist episodes, product groups, and treatment timing, and shows that the propagation of tariff costs through supply chains amplifies aggregate price impacts beyond what single-round analyses would suggest.

## 4. Evading the Ban: Smuggling, Pollution, and the Welfare Effects of China's Waste Import Restrictions
**Authors:** Hanwei Huang (London School of Economics), Yuyuan Yu (London School of Economics)
**Date:** May 15, 2026
**Source:** CEP Discussion Paper dp2181, Centre for Economic Performance, LSE — https://ideas.repec.org/p/cep/cepdps/dp2181.html
**Abstract:** This paper examines the trade-offs inherent in using import restrictions for environmental objectives, studying China's ban on solid waste imports. While the ban achieved measurable improvements in air and water quality, it simultaneously triggered significant evasion behaviors, including surging quantity underreporting, heightened smuggling-related criminal activity, and deteriorating performance among affected domestic firms. The authors develop a hybrid sufficient-statistic framework that combines reduced-form evasion elasticities with structural estimates of shadow costs to quantify the total welfare effects of the policy. Their key finding is that the environmental gains from the ban were more than offset by the costs of smuggling and losses from regulatory distortions, highlighting how import restrictions can produce unintended consequences through illicit trade channels that ultimately undermine the policy's stated objectives.

## 5. Product Scope Adjustment to the China Shock: Competition at Home and Abroad
**Authors:** Jaerim Choi (University of Hawaii), Seongin Hong (Yonsei University), Jung Hur (Yonsei University), Manho Kang (Yonsei University)
**Date:** Published online May 9, 2026
**Source:** Journal of Comparative Economics — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0147596726000454
**Abstract:** This paper examines how China's rise as a dominant exporter affects multi-product manufacturing plants' product scope decisions when they face Chinese competition simultaneously in domestic and foreign markets. Using comprehensive Korean plant-level panel data, the authors construct plant-level measures of exposure to both domestic import penetration from China and export competition with Chinese firms in third markets. They find that both forms of competition reduce overall product scope, but through distinct margins: export competition in third markets primarily reduces product creation, while domestic import penetration primarily raises product exit. Both channels increase within-plant product specialization. The results imply that the China trade shock induces a process of product rationalization that extends beyond domestic labor markets to the product-mix decisions of surviving manufacturing establishments.
