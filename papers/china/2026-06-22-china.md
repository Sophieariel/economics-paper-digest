## 1. English's Significance: Exam Performance by Subject and Future Income in China
**Authors:** Ruixue Jia, Hongbin Li, Yu Qin, Qiuyi Wang, Jing Wu
**Date:** February 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 181, Article 103756)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103756
**Abstract:** This paper uses administrative data on approximately 190,000 individuals born between 1980 and 1985 to examine how subject-specific scores on China's National College Entrance Examination (gaokao) predict long-run labor market earnings. The gaokao includes scores in Chinese language, Mathematics, and English, enabling the authors to decompose the returns to exam performance by subject. The central finding is that while all three subjects correlate positively with future income, English scores are the most influential predictor—substantially more so than Chinese or Math after controlling for overall exam performance, educational attainment, and family background. The English premium operates primarily through employer demand from foreign-invested enterprises, suggesting that the returns to language skills are driven by globalization and China's integration into international product and capital markets rather than by general cognitive ability. The results shed light on how shifts in the global economy reshape the returns to different types of human capital within a country, with implications for education policy, curriculum design, and inequality in a rapidly globalizing developing economy.

## 2. Raising Grievances to the State: The Political Economic Effects of Anti-Corruption Crackdowns on Labor Activism in China
**Authors:** Huiyi Chen
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 181, Article 103758)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103758
**Abstract:** This paper studies how government anti-corruption enforcement shapes workers' incentives to strike, using China's sweeping anti-corruption campaign launched in 2012 as a natural experiment. The author assembles a city-level panel linking the timing of high-profile corruption inspections to recorded strike incidents from 2011 to 2020. Strike incidents rise sharply following a city's first high-profile inspection—doubling within one year and tripling within two years—concentrated in wage-arrears disputes in construction and manufacturing. The mechanism runs through the weakening of firm–government ties: inspections constrained the ability of firms to use political connections to suppress labor grievances and redirected government attention toward accountability, raising workers' expected returns from collective action. Consistent with this mechanism, the strike increase is most pronounced in cities with higher pre-campaign corruption levels, where political suppression of grievances had been strongest. The findings reveal that corruption distorts labor markets not only through the standard channels of resource misallocation and rent extraction but also by systematically repressing workers' ability to voice legitimate grievances—and that anti-corruption enforcement, while not designed as a labor policy, can have large unintended effects on collective bargaining and worker welfare.

## 3. Winning Hearts After Tying Hands: The Political Impact of Land Titling Reform in China
**Authors:** Zhi-An Hu, Zhuo Nie, Jiaqi Zhao
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 182, Article 103800)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103800
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether formalizing land rights can constrain predatory behavior by local officials and, in turn, increase citizens' political trust in the state. The authors study China's rural land titling initiative, which distributed legally recognized land certificates to rural households through a staggered rollout across counties beginning in 2011. Exploiting this variation in a difference-in-differences framework, they find that land titling significantly increased political trust toward the central and local governments. The trust effect is driven primarily by the reform's constraint on officials' ability to expropriate or reallocate land without legal recourse: by formalizing property rights, the reform reduced perceived vulnerability to arbitrary state action. Economic gains from titling are limited in magnitude, indicating that the political effects operate through a rights-security channel rather than through income or wealth changes. The paper also documents that titling increased participation in government-administered social security schemes—consistent with stronger institutional trust—while reducing reliance on informal local electoral channels. The findings contribute to a literature on how procedural fairness and property rights security shape political legitimacy in autocratic regimes, and to debates about the scope and limits of administrative reform as a tool for building state–society trust in developing countries.

## 4. Moving to Opportunity for Polluting: Intra-City Evidence from China's Land Market
**Authors:** Shiyu Bo, Fan Zhang, Hongjia Zhu
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 182, Article 103810)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103810
**Abstract:** This paper documents a novel within-city "pollution haven" effect: in response to tightened environmental regulation, polluting firms do not simply shut down or abate—they relocate to less monitored locations within the same city. The authors exploit China's 12th Five-Year Plan on Air Pollution Prevention and Control, which imposed substantially more stringent emissions standards on 47 designated cities relative to 70 comparable control cities, to implement a triple-difference identification strategy combining cross-city, within-city spatial, and pre–post variation. The core finding is that after the plan's implementation, firms in polluting industries in treatment cities leased new industrial land at systematically greater distances from air-quality monitoring stations than comparable firms in control cities. Both firm-level demand responses and—primarily—supply-side land allocation decisions by local governments drive the relocation pattern: local authorities channeled new industrial land parcels to peripheral urban locations to accommodate production while meeting headline air-quality metrics at monitoring points. While the regulation generated meaningful aggregate welfare gains through improved air quality in monitored areas, these gains were partially offset by welfare losses arising from firms' reduced productivity at remote locations and by the incomplete nature of enforcement. The paper provides microeconomic evidence on how regulatory design and the spatial distribution of monitoring infrastructure interact to shape the location of economic activity in developing-country cities.

## 5. Product Quality, the Scope of Export Destinations, and Skill Demand for Sustainable Development: An Investigation for China
**Authors:** Zhuangxiong Yu, Yang Yang, Jiemiao Dong, Pundarik Mukhopadhaya
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 182, Article 103794)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103794
**Abstract:** This paper examines how firms' product quality and export-destination scope jointly determine demand for skilled labor, using firm-level data from Guangdong Province over 2013–2015. The authors extend standard skill-selection models in international trade by incorporating the breadth of a firm's export-destination portfolio as a mediating variable in the quality-to-skill-demand transmission mechanism. In contrast to prior models that assume a linear relationship between product quality and skill upgrading, the paper identifies an inverted U-shaped relationship between export-destination scope and the quality improvements associated with a given skill mix: as firms penetrate more destinations, quality pressure initially rises and then plateaus. A key finding is that when product quality surpasses a critical threshold, firms that narrow their destination scope—concentrating on high-valuation markets—experience the strongest skill-upgrading incentives, because serving demanding customers requires intensive use of high-skilled labor in production and quality control. Vertical differentiation between destination markets and trade costs (with linguistic distance mattering more than geographic distance) mediate how destination scope affects the quality–skill link. The results have implications for how trade liberalization interacts with labor market outcomes and human capital accumulation in a major exporting economy undergoing structural transformation toward higher value-added production.
