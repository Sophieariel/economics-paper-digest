## 1. Policy Experimentation in China: The Political Economy of Policy Learning
**Authors:** Shaoda Wang and David Y. Yang
**Date:** 2025 (Journal of Political Economy, Vol. 133, No. 7, pp. 2180–2228)
**Source:** https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/734873 (DOI: 10.1086/734873; NBER Working Paper No. 29402: https://www.nber.org/papers/w29402)
**Abstract:** This paper analyzes 19,812 government documents on Chinese policy experiments conducted between 1980 and 2020, constructing a database of 633 policy experiments by 98 central ministries. Three findings emerge. First, experimentation sites exhibit strong positive selection on economic development, driven by misaligned incentives across political hierarchies: lower-level governments nominate sites likely to succeed and thereby enhance their promotion prospects, while the central government selects sites that are more developed and thus more likely to generate visible results. Second, local politicians exert strategic excess effort during policy experiments that inflates apparent policy effectiveness in ways that cannot be replicated during national rollout—an observational regularity the authors term "Potemkin performance." Third, the central government fails to fully account for these distortions when evaluating experimental outcomes, systematically biasing policy learning and distorting national policy choices. The paper quantifies these distortions using a structural model and documents that their magnitude is economically large. While China's institutions enable policy experimentation at unparalleled scale—with hundreds of pilots across every policy domain simultaneously—strategic gaming by local actors significantly limits the effectiveness of the resulting policy learning. The findings have implications for the design of pilot programs in developing countries and authoritarian political systems more broadly.

## 2. When Forward-Looking Accounting Meets an Unexpected Crisis: Does the Expected Credit Loss Model Amplify Economic Downturns?
**Authors:** Chen Chen and Difang Huang
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Accounting Research); featured on VoxChina June 24, 2026
**Source:** https://voxchina.org/show-3-463.html (DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-679X.70030)
**Abstract:** This paper exploits China's staggered adoption of the Expected Credit Loss (ECL) accounting model—which requires banks to provision for anticipated future loan losses rather than realized losses—as a quasi-natural experiment to study whether forward-looking provisioning amplifies credit contractions during economic crises. Using loan-level data spanning the COVID-19 period, the authors compare listed Chinese banks that adopted ECL to unlisted banks that retained the incurred credit loss method, including specifications that match ECL and non-ECL banks serving identical borrowers. ECL-adopting banks reduced lending more sharply during COVID-19, increased interest spreads, and became more selective in evaluating new borrowers relative to their matched counterparts. Firms dependent on ECL banks experienced persistent declines in assets, loans, and revenues extending well beyond the acute pandemic phase, suggesting that procyclical provisioning generated real economic scarring. The results reveal a fundamental policy tradeoff in the design of bank accounting standards: forward-looking provisioning under ECL improves bank screening quality and loan portfolio soundness during normal times, but may require countercyclical regulatory safeguards—such as provisioning overlays or capital buffer releases—to prevent excessive credit tightening precisely when firms are most vulnerable. The paper contributes to debates about the procyclicality of international financial reporting standards (IFRS 9 and CECL) and the interaction between accounting rules and macroprudential policy.

## 3. The Crowding-In Effects of Local Government Debt in China
**Authors:** Xiaoming Li, Zheng Liu, Yuchao Peng, and Zhiwei Xu
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Monetary Economics); originally Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco Working Paper No. 2024-35 (November 2024)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304393226000371 (FRBSF Working Paper: https://ideas.repec.org/p/fip/fedfwp/99080.html)
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how a change in the composition of local government debt—induced by China's large-scale debt-to-bond swap program initiated in 2015—affected bank risk-taking, credit allocation, and local productivity. Before the swap, Chinese local governments had accumulated substantial off-balance-sheet liabilities in the form of municipal corporate debt held by banks; the swap converted this debt into formally recognized government bonds. Using a difference-in-differences identification strategy and confidential loan-level data, the authors show that the swap significantly increased bank risk-taking through a capital-regulation channel operating under Basel III: replacing municipal corporate debt (which carried positive risk weights) with government bonds (risk-weight zero) reduced risk-weighted assets and freed up regulatory capital, enabling banks to reallocate credit toward riskier private borrowers. Banks reduced credit spreads on loans to private-owned enterprises (POEs) relative to state-owned enterprises (SOEs), with significantly larger reductions in provinces with higher outstanding government debt. This reallocation of credit from lower-productivity SOEs toward more productive POEs raised local total factor productivity, generating a crowding-in—rather than the commonly assumed crowding-out—effect of local government debt on private investment. The results highlight that the form in which public debt is held by banks, not merely its quantity, shapes its macroeconomic consequences.

## 4. Bilateral Economies of Scope
**Authors:** Yao Amber Li, Sichuang Xu, Stephen R. Yeaple, and Tengyu Zhao
**Date:** 2024 (Review of Economics and Statistics); featured on VoxChina January 15, 2025
**Source:** https://direct.mit.edu/rest/article-abstract/doi/10.1162/rest_a_01543/125408/ (DOI: 10.1162/rest_a_01543; NBER Working Paper No. 32803: https://www.nber.org/papers/w32803; VoxChina: https://voxchina.org/show-3-390.html)
**Abstract:** International transactions are costly because they require investments in logistics, contracting, and the acquisition of local institutional knowledge. This paper proposes and tests the hypothesis that a portion of the fixed cost of entering a specific export market can be applied toward covering the fixed cost of importing from that same country, and vice versa—what the authors term bilateral economies of scope. Using disaggregated firm-level transactions data for Chinese firms from 2000 to 2015, the paper documents systematic patterns in bilateral trading consistent with economies of scope: firms are disproportionately likely to both export to and import from the same foreign country, and this co-location in bilateral trade relationships is robust to controls for firm heterogeneity and country characteristics. Through a structural model of firm trade participation with endogenous bilateral scope economies, the authors estimate that simultaneously exporting to and importing from the same country reduces a firm's export fixed costs by approximately 42 percent and import fixed costs by approximately 35 percent. Policy simulations indicate that trade liberalization policies—including tariff reductions and improvements in trade facilitation—produce substantially amplified effects on trade volumes and welfare when these complementarities between exporting and importing are accounted for, suggesting that standard models understate the gains from trade liberalization.

## 5. Import Competition and the Rise of Precarious Employment: Evidence from Individual-Level and Firm-Level Data in China
**Authors:** Feicheng Wang, Zhe Liang, and Hartmut Lehmann
**Date:** December 2025 (Labour Economics, Vol. 97)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0927537125001277
**Abstract:** This paper investigates the effects of trade liberalization-induced labor demand shocks on precarious (informal) employment in China, contributing to the literature on the distributional consequences of globalization. Using a local labor market approach, the authors construct regional measures of exposure to import tariff cuts by exploiting initial differences in industrial composition across prefectural cities, an identification strategy analogous to the China Shock literature applied to the domestic Chinese labor market. Linking these exposure measures to three waves of household survey data between 1995 and 2007, the paper finds that workers in regions with larger tariff cuts were significantly more likely to be employed in informal, precarious arrangements—lacking contracts, social insurance, or employment protections. At the firm level, tariff reductions increased the share of informal workers within exposed firms, consistent with firms substituting toward flexible labor as import competition eroded profit margins and increased the demand for cost adjustment. The effects are heterogeneous: they are concentrated among smaller and less productive firms, which face tighter constraints on absorbing competitive pressure through productivity improvements. The findings document that China's WTO-era trade liberalization contributed to a rise in precarious employment by weakening labor demand in tariff-exposed regions and firms, highlighting a distributional cost of trade integration that operates through labor market informality rather than unemployment alone.
