# China Economics Papers — 2026-06-25

## 1. When Forward-Looking Accounting Meets an Unexpected Crisis: Does the Expected Credit Loss Model Amplify Economic Downturns?
**Authors:** Chen Chen (Monash University), Difang Huang (Chinese Academy of Sciences)
**Date:** June 24, 2026 (VoxChina); published *Journal of Accounting Research* 2026
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-679X.70030
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether the Expected Credit Loss (ECL) accounting model — introduced to make bank provisioning more forward-looking — can paradoxically amplify credit crunches during sudden crises. Using China's staggered ECL adoption and granular loan-level data from the COVID-19 period, the researchers show that ECL-adopting banks cut lending more sharply, raised spreads, and became more selective compared to incurred credit loss banks serving identical borrowers. Real consequences followed: firms with greater pre-pandemic ECL bank exposure experienced significant declines in loans, assets, and revenue, with persistent effects throughout the two-year observation period. The study concludes that "forward-looking provisioning improves screening and loan quality, but may need countercyclical safeguards to avoid tightening credit precisely when firms need it most."

## 2. AI Patents in the United States and China: Measurement, Organization, and Knowledge Flows
**Authors:** Hanming Fang (University of Pennsylvania), Xian Gu (Durham University), Hanyin Yan (Tsinghua University), Wu Zhu (Tsinghua University)
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35022); featured VoxChina June 16, 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35022
**Abstract:** This paper develops a high-precision AI patent classifier by fine-tuning PatentSBERTa on manually labeled data, achieving 97.0% precision, 91.3% recall, and a 94.0% F1 score. The authors analyze 876,668 U.S. AI patents (1976–2023) and 651,630 Chinese AI patents (2010–2023). They find that AI innovation in the two countries is converging in scale and technological direction but remains highly asymmetric in institutional organization: U.S. innovation is dominated by large private firms concentrated in established hubs, while Chinese AI patenting shows greater geographic dispersion and more diverse institutional participation, including universities and state enterprises. AI patents yield market-value premiums for listed companies in both countries. Cross-border citations indicate that Chinese AI patents rely heavily on U.S. frontier technologies, while U.S. dependence on Chinese innovation is limited, pointing to continued knowledge integration rather than technological decoupling.

## 3. Equity Financing and Exports: Evidence from IPO Approvals in China
**Authors:** Robin Kaiji Gong (HKUST), Yao Amber Li (HKUST), Stephen Teng Sun, Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** June 3, 2026 (VoxChina); conditionally accepted, *Journal of International Economics*
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5035361
**Abstract:** This paper provides the first empirical analysis of how access to equity financing affects firm exports. The authors leverage China's unique institutional setting in which IPOs required stringent regulatory approval, and compare the export performance of successful IPO applicants with near-misses — applicants rejected at the final IPO review meeting. IPO approval increased firm exports by over 6% annually over six years (a cumulative gain of approximately 37%), primarily through extensive margin expansion into new destination-product markets rather than increased sales in existing ones. Effects concentrate among firms with low pre-existing leverage and capacity for intangible investments. Approved firms disproportionately expanded into R&D-intensive and advertising-heavy products, suggesting equity capital finances activities that debt cannot support. The authors conclude that well-functioning equity markets are "a critical but overlooked ingredient for export-led growth" in developing economies.

## 4. Expanding Access or Reshaping Markets? Evidence from Public–Private Partnership Health Insurance in China
**Authors:** Hui Ding, Xintong Wang, Xian Xu
**Date:** May 26, 2026 (VoxChina); published *Journal of Development Economics* 182: 103780 (2026)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103780
**Abstract:** China's city-customized supplemental medical insurance (CCSMI) expanded coverage through public-private partnerships to over 300 million individuals. Despite this enrollment growth, the paper shows that CCSMI crowded out private insurance purchases at both the extensive and intensive margins: private insurance policies declined 13.7%, while among remaining purchasers, coverage amounts fell 13.6% and premiums declined 4.6%. About two-thirds of the premium decline reflects lower coverage choices, while the remainder reflects competitive price adjustment by insurers. Substitution between CCSMI and private insurance offsets roughly 25% of enrollment increases, indicating that "enrollment counts alone are an incomplete metric for evaluating insurance expansion." The authors argue that better PPP design requires clearer positioning by target risks or population groups and stronger coordination in benefit structure, reimbursement rules, and pricing.

## 5. Decoding China's Industrial Policies
**Authors:** Hanming Fang (University of Pennsylvania), Ming Li (CUHK-Shenzhen), Guangli Lu
**Date:** NBER Working Paper 33814 (May 2025); revised May 2026 (SSRN)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33814
**Abstract:** This paper decodes China's industrial policies from 2000 to 2022 by employing large language models to extract and analyze rich information from a comprehensive dataset of 3 million documents issued by central, provincial, and municipal governments. Through careful prompt engineering, multistage extraction and refinement, and rigorous verification, the authors classify industrial policy documents and extract structured information on policy objectives, targeted industries, policy tones (supportive or regulatory/suppressive), policy tools, implementation mechanisms, and intergovernmental relationships. By combining this dataset with firm-level data, the paper examines the political and economic foundations of industry targeting, how policy tools vary across levels of government and regions, and the impacts on firm behavior including entry, production, and productivity growth. The study documents the political economy of industrial policy — including top-down transmission, persistence, and regional diffusion — and identifies potential downsides such as spatial inefficiencies and industry-wide overcapacity.
