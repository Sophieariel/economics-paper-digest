# China Economics Papers — 2026-06-26

## 1. Empowering Inclusive Work
**Authors:** Yanyou Chen, Mitchell Hoffman, Huilan Xu, Zhe Yuan
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35372 — https://www.nber.org/papers/w35372
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether artificial intelligence can enhance workplace outcomes for workers with disabilities, specifically deaf or hard of hearing (DHH) workers on a major Chinese food-delivery platform. Initially, DHH workers demonstrated slower performance and lower customer ratings compared to non-disabled colleagues, though they contributed more hours and showed greater retention. The platform implemented an AI-based intelligent outbound calling system designed to improve customer communication for DHH workers. Using a difference-in-differences methodology, the researchers found that the AI tool substantially improved DHH worker performance: it increased their speed and productivity—particularly in customer-facing tasks—reduced negative customer ratings significantly, and boosted labor supply and worker retention. The analysis reveals that AI reduced the disability-related hourly pay gap by approximately one-third while substantially increasing DHH worker profitability for the platform.

## 2. From Trade War to Green Transition: Optimal Electric Vehicle Tariffs with Revenue-Funded Subsidies
**Authors:** Panle Jia Barwick, Jack Collison, Pinelopi K. Goldberg, Shanjun Li, Yucheng Wang
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35334 — https://www.nber.org/papers/w35334
**Abstract:** This paper examines how governments can structure trade and industrial policy to achieve environmental goals alongside national welfare objectives. The authors develop a framework where policymakers select tariffs and production subsidies to maximize welfare—encompassing consumer surplus, domestic profits, environmental benefits, and net tariff revenue. Their analysis combines oligopoly theory with demand modeling using vehicle-level data from 13 countries (2004–2023), with a central focus on the role of Chinese EV exports. The central finding indicates that the optimal policy combines a moderate tariff on imported EVs with a subsidy to domestic EV production financed through tariff revenue. This approach outperforms both strict protectionism and unregulated markets, maintaining consumer access to affordable vehicles while accelerating electrification and supporting domestic manufacturers in a budget-neutral manner. For the United States specifically, their simulations project the optimal policy would more than double EV market share, generate over $45 billion in annual welfare gains, and avoid approximately 95 million tons of lifetime CO2 emissions. The researchers conclude that effective industrial policy requires attention to market structure and country-specific conditions.

## 3. Intergenerational Mobility in Late Qing Dynasty: Evidence from Northeast China
**Authors:** Kristina Butaeva, Steven N. Durlauf, Alexander Shapoval
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35313 — https://www.nber.org/papers/w35313
**Abstract:** This paper examines intergenerational class mobility during China's late Qing dynasty using household register data from Liaoning province. The study identifies two distinct periods with sharply different mobility characteristics. Before 1850, mobility patterns exhibited stability and convergence toward a steady state. The period starting around 1850 showed unstable intergenerational class dynamics that persisted until the dynasty's collapse, coinciding with the Opium Wars and Taiping Rebellion. While overall intergenerational mobility increased across the full timeframe, the aggregate change obscured conflicting trends: upward mobility declined even as downward mobility rose. These disparate patterns align with theoretical explanations for political instability during this historical era and contribute to the literature on long-run income inequality and social mobility in China.

## 4. College Affirmative Action and Students' Outcomes: A Matter of the Dose
**Authors:** Yi Chen, Chao Fu, Hongbin Li, Teng Li, Siyan Tang
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35281 — https://www.nber.org/papers/w35281
**Abstract:** This paper examines how varying levels of affirmative action affect beneficiary outcomes by analyzing China's ethnic minority bonus-point policy in college admissions. The authors find that while higher affirmative action doses increase recipients' admissions to colleges and elite colleges, they simultaneously produce negative consequences: lower academic rankings, shifts toward less lucrative majors, and decreased college satisfaction. Most notably, longitudinal data spanning 10–23 years post-admission reveal a non-monotonic relationship: low AA doses increase long-term consumption by 19% but high doses reduce it by 25%. These effects extend across spending categories, including child-related expenses, potentially affecting future generations. The analysis demonstrates that students from stronger academic backgrounds better withstand the negative effects of excessive affirmative action, suggesting that careful policy calibration is essential for achieving the intended equity benefits. The findings have implications for the design of affirmative action policies and contribute to the literature on income inequality and intergenerational mobility in China.

## 5. Earnings Management and Price Informativeness
**Authors:** Zhiguo He, Wenxi Jiang, Wei Xiong
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper w35178 — https://www.nber.org/papers/w35178
**Abstract:** This paper examines how stock prices relate to subsequent earnings in China's A-share market. The findings reveal a paradox: while cross-sectional analysis shows higher valuations predicting stronger future earnings, time-series evidence demonstrates that elevated stock prices actually precede weaker earnings growth three to five years later. This pattern is consistent with short-term earnings management induced by stock-price pressures. The authors trace this mechanism through non-recurring gains and losses (NRGLs), using China's 2020 delisting rule reform as a natural experiment — the reform excluded NRGLs from delisting calculations, causing NRGL usage to fall and the valuation-earnings relationship to weaken after the reform. The findings contribute to the understanding of how market pressures distort corporate reporting behavior and reduce price informativeness in Chinese financial markets.
