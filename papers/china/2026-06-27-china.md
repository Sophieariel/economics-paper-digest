# China Economics Papers — 2026-06-27

## 1. From Heat to High-Tech: How Innovation Responds to Climate Change
**Authors:** Xianling Long (Renmin University of China), Zhiqiang Wang
**Date:** July 1, 2026 (VoxChina, scheduled); published *Journal of Development Economics* 176: 103525 (2025)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2025.103525
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how firms in China respond to rising temperatures through technological innovation. Using patent-level climate exposure measures that link products to geographic demand rather than firm location, the researchers find that heat exposure significantly increases both climate-adaptation patents (8.62%) and climate-mitigation patents (10.68%), with supply chain spillovers amplifying the innovation response four- to five-fold. Key mechanisms driving the results are citizen awareness reflected in online search behavior and household demand shifts, alongside government regulatory responses through tightened climate policies. The study shows that climate-driven innovation occurs not necessarily where firms operate but where their products are used, revealing how market signals and policy incentives jointly redirect technological progress toward lower-carbon solutions. The findings have implications for the design of climate policy in emerging economies where innovation capacity is rapidly developing.

## 2. China's Global Ownership
**Authors:** Jennie Bai (Georgetown University), Luc Laeven (ECB/CEPR), Yaojun Ke, Hong Ru (Nanyang Technological University)
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35106)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This paper assembles a micro-level dataset on Chinese corporate ownership across 161,773 firms in 159 countries from 2012 to 2021, reconstructing ownership chains through offshore entities to identify ultimate Chinese control. Chinese-controlled foreign assets expanded at 20% annually, reaching $2.1 trillion or roughly 3% of global corporate assets by 2021 — substantially exceeding official FDI figures. State-owned enterprises disproportionately target R&D-intensive and supply-chain-connected businesses. Following acquisition, target firms increase capital investment and R&D expenditures, yet fail to generate higher patent output and experience a significant decline in profitability. By contrast, Chinese parent companies undergo a sharp acceleration in granted patents after their first developed-economy acquisition — an "innovation spillback" mechanism. Increased Chinese ownership also reduces R&D activity at peer firms, while overall industry innovation remains stable, consistent with a state-driven model that prioritizes technology internalization and long-term strategic positioning over near-term financial returns.

## 3. Subsidising the Supply Chain: How China's Industrial Policy Shapes Export Competitiveness
**Authors:** Wenyin Cheng, David Liang, Bo Meng, Hongyong Zhang (RIETI)
**Date:** May 20, 2026 (VoxChina); RIETI Discussion Paper 25-E-119
**Source:** https://voxchina.org/show-3-458.html
**Abstract:** This paper examines how both direct subsidies and upstream industrial subsidies to input suppliers affect Chinese export performance. Using firm-level data from 1998–2013 combined with customs records and input-output tables, the authors find that subsidies propagate through domestic value chains in economically significant ways. Direct subsidies raise export volumes substantially, while upstream subsidies generate spillover effects for downstream exporters by lowering input costs and improving supplier quality. Importantly, subsidies are associated with quality upgrading rather than simple price competition: subsidized firms improve product quality while achieving lower quality-adjusted export prices. The results suggest that industrial policy in China operates through interconnected production networks, complicating trade remedies focused on individual firms by creating value-chain multiplier effects. The findings highlight that the competitive advantage generated by China's industrial policy reflects not just firm-level transfers but system-level cost and quality advantages embedded in supply chains.

## 4. The Geography of Market Power: Rethinking Consolidation in China's Steel Industry
**Authors:** Loren Brandt (University of Toronto), Feitao Jiang, Yao Luo, Yingjun Su
**Date:** May 6, 2026 (VoxChina); University of Toronto Department of Economics Working Paper No. 820
**Source:** https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/public/workingPapers/tecipa-820.pdf
**Abstract:** This study examines how geographic factors shape market power and the welfare consequences of consolidation in China's steel sector. Because steel is heavy and costly to transport, markets are largely regional, and the large mismatch between production concentration in Hebei province and demand concentration in the Yangtze River Delta creates distinctive competitive dynamics. Using a spatial equilibrium model calibrated with plant-level data, the authors find that regionally-focused mergers led by efficient producers would generate 24% welfare improvements and 4% price reductions. However, actual mergers executed between 2018 and 2024 captured only one-quarter of potential gains, suggesting that government policy prioritized national champion formation over consumer welfare optimization. The analysis highlights how institutional frictions, ownership restrictions on cross-regional consolidation, and spatial demand shifts jointly constrain the effectiveness of state-directed consolidation, with implications for industrial policy design in China's heavy industries more broadly.

## 5. From Free Rider to Innovator: The Rise of China's Drug Development
**Authors:** Panle Jia Barwick (University of Wisconsin–Madison), Hongyuan Xia, Tianli Xia
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper 34977)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34977
**Abstract:** This paper documents and explains China's remarkable pharmaceutical transformation over the past decade. China's clinical trial participation surged from less than 8% of global volume in 2010 to surpassing the US in registered annual clinical trial volume by 2020. The authors attribute this shift primarily to the National Reimbursement Drug List (NRDL) reform, which expanded market access and reimbursement for innovative medications, thereby increasing demand-side incentives for domestic drug development. Post-reform outcomes include an 86% increase in clinical trial quantity alongside improvements in novelty, concentrated among domestic pharmaceutical firms and first- or best-in-class drug candidates. A decomposition analysis reveals the NRDL reform accounted for 43% of oncology trial growth, substantially exceeding contributions from upstream knowledge gains and talent movements (24%). The authors conclude that the long-run innovation benefits induced by demand-side incentives exceed the reform's static gains in consumer access to innovative drugs by threefold, illustrating how market design reforms can catalyze innovation in sectors where China was previously a "free rider" on global R&D.
