# China Economics Papers — 2026-06-29

## 1. When Do Financial Frictions Matter for Misallocation?
**Authors:** Yan Bai (University of Rochester), Dan Lu (University of Rochester), Xu Tian (University of Rochester), Yajie Wang (University of Rochester)
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper 34930)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34930
**Abstract:** This paper reassesses when and how financial frictions generate capital misallocation, using a novel model estimated on Chinese manufacturing firm data. The authors document substantial dispersion in average revenue products of capital (ARPK) among Chinese manufacturers, alongside a strong positive correlation between ARPK and firm-level borrowing costs — a pattern sharply at odds with the U.S. evidence, where high-ARPK firms generally face lower financing costs. The paper develops a heterogeneous-firm model with endogenous borrowing costs to separately identify the role of financial frictions versus permanent firm-level distortions (e.g., ownership-based discrimination, regulatory wedges). The central finding is that the productivity effects of financial frictions depend critically on the coexistence of other distortions: eliminating financial frictions alone raises total factor productivity by 25 percentage points when permanent distortions are present, because relaxing credit constraints allows high-productivity firms to grow despite the selection of productive firms being systematically impaired. In the absence of such permanent distortions, however, eliminating financial frictions yields TFP gains under 2 percentage points. The mechanism operates through selection: permanent distortions generate a pool of constrained, high-productivity firms for whom financial frictions are most binding. The results clarify why China's manufacturing sector exhibits such large measured misallocation, and suggest that reform packages addressing only credit market imperfections while leaving ownership and regulatory distortions in place would generate modest aggregate benefits.

## 2. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes
**Authors:** Chang Ma (Fudan University), Shang-Jin Wei (Columbia University)
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35056)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056
**Abstract:** China has maintained persistent and large current account surpluses for over two decades, yet standard trade policy explanations — sector-specific tariffs, export subsidies, or bilateral interventions — account for only a small portion of economy-wide imbalance. This paper documents the puzzles and patterns in China's external surpluses and proposes structural explanations. The authors identify two key structural drivers: skewed gender demographics arising from the one-child policy and its interaction with household saving behavior, and the dual-track financing system that systematically disadvantages non-state enterprises in accessing credit. The former amplifies precautionary saving among households concerned with marriage-market competition; the latter drives corporate saving by firms unable to externally finance profitable investment. The paper evaluates a range of short-run and structural policy responses, finding that stimulus spending can temporarily reduce the surplus by boosting imports, but durable adjustment requires reforms targeting the underlying structural imbalances — particularly credit market reform to reduce the financing advantage of state enterprises. The analysis has implications for understanding trade tensions with China: addressing bilateral trade deficits without correcting the structural macroeconomic imbalances that generate China's surplus is likely to produce substitution rather than resolution.

## 3. A Few Bad Apples? Academic Dishonesty, Political Selection, and Institutional Performance in China
**Authors:** Zhuang Liu (University of British Columbia), Wenwei Peng (Johns Hopkins University), Shaoda Wang (University of Chicago)
**Date:** January 2026 (NBER Working Paper 34747)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34747
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether academic misconduct during graduate education predicts subsequent behavioral integrity in professional settings, with applications to political selection and judicial performance in China. The authors deploy plagiarism-detection algorithms across approximately 500,000 graduate-level dissertations drawn from China's National Knowledge Infrastructure database, producing individual-level measures of academic dishonesty. Linking these measures to administrative records on career trajectories, the paper documents that individuals with plagiarism histories are more likely to advance into government positions, suggesting that selection mechanisms in China's cadre system do not screen effectively against academic misconduct — or may reward related traits. Examining judicial performance, judges with plagiarism histories in their academic records issue systematically more preferential rulings in favor of connected parties and attract a significantly greater number of appellate reversals than their clean-record counterparts. The paper identifies positive spillovers on colleagues — exposure to dishonest peers reduces institutional integrity — and shows that implementing plagiarism-detection systems produces modest improvements in integrity among future practitioners. The results contribute to the political economy of China by documenting how initial screening failures in personnel selection compound through career networks, generating measurable losses in institutional quality, and speak more broadly to the design of cadre selection systems in one-party states.

## 4. The Ins & Outs of Chinese Monetary Policy Transmission
**Authors:** Silvia Miranda-Agrippino (Federal Reserve Bank of New York), Tsvetelina Nenova (London Business School), Hélène Rey (London Business School; NBER)
**Date:** January 2026 (NBER Working Paper 34626)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34626
**Abstract:** This paper constructs a novel high-frequency indicator of People's Bank of China (PBoC) monetary policy shocks and uses it to trace both domestic transmission and international spillovers. The identification strategy accounts for the PBoC's dual mandate — targeting domestic inflation alongside the renminbi exchange rate — and models structural breaks associated with operational framework changes including the transition from quantity- to price-based instruments. Domestically, monetary transmission follows conventional patterns: a contractionary surprise raises borrowing costs, depresses investment and output, and reduces inflation, with the transmission mechanism broadly consistent with theory. Two important exceptions arise: renminbi management generates deviations from uncovered interest parity and creates exchange rate dynamics that partly offset domestic tightening, while China's partially closed capital account limits financial channel pass-through. The international effects of Chinese monetary policy are substantial. A contractionary PBoC shock depresses global commodity prices, reduces world trade volumes and manufacturing output, and generates significant spillovers to commodity-exporting economies. Pass-through to U.S. prices is economically meaningful, operating primarily through trade and input cost channels rather than through financial markets. The results underscore that Chinese monetary policy has become a first-order determinant of global macroeconomic conditions alongside Fed policy, with implications for the coordination of monetary policy internationally.

## 5. American Investment in Chinese Renminbi
**Authors:** Bruno Cavani (Columbia University), Christopher Clayton (Yale University), Amanda Dos Santos (Columbia University), Matteo Maggiori (Stanford University), Jesse Schreger (Columbia University)
**Date:** February 2026 (NBER Working Paper 34792)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34792
**Abstract:** This paper uses high-frequency, security-level data from SEC Form NPORT filings to document U.S. mutual fund and ETF investment in Chinese renminbi-denominated bonds from 2019 to 2024. The data provide an unusually precise view of American institutional portfolios' exposure to RMB assets over a period of significant geopolitical and financial turbulence. The central finding is that even as total foreign holdings of Chinese bonds recovered to near-record levels in 2024 — driven by other investor categories — U.S. holdings of RMB-denominated bonds fell sharply and persistently, with many funds exiting their positions entirely rather than reducing them partially. This bifurcation points to a compositional shift in China's foreign investor base: non-U.S. investors, particularly those in jurisdictions with different geopolitical exposures, absorbed the supply that American institutions shed. The paper explores potential drivers of this divergence including sanctions risk, fiduciary concerns, and political economy pressures on U.S. institutional investors. The results suggest that financial decoupling between the United States and China is already measurable in portfolio-level data, and that the renminbi's path toward greater international use is likely to be shaped by a geographically segmented investor base rather than uniform global adoption — with implications for the stability of Chinese sovereign bond markets and the internationalization of the RMB.
