# China Economics Papers — 2026-07-01

## 1. Empowering Inclusive Work
**Authors:** Yanyou Chen, Mitchell Hoffman, Huilan Xu, Zhe Yuan
**Date:** June 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35372)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35372
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether AI-assisted technology can improve labor market outcomes for workers with disabilities, using the experience of deaf or hard of hearing (DHH) workers on one of China's largest food-delivery platforms. The authors implement a difference-in-differences design comparing DHH and non-disabled workers before and after the platform introduces an AI-based calling system that mediates customer communication. The intervention effectively removes the primary communication barrier facing DHH couriers by converting phone calls to text-based interaction, without requiring any change in customer behavior. The findings show that access to the AI calling system improves DHH workers' delivery speed, reduces their incidence of negative customer ratings, and increases both labor supply and retention on the platform. Most strikingly, the technology eliminates approximately one-third of the disability hourly pay gap between DHH and non-disabled couriers. The results demonstrate that targeted AI tools can meaningfully reduce labor market disadvantages arising from disability, with implications for the design of workplace accommodations and platform regulation in China's rapidly expanding gig economy.

## 2. College Affirmative Action and Students' Outcomes: A Matter of the Dose
**Authors:** Yi Chen, Chao Fu, Hongbin Li, Teng Li, Siyan Tang
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35281)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35281
**Abstract:** This paper examines how the intensity of affirmative action affects the long-run outcomes of its recipients, using China's college admissions system as an empirical setting. China's ethnic minority bonus-point (jia fen) policy grants explicit score bonuses to minority applicants in the national college entrance examination (gaokao), creating a continuous policy instrument that varies across provinces, years, and applicants. The authors exploit fluctuations in exam score distributions that generate exogenous variation in effective AA intensity — the degree to which a bonus shifts a student into a higher-quality institution or major — to identify the causal effect of AA dose on educational and labor market outcomes. They find that higher AA doses increase recipients' probability of admission to colleges and elite colleges, but at the cost of lower academic rank within their entering cohort and reduced major satisfaction. Long-run effects reveal a pronounced non-linearity: modest AA doses correlate with approximately 19 percent higher consumption a decade after college, while large doses are associated with 25 percent lower consumption relative to slightly lower doses, with effects spanning multiple expenditure categories including child-related spending. Heterogeneity analyses show that students with stronger underlying academic preparation are more resilient to the negative consequences of large dose mismatches. The paper concludes that careful calibration of AA policies is crucial for achieving their intended equity goals, since the same mechanism that provides access can generate mismatch costs that undermine long-run welfare.

## 3. China's Global Ownership
**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, Hong Ru
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35106)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This paper documents and analyzes the global expansion of Chinese corporate ownership using a comprehensive dataset of 161,773 firms across 159 countries spanning 2012–2021. The authors reconstruct full ownership chains to identify the ultimate capital origin of each firm, revealing that Chinese-controlled foreign assets expanded at approximately 20 percent annually, reaching $2.1 trillion by 2021. State-owned enterprises (SOEs) account for a disproportionate share of this expansion and strategically target firms in research-intensive industries and supply-chain-linked sectors. Following acquisition, target firms increase their capital stock and R&D expenditures, but these investments do not generate commensurately higher patent output, and target profitability declines significantly post-acquisition. In contrast, the Chinese acquiring firms — particularly SOEs — accelerate their own domestic patent generation after completing initial acquisitions in developed economies, a mechanism the authors term "innovation spillback." The paper also documents negative competitive spillovers: increased Chinese ownership in a sector suppresses R&D at peer firms, though aggregate industry-level innovation remains broadly stable due to the reallocation of activity. The overall pattern characterizes China's cross-border ownership expansion as a state-directed strategy prioritizing long-run technological capacity accumulation in China itself over immediate performance gains in target firms, with implications for host-country industrial and technology policy.

## 4. When Do Financial Frictions Matter for Misallocation?
**Authors:** Yan Bai, Dan Lu, Xu Tian, Yajie Wang
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34930)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34930
**Abstract:** This paper examines the conditions under which financial frictions generate economically meaningful capital misallocation, using Chinese manufacturing firm data as the primary empirical context. The authors begin by documenting two distinctive features of the Chinese data: substantial dispersion in the average revenue product of capital (ARPK) across firms, and a strong positive relationship between ARPK and firm-level borrowing costs — a pattern strikingly absent in comparable U.S. firm-level data. To interpret these facts, the paper develops a heterogeneous-firm general equilibrium model that incorporates endogenous borrowing costs alongside permanent firm-specific distortions. The model's central finding is that financial frictions in isolation raise aggregate total factor productivity by approximately 25 percentage points when they are the only source of misallocation; however, when introduced into an economy where other firm-level distortions are absent, the productivity gain from eliminating financial frictions falls below 2 percentage points. This dramatic divergence arises because the interaction between financial constraints and pre-existing permanent distortions creates a pattern in which the most productive firms face the tightest financing constraints, amplifying misallocation far beyond what either friction type generates alone. The results imply that the large TFP gains commonly attributed to financial development in China depend critically on whether complementary reforms addressing underlying firm-level distortions are undertaken simultaneously, and that partial-equilibrium assessments of financial reform will systematically overstate the benefits of financial liberalization in isolation.

## 5. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy
**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34893)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893
**Abstract:** This paper studies whether industrial policy targeting clean energy sectors can simultaneously promote economic growth and reduce carbon emissions, using China's solar energy subsidies as a large-scale natural experiment. The authors analyze production, demand, and innovation subsidies implemented across Chinese city-regions from 2004 to 2020, a period during which the global solar sector expanded dramatically and solar panel prices fell by more than 90 percent. Using a synthetic difference-in-differences methodology applied to newly constructed city-level and firm-level datasets, the paper finds that production and innovation subsidies generated large and persistent increases in local innovation, net firm entry, manufacturing output, and exports, while demand-side installation subsidies had comparatively modest effects. Supply-side policies also produced significant reductions in local air pollution. To assess aggregate and global implications, the authors develop a structural spatial model incorporating endogenous innovation, firm heterogeneity, and knowledge spillovers. Counterfactual simulations reveal that local subsidy effects account for 40–50 percent of the aggregate changes observed in China's solar sector over the sample period. A social cost-benefit analysis finds that social benefits exceed subsidy costs by approximately 65 percent when evaluated at standard carbon prices, rising to more than double the cost when environmental co-benefits from pollution reduction are included. Innovation-focused subsidies are identified as the most cost-effective instrument. The results provide the first rigorous large-scale evaluation of China's clean energy industrial policy and offer evidence on how targeted production and innovation subsidies can accelerate the global green transition.
