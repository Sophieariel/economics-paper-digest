# China Economics Papers — 2026-07-02

## 1. Household Migration and Collateral Constraint: Cash-based Housing Resettlement in China
**Authors:** Zhiguo He (University of Chicago; NBER), Zehao Liu, Xinle Pang, Yang Su, Kunru Zou
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34982)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34982
**Abstract:** This paper examines how binding collateral constraints restrict household relocation to high-cost metropolitan areas by limiting access to home-purchase financing. The authors leverage China's shantytown renovation initiative (2015–2018) as a quasi-experimental setting in which some displaced residents received cash compensation rather than in-kind replacement housing. This relaxation of liquidity constraints enabled households to upgrade their residential locations, driving up property values in destination markets and generating persistent spillovers through migration networks: cities receiving cash inflows experienced robust and sustained housing price appreciation. The study documents two distinct migration channels through which cash transfers amplify housing expenditures: existing urban migrants in expensive cities used origin-city cash transfers to purchase homes locally and anchor themselves in high-rent markets, while approximately 22% of cash recipients migrated to new cities in search of employment. A calibrated dynamic spatial equilibrium model incorporating collateral constraints corroborates the observed migration patterns and implies that endogenous relocation responses magnify household housing expenditure by roughly 40% relative to scenarios in which spending is voucher-restricted. The program accounts for over 20% of documented housing price appreciation during 2016–2020, and the results speak directly to how financial and housing policies interact with household location decisions and fiscal inequality across Chinese cities.

## 2. Contract Design and Insurance Demand
**Authors:** Jing Cai (University of Maryland; NBER)
**Date:** February 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34797)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34797
**Abstract:** This paper studies how insurance contract design influences farmer participation through field experiments with rice farmers in China. The key intervention compares offering a single standardized contract against providing a menu of multiple options with varying premium levels and payouts. Results show that expanding the contract menu substantially increases take-up—raising insurance adoption from roughly 30% to 50%—with demand concentrated in the basic, lowest-cost option. To disentangle the mechanisms, the paper tests relative pricing and information delivery separately and finds that context effects arising from menu comparisons, rather than improved information interpretation, drive the adoption increase. Participants are more likely to purchase coverage when the menu makes the baseline option appear attractively priced relative to more expensive alternatives, consistent with reference-point effects and relative valuation rather than absolute willingness to pay. The paper further demonstrates that menu design interacts with contract attractiveness in predictable ways: offering a dominated option alongside a focal contract can steer demand, and the same insurance product achieves different take-up rates depending on the alternatives presented alongside it. The findings challenge single-contract thinking in agricultural insurance design and show that menu architecture is a powerful and underexploited tool for increasing coverage among smallholder farmers in developing economies.

## 3. Strategic Emissions in the Rain
**Authors:** Runhao Zhao (Peking University), Ye Yuan (Renmin University of China)
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 181, Article 103731)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103731
**Abstract:** This paper documents a novel "polluting-in-the-rain" phenomenon in which Chinese industrial firms strategically time the release of untreated wastewater to coincide with heavy rainfall events, exploiting natural dilution effects to evade water quality concentration standards. Using discharge monitoring data from China's national surface-water quality network merged with precipitation records and industrial production data, the authors show that wastewater discharge is significantly elevated at upstream industrial sources during and immediately following rainfall, with effects concentrated at monitoring stations downstream from facilities subject to threshold-based regulations. The evasion strategy operates through a "store-then-discharge" mechanism: firms accumulate untreated effluent during dry periods and release it when rainfall provides a natural dilution buffer that prevents downstream concentrations from triggering regulatory responses. The study estimates approximately 75,000 tons of annual chemical oxygen demand (COD) emissions attributable to strategic rain-day discharge, which offsets roughly 43% of China's national industrial pollution reduction targets and substantially overstates the effectiveness of aggregate abatement efforts. The paper demonstrates that threshold-based monitoring systems are structurally vulnerable to this form of temporal evasion and recommends deployment of real-time, continuous end-of-pipe monitoring at emission sources as a cost-effective countermeasure. The findings contribute to a broader literature on strategic regulatory evasion and have direct implications for the design of environmental enforcement systems in economies where monitoring is infrequent or geographically sparse.

## 4. Information Acquisition By Mutual Fund Investors: Evidence from Stock Trading Suspensions
**Authors:** Clemens Sialm (University of Texas at Austin; NBER), David X. Xu (Southern Methodist University)
**Date:** November 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 34520)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34520
**Abstract:** This paper examines how mutual funds' liquidity creation affects information acquisition by investors, using the distinctive institutional setting of China's equity markets where trading suspensions are widespread and affect approximately 80% of listed firms at some point. When a stock is suspended, mutual funds holding the suspended shares cannot mark their positions to market, creating significant net asset value (NAV) mispricings that generate exploitable opportunities for sophisticated investors. The authors document that investors actively acquire information about suspended stocks held by mutual funds, driving abnormal inflows into funds holding underpriced suspended securities. This information acquisition is reflected in measurable increases in search activity, analyst coverage, and other proxies for research effort directed at affected firms. When trading eventually resumes, stock prices exhibit large, firm-specific price movements that are predictable from pre-resumption fund flows, confirming that the information acquired during the suspension period is subsequently incorporated into market prices. The results establish that the liquidity transformation services provided by mutual funds—converting illiquid positions into redeemable shares—create incentives for investors to engage in costly information production about illiquid, information-sensitive assets. The Chinese trading suspension setting provides unusually clean identification for this mechanism because suspensions are plausibly exogenous to investor information-acquisition incentives and are far more frequent than in other markets.

## 5. Sex and the City: Demographics, Spatial Sorting, and the Marriage Market
**Authors:** Min Fang (Peking University), Zibin Huang (Chinese University of Hong Kong), Yushi Wang, Yu Alan Yang
**Date:** June 2025 (SSRN Working Paper No. 5327630)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5327630
**Abstract:** This paper studies how the interaction between demographic changes and spatial sorting affects marriage matching across space and quantifies the aggregate implications for national marriage rates in China. The authors document a set of stylized facts on the joint patterns of dramatic gender-biased spatial demographic changes, persistent marital social norms, and the diverging spatial distribution of singlehood characterized by a high singles rate for females (males) in more (less) developed cities. Women have increasingly out-educated men and sort disproportionately into service-sector employment in large, more-developed cities, while the persistent social norm of female hypergamy—the preference to marry a partner of equal or higher status—generates an intensive spatial asymmetry: highly educated women in developed cities face a shortage of comparable male partners, while less-educated men in underdeveloped regions face a corresponding deficit of female partners. To quantify the aggregate implications, the authors build a prefecture-level spatial equilibrium model with multi-sector and multi-skill production, endogenous migration, and local marriage markets. The model reveals that, without gender-specific spatial demographic changes, the national singles rate would be 30% lower for women on average and over 50% lower for college-educated women. The paper also demonstrates that marriage subsidies prove ineffective because they address affordability constraints rather than the underlying geographic mismatch problem, and projects that spatial sorting-driven singlehood will intensify through 2030. The findings contribute to the literature on declining marriage rates in East Asia and speak to the intersection of urbanization, labor market sorting, and household formation in developing economies.
