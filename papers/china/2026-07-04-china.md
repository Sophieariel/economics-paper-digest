# China Economics Papers — 2026-07-04

## 1. Trade and Industrial Policy in Supply Chains: Directed Technological Change in Rare Earths
**Authors:** Laura Alfaro (Harvard University; NBER), Harald Fadinger (University of Vienna), Jan S. Schymik, Gede Virananda
**Date:** May 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 33877; revised March 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33877
**Abstract:** This paper examines how trade restrictions on critical supply-chain inputs can paradoxically stimulate foreign technological innovation, using China's 2010 restrictions on rare earth element (REE) exports as the empirical setting. China's REE export curbs, which targeted materials essential for high-technology manufacturing, triggered a surge in global innovation aimed at increasing REE input-efficiency among overseas manufacturers. The authors develop a quantitative trade model that incorporates comparative advantage, endogenous technological development, and supply chain interdependencies to trace the consequences of input protectionism through the innovation channel. The central finding is that China's export restrictions induced substantial directed technological change abroad: firms in affected industries invested significantly in developing production methods that economize on REE inputs, and this innovation response substantially mitigated the foreign welfare losses that would otherwise have resulted from supply constraints. The results illustrate a general mechanism through which protectionist policies on critical inputs may inadvertently accelerate the development of the very technological capabilities they sought to disadvantage — a dynamic with implications for ongoing debates about export controls on strategic materials and the design of industrial policy in supply chains.

## 2. China's Economic Slowdown: A Firm-Level Perspective
**Authors:** Loren Brandt (University of Toronto), Ruochen Dai, Gueorgui Kambourov (University of Toronto), Zhenjie Qian, Kjetil Storesletten (University of Minnesota), Luhang Wang
**Date:** June 2026 (presented at NBER East Asian Seminar on Economics, June 7, 2026, Tsinghua University)
**Source:** https://conference.nber.org/agenda/simple_printable?conf_id=EASE26
**Abstract:** This paper provides a firm-level decomposition of China's economic slowdown, using comprehensive administrative panel data covering the near-universe of Chinese firms across all sectors and ownership types. The authors document a pronounced deceleration in aggregate productivity and output growth beginning in the mid-2010s and extending through the recent period, and decompose the slowdown into contributions from declining incumbent firm productivity, weakening entry and exit dynamics, and diminished allocative efficiency. A central finding is that the loss of economic dynamism is not uniform across the firm population: the reduction in firm entry, particularly the entry of high-productivity private-sector firms, accounts for a disproportionate share of aggregate growth deceleration relative to changes in incumbent performance. State-sector expansion and tightening regulatory entry barriers appear to crowd out the private-sector dynamism that drove earlier growth phases. The paper connects to the team's broader research program on barriers to entry, serial entrepreneurship, and productivity dynamics in China, providing a unified empirical account of how firm-level forces have shaped the trajectory of China's aggregate economic performance over the transition period and into the current slowdown.

## 3. Industrial Policies in China: The Role of State Capital
**Authors:** Chong-En Bai (Tsinghua University), Futian Song, Binzhen Wu (Tsinghua University)
**Date:** June 2026 (presented at NBER East Asian Seminar on Economics, June 7, 2026, Tsinghua University)
**Source:** https://conference.nber.org/agenda/simple_printable?conf_id=EASE26
**Abstract:** This paper examines how state capital — encompassing government-backed equity investment funds, state-owned enterprise investment in private firms, and government guidance funds — shapes the design and effectiveness of China's industrial policy. China has deployed trillions of yuan through state capital instruments to target strategic industries, making state equity participation a central tool of contemporary industrial policy alongside traditional subsidies and regulatory protection. The authors combine administrative records on state capital flows with firm-level data to assess whether state equity investment enhances or distorts industrial policy targeting, and to measure downstream effects on firm investment, innovation, and productivity. The analysis exploits variation in the timing and sector allocation of state capital deployments across regions and industries to identify causal effects. The paper documents that state capital instruments significantly influence firm behavior, but that the effects are heterogeneous: firms receiving state capital in genuinely competitive technology sectors show improved innovation and survival outcomes, while state capital allocated to declining industries generates resource misallocation and delays market exit. The results have implications for evaluating China's evolving industrial policy toolkit and for understanding the welfare consequences of state equity participation as an instrument of strategic policy.

## 4. Super Factory Comes to Town: Quantifying the Agglomeration Spillovers
**Authors:** Jianpeng Deng, Hong Ma (Tsinghua University), Hongbing Wei
**Date:** June 2026 (presented at NBER East Asian Seminar on Economics, June 7, 2026, Tsinghua University)
**Source:** https://conference.nber.org/agenda/simple_printable?conf_id=EASE26
**Abstract:** This paper quantifies the agglomeration spillovers generated by the opening of "super factories" — the massive manufacturing facilities that have become emblematic of China's industrial scale, including gigafactories for electric vehicle batteries and solar panels, large-scale semiconductor fabs, and other facilities employing tens of thousands of workers. Using a quasi-experimental design that exploits variation in the timing and location of super factory openings across Chinese cities and counties, the authors estimate the causal effects of these large plant openings on surrounding firms, workers, and local economies. The analysis documents substantial positive spillovers operating through multiple channels: input-output linkages that generate demand for local suppliers; labor market pooling that improves worker-firm matching in nearby industries; and knowledge externalities that increase local firm productivity and patent activity. The magnitude of spillovers varies with the industry distance between the super factory and local incumbents — vertically linked suppliers benefit disproportionately — and decays sharply with geographic distance. Negative competition and congestion effects are concentrated among firms in directly competing product markets. Quantitative analysis translates the estimated spillover magnitudes into aggregate welfare assessments, finding that the social returns to super factory openings substantially exceed their private returns and direct subsidy costs, suggesting significant underinvestment from a social planning perspective.

## 5. How Do Gig Workers Respond to Unanticipated Income Shocks? Within-Shift Evidence from Food Delivery Riders in China
**Authors:** Dandan Zhang (Peking University), Yuxiao Fu
**Date:** June 2026 (presented at NBER East Asian Seminar on Economics, June 8, 2026, Tsinghua University)
**Source:** https://conference.nber.org/agenda/simple_printable?conf_id=EASE26
**Abstract:** This paper uses high-frequency administrative data from a major Chinese food delivery platform — covering the complete within-shift order histories of a large sample of riders — to study how gig workers adjust labor supply in response to unanticipated income shocks occurring during a shift. China's food delivery industry, with a workforce exceeding 13 million riders, provides an unusually rich setting for studying behavioral labor supply because platform data record earnings and work effort at the order level, enabling the authors to identify responses to realized income relative to expected earnings benchmarks with high precision. The central empirical challenge is separating income effects — which should reduce effort after positive shocks under neoclassical preferences — from reference-dependent behavior, wherein workers target a daily income level and reduce effort upon hitting that target regardless of hours worked. Exploiting algorithmic variation in platform-assigned order characteristics that generate plausibly exogenous within-shift earnings surprises, the authors document that Chinese food delivery riders exhibit reference-dependent labor supply: positive unanticipated income shocks significantly increase the probability of early shift termination, and the response is concentrated around earnings that cross salient daily income thresholds. Riders with lower baseline earnings and weaker platform tenure show stronger reference-point behavior. The findings contribute to the behavioral labor economics literature and carry implications for platform design, rider welfare, and the efficiency properties of gig economy labor markets in China.
