## 1. Expected Insurance Coverage and Pharmaceutical Innovation: Evidence from China's National Drug Price Negotiation Policy
**Authors:** Chenyuan Liu (Tsinghua University), Yi Lu, Ronghe Sun, Wanyu Yang
**Date:** June 2026 (presented at NBER East Asian Seminar on Economics, June 8, 2026, Tsinghua University)
**Source:** https://conference.nber.org/agenda/simple_printable?conf_id=EASE26
**Abstract:** This paper studies how developing countries can promote pharmaceutical innovation when centralized drug price negotiations risk eroding firms' incentives to invest in R&D. China's National Drug Price Negotiation policy pairs substantial price cuts with expanded public insurance coverage for newly approved innovative drugs, effectively enlarging the market for covered products. The authors exploit this policy design as a natural experiment, using a difference-in-differences approach that compares clinical trial activity for new drugs (treated) against vaccines (untreated) within the same disease categories, before and after the policy's introduction. The key finding is that the policy significantly stimulates pharmaceutical innovation: inclusion in the negotiation process increases the number of clinical trials by approximately 0.56 per disease per year. The results suggest that market-size expansion through insurance coverage can offset the innovation-dampening effect of negotiated price reductions, providing a model for how governments in lower-income settings might balance affordability goals with incentives for new drug development.

## 2. When Do Financial Frictions Matter for Misallocation?
**Authors:** Yan Bai, Dan Lu, Xu Tian, Yajie Wang
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34930)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34930
**Abstract:** This paper reassesses how financial frictions contribute to capital misallocation in China, using a model disciplined by both firm-level borrowing costs and the average revenue product of capital (ARPK). Analyzing Chinese manufacturing firm data, the authors document substantial dispersion in ARPK alongside a strong positive relationship between ARPK and the borrowing costs firms face — a pattern notably absent in comparable U.S. data. They build a heterogeneous-firm model that incorporates endogenous borrowing costs alongside exogenous capital distortions (permanent wedges). Counterfactual analysis reveals a striking interaction: eliminating financial frictions alone raises total factor productivity (TFP) by 25 percentage points, but when other capital distortions are simultaneously removed, the marginal TFP gain from eliminating financial frictions falls to less than 2 percentage points. The key mechanism is that financial frictions are most distortionary when they interact with other sources of misallocation — specifically, when productive firms face both high capital wedges and high borrowing costs. The findings imply that financial reform in isolation may produce large measured productivity gains, but the welfare case depends critically on the coexistence of other distortions in the economy.

## 3. China's Global Ownership
**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, Hong Ru
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35106)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This paper studies the global footprint and real effects of Chinese overseas corporate ownership using a comprehensive micro-level dataset of 161,773 firms across 159 countries from 2012 to 2021. The authors reconstruct multi-layered ownership chains — tracing capital flows through offshore tax havens — to identify each firm's ultimate beneficial owner. Chinese-controlled foreign assets expanded at approximately 20% annually over the sample period, reaching $2.1 trillion, or roughly 3% of global corporate assets, by 2021. Chinese investors — particularly state-owned enterprises (SOEs) — strategically target R&D-intensive firms and those embedded in global supply chains. Following Chinese acquisition, target firms increase both capital investment and R&D expenditure, yet these additional inputs fail to generate higher patent output and are accompanied by a significant decline in profitability. A notable finding is an "innovation spillback" channel: while acquired firms' own innovation stagnates, their Chinese parent companies experience accelerated patent growth following acquisitions of developed-economy targets. Chinese ownership also crowds out R&D investment among peer firms, though industry-level aggregate innovation remains stable. The paper characterizes China's cross-border ownership strategy as state-driven technology internalization, prioritizing the transfer of knowledge to domestic parents over near-term financial returns in the target firms.

## 4. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes
**Authors:** Chang Ma, Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35056)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056
**Abstract:** This paper examines why China maintains a persistent and substantial current account surplus despite sustained pressure from trading partners and ongoing debate about its root causes. The authors evaluate a wide range of explanations — spanning industrial and trade policies, demographic factors, financial market structure, and macroeconomic conditions — using a combination of cross-country panel analysis and China-specific structural estimation. A central finding is that while sector-level industrial and trade policies generate localized imbalances in specific industries, they have minimal impact on the aggregate economy-wide current account surplus. In contrast, structural features of the Chinese economy prove far more important: an unbalanced sex ratio — which intensifies household saving through precautionary and marriage-market motives — and unequal access to external financing between state-owned enterprises and private non-state firms collectively account for a significant share of the observed surplus. Short-term macroeconomic stimulus can temporarily increase imports and reduce the surplus, but the authors conclude that any durable resolution requires addressing these underlying structural distortions. The findings have implications both for China's domestic policy reform agenda and for ongoing international negotiations over global current account imbalances.

## 5. A Few Bad Apples? Academic Dishonesty, Political Selection, and Institutional Performance in China
**Authors:** Zhuang Liu, Wenwei Peng, Shaoda Wang
**Date:** January 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34747)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34747
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether academic dishonesty during schooling has lasting effects on career trajectories and professional conduct, focusing on China's public sector. The authors apply plagiarism-detection algorithms to approximately 500,000 publicly available Chinese graduate dissertations, identifying misconduct at scale and validating the measure against incentivized behavioral tests of honesty in a subsample of subjects. The empirical analysis documents four central findings. First, plagiarists disproportionately enter government careers and advance more rapidly within the public sector than their peers, suggesting that the political selection process does not screen effectively for integrity. Second, judges with plagiarism histories in their dissertations perform significantly worse in office: they issue more preferential rulings and face higher rates of appeal and reversal. Third, dishonest judges generate negative spillovers onto colleagues and other legal professionals with whom they interact, degrading institutional performance beyond their own direct decisions. Fourth, strengthening academic integrity enforcement — as occurred through policy changes affecting dissertation oversight — produces modest but statistically significant improvements in the future professional conduct of affected cohorts. The paper provides rare micro-level evidence linking pre-career behavior to governance quality, with implications for the design of civil service selection mechanisms and anti-corruption policy in developing economies.
