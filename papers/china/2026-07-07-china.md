# China Economics Papers — 2026-07-07

## 1. Preferential Credit Policy with Sectoral Markup Heterogeneity
**Authors:** Kaiji Chen (Emory University), Yuxuan Huang, Xuewen Liu, Zhikun Lu, Yong Wang
**Date:** July 7, 2026 (Journal of International Economics, Volume 161, Article 104238)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jinteco.2026.104238 (VoxChina: https://www.voxchina.org/show-3-467.html)
**Abstract:** Many emerging economies employ preferential credit policies that target selected sectors. This paper quantifies the implications of such policies for aggregate productivity and welfare. Using Chinese firm-level data from 2009–2020, the authors first document that sectors with higher markups receive larger credit subsidies and exhibit higher revenue-based total factor productivity — a pattern suggesting that credit subsidies are directed toward high-return activities that may be underfunded in market equilibrium. Motivated by these facts, the paper develops a multi-sector quantitative model with endogenously determined markups and calibrates it to match the distribution of sales both within and across sectors. The central finding is that preferential credit subsidies raise aggregate productivity and welfare by reallocating market shares toward high-markup sectors. These gains persist in an extended framework with endogenous firm entry, indicating that static reallocation benefits are not negated by dynamic firm-selection distortions. The results imply that, in a second-best environment where credit market imperfections generate systematic underfinancing of high-return sectors, well-targeted industrial policies can enhance rather than reduce overall efficiency. Counterfactual analysis indicates that removing preferential credit policies would nearly double productivity losses, underscoring their aggregate importance in the current Chinese financial system. The paper contributes to ongoing debates about whether state-directed credit in China distorts or corrects underlying market failures, providing quantitative evidence that the net efficiency effect of observed credit subsidies is positive under empirically calibrated parameters.

## 2. Policy Experimentation in China: The Political Economy of Policy Learning
**Authors:** Shaoda Wang (University of Chicago), David Y. Yang (Harvard University)
**Date:** 2025 (Journal of Political Economy, Vol. 133, No. 7, pp. 2180–2228)
**Source:** https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/734873 (NBER Working Paper No. 29402: https://www.nber.org/papers/w29402)
**Abstract:** This paper analyzes 19,812 government documents on Chinese policy experiments conducted between 1980 and 2020, constructing a database of 633 policy experiments by 98 central ministries. Three findings emerge. First, experimentation sites exhibit strong positive selection on economic development, driven by misaligned incentives across political hierarchies: lower-level governments nominate sites likely to succeed and thereby enhance their promotion prospects, while the central government selects sites that are more developed and thus more likely to generate visible results. Second, local politicians exert strategic excess effort during policy experiments that inflates apparent policy effectiveness in ways that cannot be replicated during national rollout — an observational regularity the authors term "Potemkin performance." Third, the central government fails to fully account for these distortions when evaluating experimental outcomes, systematically biasing policy learning and distorting national policy choices. The paper quantifies these distortions using a structural model and documents that their magnitude is economically large. While China's institutions enable policy experimentation at unparalleled scale — with hundreds of pilots across every policy domain simultaneously — strategic gaming by local actors significantly limits the effectiveness of the resulting policy learning. The findings have implications for the design of pilot programs in developing countries and authoritarian political systems more broadly.

## 3. The Crowding-In Effects of Local Government Debt in China
**Authors:** Xiaoming Li, Zheng Liu (Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco), Yuchao Peng, Zhiwei Xu
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Monetary Economics)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304393226000371 (FRBSF Working Paper 2024-35: https://ideas.repec.org/p/fip/fedfwp/99080.html)
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how a change in the composition of local government debt — induced by China's large-scale debt-to-bond swap program initiated in 2015 — affected bank risk-taking, credit allocation, and local productivity. Before the swap, Chinese local governments had accumulated substantial off-balance-sheet liabilities in the form of municipal corporate debt held by banks; the swap converted this debt into formally recognized government bonds. Using a difference-in-differences identification strategy and confidential loan-level data, the authors show that the swap significantly increased bank risk-taking through a capital-regulation channel operating under Basel III: replacing municipal corporate debt (which carried positive risk weights) with government bonds (risk-weight zero) reduced risk-weighted assets and freed up regulatory capital, enabling banks to reallocate credit toward riskier private borrowers. Banks reduced credit spreads on loans to private-owned enterprises (POEs) relative to state-owned enterprises (SOEs), with significantly larger reductions in provinces with higher outstanding government debt. This reallocation of credit from lower-productivity SOEs toward more productive POEs raised local total factor productivity, generating a crowding-in — rather than the commonly assumed crowding-out — effect of local government debt on private investment. The results highlight that the form in which public debt is held by banks, not merely its quantity, shapes its macroeconomic consequences.

## 4. Desired Fertility, Realized Fertility, and the Effects of China's Universal Two-Child Policy
**Authors:** Hanming Fang (University of Pennsylvania), Chang Liu
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 182, Article 103796)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103796
**Abstract:** This paper studies the impact of China's 2016 Universal Two-Child (UTC) policy, which lifted birth restrictions for all married couples. Drawing on the 2017 China Fertility Survey — sampling 249,946 women aged 15–60 across 2,737 counties in all 31 provinces — the authors compare women who had one child in late 2015 and became newly eligible under the UTC against those who had already been eligible for a second birth. The study documents a markedly compressed distribution of fertility desires: 29.6% of women prefer one child, 58.1% prefer two, and only 11.4% prefer three or more. Despite this, the UTC raised births by only approximately 0.025 children per newly eligible woman — roughly 744,000 additional births per year, just 4.1% of China's annual total — releasing only 16.6% of China's fertility potential. Childrearing costs, proxied by local housing prices, significantly dampen the policy's effectiveness: a one-standard-deviation increase in log housing prices reduces policy-induced births by 0.007. The findings indicate that China's fertility shortfall is driven primarily by what women want rather than by government restrictions, and that relaxing birth limits without addressing the economic costs of child-rearing — particularly housing — will have limited demographic impact. The paper contributes to a growing literature on the determinants of fertility in middle-income countries undergoing rapid demographic transition, with implications for how China's subsequent Three-Child and pro-natalist policies are likely to fare.

## 5. The Stalled Quiet Revolution: Population Control, Skewed Sex Ratios, and the Widening Gender Gap in Labor Force Participation
**Authors:** Zihao Chen, Yawen Ding, Xu Tian
**Date:** June 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Vol. 182, Article 103798)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103798
**Abstract:** China's female labor force participation (FLFP) has declined over recent decades, bucking the global "quiet revolution" of rising women's economic engagement. This paper attributes the widening gender gap to the long-run unintended consequences of China's One-Child Policy (OCP), which skewed local sex ratios toward males. Exploiting cross-county variation in sex-ratio distortions generated by the OCP as the primary identification strategy, the authors find that the policy reduced women's labor force participation by 12 percentage points relative to male counterparts. Mechanism analysis reveals that women exposed to more male-skewed sex ratios exhibit higher marriage and fertility rates, improved spousal income, greater intra-household bargaining power, and more leisure time — all factors that reduce labor market attachment. These household-level responses, rather than labor demand effects, account for most of the FLFP decline. The paper connects China's demographic interventions directly to its labor market outcomes, arguing that the stalling of China's gender gap closure is a structural consequence of birth-control policy rather than cultural preferences, with implications for how fertility policy reforms interact with female labor supply. The results highlight a rarely noted but quantitatively important externality of China's population control program: by compressing male-to-female ratios, the OCP reshaped marriage market conditions in ways that ultimately reduced women's economic activity, counteracting the human-capital gains that expanded education delivered to Chinese women over the same period.
