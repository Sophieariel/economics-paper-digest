# China Economics Papers — 2026-07-09

## 1. From Trade Protectionism to Legal Protectionism: How the Trump Trade War Harms US Firms in China
**Authors:** Shiyu Bo (Jinan University), Wen Liu
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304387826001124
**Abstract:** This paper documents a previously overlooked spillover of trade conflicts into the legal system, showing that the US–China trade war reduced the ability of US firms to enforce contracts through Chinese courts. Using the universe of enterprise-to-enterprise civil litigation judgments in China from 2014 to 2020 — approximately one million cases — the authors find that US tariff shocks led to a sizable increase in the win rates of Chinese defendants against US plaintiffs in cities with greater exposure to bilateral tariff increases. The effect operates primarily through shifts in judges' attitudes rather than government-directed judicial capture: the mechanism is driven by citizen awareness and nationalist sentiment, which pressures less experienced judges to favor domestic parties, while highly professional judges serve as a significant buffer against bias. Consistent with nationalist rather than local protectionist motivation, the effect shields all Chinese firms — not merely local ones — in lawsuits against US plaintiffs. Beyond win rates, the trade war reduced trial efficiency involving US firms, measured by longer trial durations, elevated appeal rates, and more frequent use of discretionary procedural codes. The findings reveal that trade conflicts can generate legal protectionism as a second-order consequence, extending the economic costs of tariff wars beyond the directly affected sectors to the broader institutional environment for cross-border commerce.

## 2. Preferential Credit Policy with Sectoral Markup Heterogeneity
**Authors:** Kaiji Chen (Emory University), Yuxuan Huang (Peking University), Xuewen Liu (University of Hong Kong), Zhikun Lu (Emory University), Yong Wang (Peking University)
**Date:** February 2026 (forthcoming, Journal of International Economics, Vol. 161, Article 104238)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4847111
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether preferential credit policies directed at specific sectors can improve aggregate efficiency, using China's credit allocation system as the empirical setting. The authors document a novel fact using Chinese firm-level data from 2009 to 2020: sectors with higher markups — reflecting underfunded activities with returns above the cost of capital — receive disproportionately larger credit subsidies, and these sectors exhibit correspondingly higher revenue-based total factor productivity. To assess the aggregate welfare implications, the paper develops a quantitative general equilibrium model that distinguishes the allocative and selection effects of preferential credit. The central finding is that while preferential credit weakens firm selection within targeted sectors by enabling less productive firms to survive, the policy improves aggregate efficiency by reallocating capital toward high-return activities that would otherwise be systematically underfunded. A counterfactual elimination of the policy would nearly double measured productivity losses: efficiency losses in manufacturing would increase from 2.9 percent to 5.5 percent. These results challenge the standard presumption that industrial credit policies distort resource allocation and reduce welfare. In a second-best environment characterized by pre-existing capital misallocation — where markets fail to channel resources to their highest-value uses — well-targeted credit interventions can enhance rather than reduce overall efficiency by correcting distortions that market forces alone cannot resolve.

## 3. The Geopolitics of Industrial Policy: Evidence from Export Credits
**Authors:** Adrien Matray (Princeton University), Karsten Müller (National University of Singapore), Zekai Shen, Chenzi Xu (University of California, Berkeley)
**Date:** 2026 (Working Paper; presented at NBER Chinese Economy Working Group Meeting, Spring 2026)
**Source:** https://chenzi-xu.com/
**Abstract:** This paper studies export credits — officially supported government financing that enables foreign buyers to purchase domestic exports — as a major but understudied tool of industrial policy with growing geopolitical significance. The authors construct the first comprehensive global dataset characterizing the export credit market across countries and over time, documenting that export credits account for over 60 percent of developing countries' external debt and frequently exceed half of their total imports. Using this novel dataset, the paper demonstrates that geopolitical considerations are a first-order determinant of export credit allocation: bilateral geopolitical alignment strongly predicts both the volume and terms of export financing flows across country pairs. The identification exploits quasi-random variation from the rotation of non-permanent members onto the United Nations Security Council, finding that countries gaining temporary UNSC membership experience significant inflows of export credits from permanent members, consistent with export finance being deployed as a tool of political influence rather than purely commercial logic. A central empirical finding is that China now provides more export credits than all OECD countries combined, reflecting the dramatic expansion of Chinese development finance over the past two decades. Among OECD economies, the geopolitical component of export credit allocation has increased substantially in recent years and now rivals its role in Chinese lending, suggesting that geopolitical competition over export markets and strategic industries is reshaping the global landscape of officially supported trade finance.

## 4. United States Financial Sanctions and the Surge of Chinese Lending
**Authors:** Daniel Berkowitz (University of Pittsburgh), Yichen Sun, Zehao Wang, Sili Zhou (University of Macau)
**Date:** December 2025 (Working Paper; presented at NBER Chinese Economy Working Group Meeting, Spring 2026)
**Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2026/program/paper/DkG46fSs
**Abstract:** This paper documents how United States financial sanctions have inadvertently accelerated the expansion of Chinese lending in global financial markets. Using detailed firm- and deal-level data on the global syndicated loan market from 1990 to 2023, the authors show that US financial sanctions significantly reduced the supply of US-origin and G7-origin syndicated loans to companies in sanctioned countries, consistent with targeted sanctions reducing access to Western financial markets. This contraction in Western credit supply was partially but substantially offset by a surge in Chinese-origin syndicated lending to the same borrowers. The paper then examines the downstream consequences of this substitution for borrower behavior and contract terms. Firms in sanctioned countries that shifted toward Chinese lenders became significantly less transparent: they reduced financial disclosures and experienced elevated rates of delistings and mergers — patterns consistent with reduced governance and monitoring pressure. Compared to US-origin loan contracts, Chinese-origin syndicated loans featured shorter maturities, more covenant provisions, and higher refinancing rates, granting Chinese lenders stronger ongoing control rights over borrowers. The results suggest that US financial sanctions, by excluding sanctioned entities from Western capital markets, have contributed to the expansion of Chinese financial influence in developing and emerging economies, with implications for the effectiveness of sanctions as a foreign policy instrument and for the geopolitical competition over global financial architecture.

## 5. Microclimate Risks and Consumer Adaptation
**Authors:** Jianwei Xing, Kangkai Wang, Hua Zhang, Shuang Zhang, Eric Zou (University of Michigan)
**Date:** 2026 (Working Paper; presented at NBER Chinese Economy Working Group Meeting, Spring 2026)
**Source:** https://www.jianweixing.com/
**Abstract:** This paper studies how sub-city temperature variation shapes business outcomes in urban economies, using a combination of high-resolution satellite microclimate data and geo-located administrative records covering over 150,000 restaurants and eatery service establishments in a Chinese mega-city. Standard analyses of climate risks aggregate temperature to the city or regional level, obscuring the substantial variation in thermal conditions within cities arising from differential exposure to solar radiation, impervious surfaces, vegetation cover, and building density. The authors document that within-city temperature variation on hot days is economically significant: locations in urban heat island zones experience meaningfully higher ambient temperatures than cooler neighborhoods within the same city, with differences that are persistent and predictable. Merging microclimate measures with granular data on revenues and consumer foot traffic, the paper estimates a damage function for microclimate shocks on business performance, finding that above-threshold local temperature increases cause significant reductions in consumer visits and business revenues, with effects concentrated on establishments lacking air conditioning and situated in high-density built environments. Urban green spaces — parks, street trees, and vegetated areas surrounding businesses — substantially mitigate exposure to microclimate shocks, generating a measurable buffer in consumer activity during hot days. The results imply that location-level microclimate management — through urban greening, surface material choices, and building design — constitutes an important and underappreciated dimension of business adaptation strategy under rising climate risks, and that city-level climate analyses understate the distributional consequences of warming for urban commercial activity.
