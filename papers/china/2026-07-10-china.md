# China Economics Papers — 2026-07-10

## 1. Household Migration and Collateral Constraint: Cash-based Housing Resettlement in China
**Authors:** Zhiguo He (University of Chicago; NBER), Zehao Liu, Xinle Pang, Yang Su, Kunru Zou
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34982)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34982
**Abstract:** This paper studies how collateral constraints shape household migration decisions, using China's Shantytown Renovation Program (2015–2018) as a natural experiment. The program displaced millions of urban residents from informal housing settlements and compensated them either in cash or with replacement housing units. The cash treatment is the key source of exogenous variation: by converting illiquid housing assets into liquid funds, cash resettlement relaxed the collateral constraints that would otherwise prevent displaced households from purchasing homes in higher-tier cities with more expensive housing markets. The authors document that cash-compensated households were significantly more likely to migrate across city tiers and to purchase housing in higher-cost locations, relative to voucher-compensated counterparts who were restricted to local purchases. They develop and estimate a dynamic spatial equilibrium model incorporating collateral constraints that rationalizes these behavioral differences and traces the general-equilibrium effects on local housing markets. The central quantitative finding is that endogenous migration amplifies household housing expenditure responses by approximately 40 percent and accounts for over 20 percent of the housing price increases observed in destination cities during 2016–2020. The results highlight collateral constraints as a mechanism linking housing wealth liquidity to geographic mobility, with implications for understanding China's urbanization dynamics and for the welfare consequences of resettlement policy design.

## 2. The Remains of the Trade: The U.S.–China Trade War and Its Aftermath
**Authors:** Pol Antràs (Harvard University; NBER), Andrea F. Presbitero (IMF)
**Date:** February 2026 (prepared for the 2nd Annual AlUla Conference for Emerging Market Economies, February 8–9, 2026)
**Source:** https://antras.scholars.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum5876/files/2026-03/PAAPAlUla_1.pdf
**Abstract:** This paper documents the evolving empirical patterns of what the authors term the "Great Reallocation" — the large-scale restructuring of global trade associated with the ongoing U.S.–China decoupling. Drawing on granular bilateral trade data spanning from the first Trump administration's tariff escalation through the second administration's Liberation Day tariff announcement of April 2025, the paper tracks how U.S. import sourcing has shifted away from China and how Chinese exports have been diverted toward alternative markets. A central finding is that the pace of reallocation has accelerated markedly under the second Trump administration, with Liberation Day serving as a structural break: the announcements of that day appear to have been interpreted as a signal of permanent change in the U.S.–China trade relationship, triggering a sustained acceleration in reallocation. Empirically, Chinese exports to the United States declined sharply following the November 2024 election, while Chinese exports to Europe and other Asian economies rose substantially, reflecting trade diversion rather than global trade destruction. The paper proposes and operationalizes a taxonomy of third-country spillovers from U.S.–China decoupling: export competition effects (as China redirects exports into third-country markets), import exposure effects (as third countries absorb more Chinese goods), sourcing opportunity effects (as third-country suppliers gain access to U.S. procurement), and rerouting potential (as goods are transshipped through third countries to circumvent tariffs). Geopolitical alignment emerges as an increasingly strong predictor of bilateral trade flows. The results have implications for the design of trade policy in a geopolitically fragmented global economy and for understanding network consequences of bilateral decoupling.

## 3. Accounting for the Evolution of China's Production and Trade Patterns
**Authors:** Hanwei Huang (London School of Economics), Jiandong Ju (Tsinghua University), Vivian Z. Yue (Emory University; NBER)
**Date:** March 2026 (International Economic Review; NBER Working Paper No. 32415)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w32415
**Abstract:** This paper studies the evolution of China's production and trade patterns over the period of China's integration into the global economy, documenting new empirical facts about structural change in China's comparative advantage and developing a quantitative framework to decompose the underlying driving forces. Using comprehensive microdata at the industry and firm levels, the authors document pronounced shifts in both what China produces and what it exports: production and exports have moved systematically toward capital-intensive goods over time, consistent with China's rapid capital accumulation, but with substantial heterogeneity across industries and significant offsetting forces from within-industry productivity growth. To decompose the mechanisms, the authors develop and estimate a quantitative trade model that combines Ricardian comparative advantage with Heckscher–Ohlin factor-endowment forces and heterogeneous firms. Counterfactual analysis reveals that capital deepening was the primary driver of the shift toward capital-intensive production and exports, but that labor-biased productivity growth — efficiency gains that are relatively more embodied in labor-intensive technologies — acted as a significant counterforce, attenuating the capital-intensity shift. Two additional findings are particularly notable: China's own trade openness, measured as the ratio of exports to GDP, peaked around the mid-2000s and declined thereafter; yet the world's exposure to Chinese exports — the share of global absorption sourced from China — rose continuously throughout the period. The paper thus provides a unified empirical and quantitative account of China's evolving role in the global trading system and of the forces that have shaped its pattern of specialization.

## 4. Tracing Effects of Sanctions through the Value Chain: Evidence from the US Cotton Ban
**Authors:** Jean-François Maystadt (KU Leuven), Kampui Tsang (Chinese University of Hong Kong), Johannes Van Biesebroeck (KU Leuven; CEPR)
**Date:** April 2025 (working paper); presented at 3rd Kiel-Göttingen-CEPR Conference on China in the Global Economy, June 30–July 1, 2026, Berlin
**Source:** https://econ.cuhk.edu.hk/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Biesebroeck.pdf
**Abstract:** This paper examines how economic sanctions propagate through domestic supply chains, using the United States' import ban on cotton products originating from China's Xinjiang region as the empirical setting. The Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act, implemented in December 2020, prohibited imports of goods linked to forced labor in Xinjiang and created exogenous variation in sanction exposure across Chinese regions and industries based on the geographic and sectoral composition of cotton production and processing. The authors trace the effects of the ban through China's domestic input-output network, estimating impacts at the industry and province level. At the bilateral China–U.S. level, the sanction reduced China's cotton and cotton-product exports to the United States by 17 percent at the intensive margin (existing trade flows) and 5 percent at the extensive margin (product-destination pairs). Domestic supply chain tracing reveals that provinces economically integrated with Xinjiang's cotton-growing regions suffered disproportionately large export losses, consistent with the ban propagating backward through the input-output network. A notable finding is that despite the European Union imposing no comparable sanctions on Chinese cotton products, China's cotton-related exports to the EU declined significantly — an effect attributed to multi-market supply-chain reorganization responses. The results contribute to the emerging literature on the effectiveness and unintended consequences of humanitarian trade sanctions, and carry implications for debates about coordinating sanction regimes across trading partners when global value chains tightly link targeted and non-targeted markets.

## 5. The Misuse of China's R&D Subsidies: Noncompliance and Its Impact on Policy Effectiveness
**Authors:** Philipp Boeing (ZEW – Leibniz Centre for European Economic Research), Bettina Peters (ZEW; University of Luxembourg)
**Date:** March 2026 (ZEW Discussion Paper No. 24-077; SSRN No. 5045083)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5045083
**Abstract:** This paper investigates the extent and consequences of noncompliance with R&D subsidy requirements in China, where firms receiving government grants are legally obligated to invest the funds in research and development activities. Using administrative panel data on Chinese manufacturing firms from 2001 to 2011 matched to detailed R&D expenditure records, the authors develop and implement an empirical framework to identify the share of grantees that misappropriate subsidy funds for non-R&D purposes. The identification strategy exploits the one-sided nature of noncompliance — firms cannot report spending more on R&D than they received in grants, but can spend less — and applies an instrumental variable approach to separate the causal effect of R&D subsidies for compliant firms from the diluted effect observed in the full population of recipients. The central finding is striking in its magnitude: approximately 42 percent of R&D subsidy grantees misappropriated their grants, and these noncompliant firms account for roughly 53 percent of total subsidy expenditure. The policy consequences are substantial: noncompliance generates medium-level partial crowding out of private R&D investment and reduces the aggregate causal impact of China's R&D subsidy program by more than half relative to what full compliance would have achieved. The results raise important questions about the design of innovation subsidy programs in environments with limited monitoring capacity and carry implications for evaluating the effectiveness of China's ambitious research and development promotion policies, which have directed trillions of yuan toward innovation-driven industrial upgrading.
