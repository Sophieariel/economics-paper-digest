## 1. China's Global Ownership
**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, Hong Ru
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This paper examines Chinese corporate acquisitions across 161,773 firms in 159 countries from 2012–2021, tracing ownership through offshore structures to identify China's true global investment footprint—$2.1 trillion by 2021. Chinese-controlled foreign assets expanded at 20% annually over the sample period. State-owned enterprises strategically target R&D-intensive companies in developed economies. Despite increased capital injections and research expenditures, target firms' profitability declines after acquisition, suggesting that financial returns are not the primary motive. Chinese parent companies experience accelerated patent growth following acquisitions in high-income countries, while competing foreign firms reduce their own R&D investment in response. The authors conclude that China employs a "distinctively state-driven model of global ownership" that prioritizes technological knowledge transfer to domestic operations over immediate financial gains—a strategy with significant implications for global innovation competition and regulatory responses to Chinese outbound investment.

## 2. The Stalled Quiet Revolution: Population Control, Skewed Sex Ratios, and the Widening Gender Gap in Labor Force Participation
**Authors:** Zihao Chen, Yawen Ding, Xu Tian
**Date:** 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304387826000817
**Abstract:** China's declining female labor force participation stands in stark contrast to the global "quiet revolution" of rising women's economic engagement. This paper offers a novel explanation through the long-term unintended effects of population control policies, which distorted sex ratios in both marriage and labor markets. Using a cohort difference-in-differences strategy that exploits cross-cohort and cross-prefecture variation in One-Child Policy exposure intensity, the study finds that the policy significantly reduced women's labor force participation by 12 percentage points relative to their male counterparts. The widening gender gap is primarily driven by male-biased sex ratios resulting from the One-Child Policy: women exposed to more skewed sex ratios exhibit higher marriage rates, higher fertility rates, greater intra-household bargaining power (proxied by spousal income differentials and asset control), extended leisure time, and fewer ambition-related psychological traits. Labor market demand-side distortions play a secondary role in explaining the participation decline. The findings establish a causal link from demographic engineering through marital market distortions to aggregate female labor supply, with implications for understanding the consequences of population policy beyond fertility outcomes. *Journal of Development Economics*, Vol. 182.

## 3. Desired Fertility, Realized Fertility and the Effects of China's Universal Two-Child Policy
**Authors:** Hanming Fang, Chang Liu
**Date:** 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304387826000799
**Abstract:** This paper examines the effectiveness of China's Universal Two-Child Policy (implemented January 2016), which relaxed the One-Child Policy to permit all couples to have two children. Using data from the 2017 China Fertility Survey linked to the 2020 population census, the authors estimate that the policy increased births by only 0.023 per eligible woman in 2016–2017, driven almost exclusively by women who desired two or more children prior to the reform. Survey evidence reveals that desired fertility in China is highly compressed: 0.9% of women aged 20–40 report no desired children, 29.6% prefer one child, 58.12% prefer two, and only 11.37% prefer three or more. The paper argues that "women's desired fertility has become the binding constraint in low-fertility contexts": once fertility desires fall below policy ceilings, removing quotas alone cannot generate a substantial rebound. Policy effectiveness is strongly heterogeneous across regions, with higher housing prices reducing policy-induced births (a one-standard deviation increase in log housing prices reduces policy-induced births by 0.007). The results imply that pro-natalist policies relying solely on quota liberalization will be ineffective in post–One-Child Policy China, and that raising desired fertility requires broader interventions addressing childcare costs and female labor market compatibility. *Journal of Development Economics*, Vol. 182.

## 4. Moving to Opportunity for Polluting: Intra-City Evidence from China's Land Market
**Authors:** Shiyu Bo, Fan Zhang, Hongjia Zhu
**Date:** 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304387826000933
**Abstract:** This paper examines how air pollution regulations affect the intra-city location of industrial activity in China, extending the "pollution haven" hypothesis from the cross-country and cross-city levels to the within-city level. The empirical strategy exploits China's 12th Five-Year Plan on Air Pollution Prevention and Control (2011), which subjected 117 cities—47 treatment cities and 70 controls—to differential regulation stringency. Using a triple-difference design that compares polluting versus non-polluting industry land allocations across treatment and control cities, before and after the regulatory change, the authors find that after the implementation of more stringent regulation, land newly leased to firms in polluting industries in treated cities is located significantly farther from pollution monitoring stations. The mechanism operates through both supply and demand channels: local governments shift industrial land supply away from monitoring station catchment areas, and land buyers simultaneously reduce bids for plots near monitoring stations. The resulting air quality improvements near monitoring stations are partly offset by production losses from remote locations and by increased pollution in peripheral urban zones, implying that within-city pollution leakage is a first-order consideration in evaluating urban environmental policy. *Journal of Development Economics*, Vol. 182.

## 5. Where Has All the Dynamism Gone? Productivity Growth in China's Manufacturing Sector, 1998–2013
**Authors:** Loren Brandt, Johannes Van Biesebroeck, Luhang Wang, Yifan Zhang
**Date:** 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304387826000039
**Abstract:** This paper documents and decomposes a pronounced slowdown in total factor productivity (TFP) growth across China's manufacturing sector over 1998–2013. To overcome data limitations from the discontinuation of China's Annual Survey of Industrial Enterprises after 2007, the authors use the annual firm survey conducted by China's State Taxation Administration (STA), recovering the original sampling scheme and constructing comparable samples for 2007–2013. The analysis reveals that the productivity growth slowdown cuts across all industries, all ownership types (state-owned, private, foreign), and all regions—ruling out composition effects as the primary explanation. The loss of dynamism in the private sector is especially prominent, with private-sector TFP growth decelerating sharply after the mid-2000s. The reduced contribution of firm entry to aggregate productivity growth is a particularly striking pattern: new entrants in the later period are less productive relative to incumbents than in the earlier period, and net entry rates decline markedly. The paper connects this finding to barriers to entry, rising fixed costs, and tightening regulatory burdens that may crowd out the most productive potential entrants. The results contribute to understanding the medium-run sources of China's economic slowdown and raise questions about the sustainability of productivity-led growth under the current institutional environment. *Journal of Development Economics*, Vol. 181.
