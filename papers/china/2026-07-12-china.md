# China Economics Papers — 2026-07-12

## 1. China's Global Ownership
**Authors:** Jennie Bai (Georgetown University), Luc Laeven (IMF; CEPR), Yaojun Ke, Hong Ru (HKUST)
**Date:** April 27, 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35106)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This paper documents the scale, composition, and real effects of Chinese overseas corporate ownership using a newly assembled dataset covering 161,773 firms across 159 countries from 2012 to 2021. The authors trace ownership chains through offshore tax havens to identify Chinese-controlled foreign assets, which expanded at approximately 20 percent annually to reach $2.1 trillion — roughly 3 percent of global corporate assets — by 2021. Chinese investors, particularly state-owned enterprises, disproportionately target R&D-intensive firms and firms embedded in supply chains that feed back into the Chinese domestic economy, a pattern consistent with a strategic technology-acquisition motive rather than passive portfolio diversification. Following acquisition, target firms increase capital expenditure and R&D spending, but these inputs fail to translate into local innovative output: patent counts and citations from target firms stagnate or decline post-acquisition. In striking contrast, Chinese parent companies experience a sharp acceleration in granted patents after acquiring developed-economy firms, providing the paper's central finding of a novel "innovation spillback" mechanism. Cross-border knowledge flows run predominantly from acquired subsidiaries back toward Chinese headquarters rather than diffusing within the host economy, and non-target competitor firms in the same technology space reduce their own research investments in response. The results challenge the view that foreign direct investment uniformly benefits the host economy and point toward systematic technology extraction as a strategic motivation for Chinese outward FDI, with implications for the design of investment screening policies in advanced economies.

## 2. The Generative AI Learning Penalty: Evidence from Chinese Secondary Education
**Authors:** David Strömberg (Stockholm University; CEPR), Victor Lei (University of Hong Kong), Yanhui Wu (University of Hong Kong)
**Date:** June 2, 2026 (CEPR Discussion Paper No. 21577)
**Source:** https://ideas.repec.org/p/cpr/ceprdp/21577.html
**Abstract:** This paper provides the first large-scale experimental evidence on how access to generative artificial intelligence affects student learning outcomes, exploiting 30 months of richly detailed panel data on 26,811 Chinese middle and high school students in grades 7–12. The dataset combines monthly closed-book examination scores, high-school and college entrance examination results, and granular homework performance records — including submission time and completion speed — across nine subjects, enabling the authors to separately identify AI effects on the in-class learning environment from its effects on measured homework performance. The results reveal a sharp divergence between short-run productivity measures and long-run human capital accumulation: AI adoption raised homework scores by 18 percent and reduced homework completion time by 30 percent, consistent with widespread use of AI to complete assignments directly. However, monthly closed-book exam scores fell by 20 percent within six months of AI availability, and scores on high-stakes college and high-school entrance examinations declined by 18–24 percent, with the full penalty emerging only after approximately two years of continued AI access. The heterogeneous effects are economically revealing: learning losses are largest in social science subjects, followed by STEM and languages; they are especially pronounced among junior-grade students, high-achieving students, and boys. Behavioral fingerprinting of homework submission patterns identifies that roughly 80 percent of AI users display completion-time signatures consistent with homework outsourcing — extremely rapid submission combined with high homework accuracy — and this subgroup drives the examination performance declines. The findings carry implications for educational technology policy, for China's human capital investment strategy, and for the broader literature on automation and skill formation.

## 3. Market Contraction and Innovation Divergence: The Impact of the US–China Trade War on Chinese Innovation
**Authors:** Xiao Ma (Johns Hopkins University), Yueyuan Ma (University of Rochester), Hanyi Tao (Stanford University), Yiran Zhang (Princeton University)
**Date:** January 2026 (SSRN Working Paper; presented at NBER Chinese Economy Working Group Meeting, Spring 2026)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5455894
**Abstract:** This paper examines how the US–China trade war reshaped the direction and intensity of Chinese innovation. The authors break down patent abstracts into technical terms through large-scale textual analysis, enabling systematic measurement of the technological similarity between Chinese and US patent portfolios over time. Using variation in US import tariff exposure across Chinese industries as the identification strategy, they document that higher tariffs reduce not only the volume of Chinese patent filings but also the degree of technological alignment between Chinese innovation and the US frontier, with effects concentrated in alignment with the most recent American patents rather than older technology stocks. Heterogeneous firm modeling links these patterns to the demand channel: US tariffs contract the addressable market for Chinese exporters, reducing the value of developing technologies suited to US consumer preferences. Quantitative analysis attributes roughly 27 percent of the observed China–US innovation divergence to this demand contraction channel. The downstream consequences for trade are substantial: the combined changes in innovation intensity and direction caused by US tariffs generate a 3.5 percent reduction in Chinese firm exports by 2021, with the shift in innovation direction alone — holding quantity fixed — accounting for 14 percent of this export decline. The results contribute to a growing literature on the determinants of innovation direction and suggest that trade barriers have persistent effects on technological trajectories that outlast the tariffs themselves, as domestic Chinese firms pivot R&D resources toward domestic market needs and away from developed-economy technology standards.

## 4. When Do Financial Frictions Matter for Misallocation?
**Authors:** Yan Bai (University of Rochester; NBER), Dan Lu (University of Rochester), Xu Tian (University of Toronto), Yajie Wang
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34930)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34930
**Abstract:** This paper reassesses the contribution of financial frictions to capital misallocation in China, a question that has generated conflicting evidence in earlier work. Using administrative firm-level data from China's manufacturing sector, the authors document two striking empirical patterns absent in comparable US data: substantial dispersion in the average revenue product of capital across Chinese firms, and a strong positive correlation between a firm's capital productivity and the borrowing costs it faces — meaning the most productive firms pay the highest cost of capital. These facts suggest that financial frictions systematically constrain the firms that would most benefit from additional investment. To quantify the macroeconomic consequences, the authors develop a heterogeneous-firm model that incorporates endogenous firm-specific borrowing costs alongside additional permanent capital distortions modeled as exogenous wedges. The key finding is that the macroeconomic impact of financial frictions is highly sensitive to the presence of other distortions: in an economy with permanent capital wedges, eliminating financial frictions raises total factor productivity by 25 percentage points. However, when those additional distortions are removed first, reducing financial frictions alone increases TFP by less than 2 percentage points. This stark interaction arises because permanent firm-level distortions generate endogenous financial heterogeneity — the firms most penalized by capital wedges also face the harshest borrowing costs — so that the welfare cost of financial frictions is amplified by the presence of complementary misallocation sources. The results carry practical implications for the sequencing of financial reform in China: liberalizing credit markets in isolation from product and factor market reforms is unlikely to yield large aggregate productivity gains.

## 5. Where Has All the Dynamism Gone? Productivity Growth in China's Manufacturing Sector, 1998–2013
**Authors:** Loren Brandt (University of Toronto), Johannes Van Biesebroeck (KU Leuven; CEPR), Luhang Wang, Yifan Zhang (Chinese University of Hong Kong)
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of Development Economics, Volume 181)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304387826000039
**Abstract:** This paper provides a firm-level accounting of China's manufacturing productivity slowdown using a novel alternative data source — the annual enterprise survey conducted by China's State Taxation Administration (STA) — to resolve longstanding data quality problems that have hampered analysis after 2007. The National Bureau of Statistics' widely used Annual Survey of Industrial Firms suffers from documented issues in the post-2007 period, including missing value-added and intermediate-input data and concerns about over-reporting, limiting confidence in productivity estimates derived from it. The STA survey, while not designed as a representative sample, covers the same firms across the same years and provides reliable financial information. The authors develop a novel methodology to recover the implicit sampling scheme of the STA survey by comparing the joint distribution of key observable variables across both datasets, and use this recovered scheme to simulate samples for 2007–2013 that are directly comparable to the NBS benchmark years. Their productivity estimates reveal a marked and broad-based slowdown in revenue-based total factor productivity growth: the deceleration cuts across all industries, all ownership categories, and all regions, ruling out compositional explanations. The loss of dynamism is especially concentrated in two places: the private sector, which had been the primary engine of productivity growth through the 2000s, and the entry margin, with new firm creation contributing substantially less to aggregate productivity gains than in earlier periods. The results are consistent with a tightening of entry barriers, rising costs of doing business, and a diminished role for market selection in allocating resources toward productive uses — patterns that set the stage for China's subsequent aggregate growth deceleration.
