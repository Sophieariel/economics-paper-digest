# China Economics Papers — 2026-07-13

## 1. Firm Entry and Market Interdependence: Evidence from Anti-Dumping Tariffs on Chinese Exports
**Authors:** Cheng Chen (Clemson University), Zhang Chen (Hong Kong University of Science and Technology), Chang Sun (University of Hong Kong)
**Date:** Presented at NBER East Asian Seminar on Economics, June 7, 2026 (Tsinghua University, Beijing)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/conferences/east-asian-seminar-economics-2026
**Abstract:** This paper studies how a unilateral anti-dumping tariff can depress Chinese exports across markets through an entry-based mechanism. Using Chinese Customs data matched with anti-dumping cases filed against China by importing countries, the authors document that exports of investigated products decline not only in the focal market where the tariff is imposed but also in third markets where no investigation was initiated. In third markets, the export decline is driven primarily by a fall in the number of exporters rather than a reduction in export quantities per firm, and is accompanied by higher unit values — consistent with selection of only high-productivity firms continuing to export. The authors explain these patterns with a model in which firms make interdependent entry decisions across export destinations, so that the fixed costs and profitability of entering one market depend on joint entry decisions across markets. Under such interdependence, a tariff in one destination effectively raises the cost threshold for entry into all markets, generating cross-market policy spillovers without any direct policy action in those markets. The entry margin alone can account for the heterogeneous tariff responses observed across products and destination-market pairs. The findings have implications for the geographic scope of trade policy effects and for measuring the true welfare costs of anti-dumping instruments.

## 2. China's Global Ownership
**Authors:** Jennie Bai (Georgetown University), Luc Laeven (IMF), Yaojun Ke, Hong Ru (Nanyang Technological University)
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35106)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This paper constructs a novel dataset spanning 161,773 firms across 159 countries from 2012 to 2021 to document and analyze Chinese corporate investment abroad. The authors trace full ownership chains through offshore holding structures and shell entities to identify ultimate Chinese beneficial control — revealing a Chinese global footprint substantially larger than official statistics based on direct ownership suggest. Key findings: Chinese-controlled foreign assets expanded at approximately 20% annually over the sample period, reaching $2.1 trillion or roughly 3% of global corporate assets by 2021. State-owned enterprises (SOEs) account for a disproportionate share of this expansion and systematically target research-intensive and global supply-chain-connected firms. While acquisition targets exhibit increased capital investment and R&D spending following Chinese ownership, these investments do not translate into higher patent output and are accompanied by declining target-firm profitability. The authors document an "innovation spillback" channel: Chinese parent companies sharply accelerate their own domestic patent grants in the years following their first acquisition of a developed-economy firm in the same technology space, even as innovation at the target stagnates. Chinese ownership also generates negative externalities for competing peer firms' R&D activity, though aggregate industry-level innovation remains broadly stable. The paper characterizes this as a distinctly state-directed ownership model that prioritizes long-run technological capacity-building and market access over short-run financial returns — a pattern qualitatively distinct from private cross-border acquisitions in the same period.

## 3. When Do Financial Frictions Matter for Misallocation?
**Authors:** Yan Bai (University of Rochester), Dan Lu (University of Rochester), Xu Tian (University of Rochester), Yajie Wang
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34930)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34930
**Abstract:** This paper revisits the quantitative importance of financial frictions for capital misallocation by developing a model that is jointly disciplined by firm-level borrowing costs and the average revenue product of capital (ARPK). Using panel data from Chinese manufacturing firms, the authors document two empirical facts absent in comparable U.S. data: substantial dispersion in ARPK across firms, and a strong positive cross-sectional relationship between ARPK and the borrowing costs firms face — implying that the most capital-productive firms are precisely those facing the highest cost of capital. A calibrated heterogeneous-firm model reveals that the welfare consequences of financial frictions depend critically on the presence of other capital distortions. When permanent firm-level distortions — such as size-dependent regulations, discriminatory tax treatment, or ownership-linked subsidies common in China — coexist with financial frictions, eliminating financial frictions raises total factor productivity by 25 percentage points. Without these co-existing distortions, the same reform raises TFP by less than 2 percentage points. The large difference arises from an interaction mechanism: permanent distortions generate endogenous financial heterogeneity and selection, making the most productive firms the most financially constrained and therefore the most sensitive to financial market reforms. The findings imply that the Chinese context — with its distinctive mix of private-sector financial repression and state-sector preferential access — is especially susceptible to misallocation from financial frictions, and that financial reform is a complement to, rather than a substitute for, structural reforms addressing underlying distortions.

## 4. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy
**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess (London School of Economics), Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen (MIT), Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34893)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether industrial policies that promote clean energy can deliver a "ray of hope" — simultaneously spurring economic growth, expanding welfare, and reducing carbon emissions. The empirical setting is China's large-scale solar energy subsidies, whose implementation varied across city-regions from 2004 to 2020, creating cross-sectional and temporal variation that the authors exploit using a synthetic-difference-in-differences design. They find that production and innovation subsidies were more effective than demand-side installation subsidies in generating large, persistent increases in local solar innovation, net firm entry, production volumes, and export activity. Demand-side policies did, however, reduce local air pollution. Using a structural spatial model with endogenous innovation and technology diffusion, the authors conduct counterfactual analysis to translate local effects into aggregate implications. City-level policy variation explains 40–50% of the aggregate changes in Chinese solar innovation, prices, and revenues observed over the period. The social benefits to Chinese citizens exceed total subsidy costs by 65%, rising to double when environmental co-benefits are included. Among subsidy types, innovation subsidies are the most cost-effective. The results provide microeconomic evidence for the role of industrial policy in driving a global green energy transition: Chinese solar subsidies contributed to the dramatic fall in global solar panel prices observed since 2010, with spillovers extending well beyond China's borders.

## 5. A Few Bad Apples? Academic Dishonesty, Political Selection, and Institutional Performance in China
**Authors:** Zhuang Liu, Wenwei Peng, Shaoda Wang (University of Chicago)
**Date:** January 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34747)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34747
**Abstract:** This paper investigates the relationship between academic dishonesty among Chinese graduate students and subsequent performance in public-sector careers, using plagiarism-detection algorithms applied to approximately 500,000 Chinese graduate dissertations. The authors match dissertation-level plagiarism scores to detailed career records for a large sample of government officials and judges. Key findings: plagiarism is pervasive in the dissertation corpus, and higher plagiarism rates at the dissertation stage predict subsequent adverse political selection — individuals with higher plagiarism scores are significantly more likely to secure government positions and advance in rank, consistent with institutional conditions that reward social connections over meritocratic ability and that fail to screen for academic integrity. In the judicial context, judges with plagiarism histories issue more preferential rulings — favoring connected or high-status litigants — and attract a significantly greater number of subsequent appeals, suggesting lower decision quality. The introduction of court livestreaming partially mitigates these effects by increasing judicial transparency and public accountability. The paper also presents quasi-experimental evidence that enforcement of academic integrity standards produces modest downstream improvements in the professional behavior of affected individuals. The findings contribute to the political economy literature on bureaucratic quality, meritocracy, and the channels through which educational institutions shape the quality of public governance in China.
