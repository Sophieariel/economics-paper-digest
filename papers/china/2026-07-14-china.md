# China Economics Papers — 2026-07-14

## 1. Empathy, Social Networks, and Academic Achievement: Experimental Evidence on Cross-Productivity in Skill Formation
**Authors:** Flavio Cunha, Qinyou Hu, Yiming Xia, Naibao Zhao
**Date:** June 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35370)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35370
**Abstract:** The researchers evaluate a parent-directed empathy development program for middle school students in China using a randomized trial with 2,246 students. Over a four-month period, the mobile app intervention yielded short-run gains in empathy and reduced bullying but no immediate academic effects. The long-term results proved more significant: treatment students were 3.2 percentage points more likely to enter elite high schools and 2.5 percentage points less likely to miss the entrance exam. The authors attribute these gains to improved self-beliefs and a restructuring of classroom friendship networks, demonstrating a social channel of cross-productivity: non-cognitive investments can reshape peer relationships to improve academic outcomes.

## 2. Intergenerational Mobility in Late Qing Dynasty: Evidence from Northeast China
**Authors:** Kristina Butaeva, Steven N. Durlauf, Alexander Shapoval
**Date:** June 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35313)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35313
**Abstract:** This paper examines intergenerational mobility across social classes during the late Qing dynasty employing a remarkable dataset from Liaoning province in Northeast China. The authors identify two distinct periods: before 1850, mobility exhibited stability; after 1850, class dynamics became unstable until the dynasty's collapse. The transition aligns with the Opium Wars and Taiping Rebellion. Using Markov-chain measures, the authors document increased overall intergenerational mobility, though this masked a decline in upward mobility alongside a rise in downward mobility — disparate patterns that resonate with broader theories of political instability.

## 3. College Affirmative Action and Students' Outcomes: A Matter of the Dose
**Authors:** Yi Chen, Chao Fu, Hongbin Li, Teng Li, Siyan Tang
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35281)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35281
**Abstract:** This paper examines how varying levels of affirmative action affect student outcomes by analyzing the ethnic minority bonus-point policy in China's centralized college admissions system. The researchers tracked students from college entrance exams through financial records spanning 10–23 years later. They discovered a non-linear relationship: lower affirmative action doses increase long-term consumption by 19% but high doses reduce it by 25%. The findings indicate that modest affirmative action benefits disadvantaged students across multiple measures, including career earnings and life satisfaction, yet excessive preferential treatment generates negative long-term consequences, particularly for those from weaker academic backgrounds.

## 4. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes
**Authors:** Chang Ma, Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35056)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056
**Abstract:** China's substantial current account surplus creates friction with trading partners. The research indicates that while industrial and trade policies often lead to sector-level imbalances, they play a relatively limited role in the economy-wide surplus. Instead, structural elements prove more significant — specifically an imbalanced sex ratio and unequal financing access between state-owned and non-state enterprises. Although macroeconomic stimulus can temporarily boost imports and reduce the surplus, addressing the surplus long-term requires reforms aiming at addressing the structural problems.

## 5. Rural-Urban Migration and Market Integration
**Authors:** Dennis Egger, Benjamin Faber, Ming Li, Wei Lin
**Date:** August 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 34098)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34098
**Abstract:** This paper examines how reducing rural-urban migration barriers affects trade and investment flows between cities and countryside regions. The authors analyze a policy experiment involving worker eligibility for urban residence registration (Hukou) across different locations in China. Results show that increased migration eligibility boosts rural-urban exports, imports, capital movements, and buyer-seller connections. Using a spatial equilibrium model, they find that a 10% rise in migration market access correlates with 1.5% growth in trade market access and 2% growth in investment market access. The knock-on effects on market integration were on average larger among the urban destinations compared to the rural origins.
