# China Economics Papers — 2026-07-16

## 1. Preferential Credit Policy with Sectoral Markup Heterogeneity
**Authors:** Kaiji Chen (Emory University), Yuxuan Huang, Xuewen Liu, Zhikun Lu, Yong Wang
**Date:** February 2026 (Journal of International Economics, Vol. 161, 104238, 2026); featured VoxChina July 14, 2026
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4847111
**Abstract:** This paper studies how preferential credit policies that target selected sectors affect aggregate productivity and welfare in emerging economies. Using Chinese firm-level data from 2009–2020, the authors document that sectors with higher markups receive larger credit subsidies and exhibit higher revenue-based productivity. They develop a multi-sector quantitative model with endogenously determined markups and calibrate it to match the distribution of sales both within and across sectors. The central finding is that preferential credit subsidies raise aggregate productivity and welfare by reallocating market shares toward sectors with higher markups, thereby reducing markup dispersion across the economy. A counterfactual analysis reveals that removing these policies would nearly double estimated productivity losses — from 2.9% to 5.5% — implying that well-targeted industrial credit policies can enhance rather than reduce overall efficiency, provided that the targeted sectors are those with genuine market distortions and elevated capital returns. The paper also shows that these gains persist in an extended framework with endogenous firm entry. The results have implications for evaluating the efficiency effects of directed credit and industrial policy in contexts where market power distortions and capital misallocation coexist, as in China.

## 2. Household Migration and Collateral Constraint: Cash-based Housing Resettlement in China
**Authors:** Zhiguo He (Stanford University), Zehao Liu (Renmin University of China), Xinle Pang (University at Buffalo, SUNY), Yang Su (Chinese University of Hong Kong), Kunru Zou (Hong Kong Baptist University)
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34982); featured VoxChina July 8, 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34982
**Abstract:** This paper examines how collateral constraints limit household relocation to high-cost urban areas and studies whether relaxing them through cash transfers can induce migration and amplify housing market effects. The empirical setting is China's shantytown renovation program (2015–2018), which converted illiquid shanty-house assets into portable cash payments. The authors show that this liquidity injection — by relaxing household borrowing constraints — enabled residents to purchase homes in higher-tier cities, generating migration flows that would not have occurred under voucher-based resettlement schemes. Using a spatial economic model that incorporates collateral restrictions on home purchases, they estimate that migration responses amplify household housing expenditure by approximately 42.5% relative to the counterfactual without the cash-based policy. The program accounts for roughly 20% of China's housing price appreciation during 2016–2020. The paper provides micro-level evidence on how borrowing constraints shape residential sorting and offers a causal estimate of the contribution of government-administered housing policy to China's housing boom. The findings highlight the role of asset liquidity in determining the effectiveness of cash transfer programs intended to promote housing market mobility and urbanization.

## 3. AI Patents in the United States and China: Measurement, Organization, and Knowledge Flows
**Authors:** Hanming Fang (University of Pennsylvania), Xian Gu, Hanyin Yan, Wu Zhu
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35022); featured VoxChina June 16, 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35022
**Abstract:** This paper develops a high-precision classifier to identify artificial intelligence patents by fine-tuning PatentSBERTa on manually labeled USPTO data, achieving 97.0% precision, 91.3% recall, and a 94.0% F1 score. The classifier generalizes effectively to Chinese patent records. Applying it to the full universe of granted U.S. patents (1976–2023) and Chinese patents (2010–2023), the authors document rapid AI patent growth in both countries and broad convergence in AI patenting intensity and subfield composition over time. China has recently surpassed the United States in annual AI patent counts. However, the organizational patterns of AI innovation diverge sharply: American AI patenting is concentrated among large private-sector incumbents and established geographic hubs (Silicon Valley, Seattle, New York), whereas Chinese AI patenting is more geographically diffuse and institutionally diverse, spanning universities, state-owned enterprises, and private firms across many provinces. Analysis of cross-border citation flows reveals continued technological interdependence rather than decoupling, with Chinese AI inventors relying significantly more heavily on U.S. frontier knowledge than vice versa. The asymmetry in knowledge dependence persists even as the volume gap has closed, suggesting that China's lead in raw patent counts does not yet reflect technological leadership at the frontier.

## 4. From Free Rider to Innovator: The Rise of China's Drug Development
**Authors:** Panle Jia Barwick (Cornell University), Hongyuan Xia, Tianli Xia
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34977); featured VoxChina July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34977
**Abstract:** This paper examines China's transformation in pharmaceutical innovation from a "free rider" on global drug development to a global leader in clinical trial activity. In 2010, China accounted for less than 8% of worldwide clinical trials; by 2020, it had surpassed the United States in annual registered clinical trial volume. To study this transformation, the authors compile a comprehensive, synchronized database spanning the pharmaceutical drug development supply chain — covering scientific publications, clinical trials, drug development milestones for China, the U.S., and Europe, alongside drug sales and government policies. Using this database, the authors identify the 2017 National Reimbursement Drug List (NRDL) reform as the primary driver. The NRDL enabled the government to negotiate steep price reductions in exchange for guaranteed reimbursement coverage and near-universal patient access, dramatically expanding the effective market size for innovative drugs. The reform is followed by an 86% increase in trial quantity and heightened novelty, concentrated among reform-exposed disease categories and domestic pharmaceutical firms. A decomposition analysis reveals the NRDL accounts for 43% of oncology trial growth, substantially exceeding contributions from knowledge accumulation and talent flows (24% combined). A structural model implies that dynamic welfare gains from induced innovation exceed the reform's static gains from expanded drug access by a factor of three. The findings offer evidence that demand-side market expansion policies can be a potent instrument for stimulating pharmaceutical innovation in emerging economies.

## 5. Intergenerational Mobility in Late Qing Dynasty: Evidence from Northeast China
**Authors:** Kristina Butaeva, Steven N. Durlauf (University of Chicago), Alexander Shapoval
**Date:** June 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35313)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35313
**Abstract:** This paper examines intergenerational class mobility patterns during China's late Qing era, using historical administrative data from Liaoning province in Northeast China. The authors identify two qualitatively distinct epochs of mobility dynamics. Prior to approximately 1850, mobility patterns were stable and exhibited convergence toward a steady state, consistent with a society in which class status was reproduced with regularity. Beginning around 1850, coinciding with the Opium Wars and the Taiping Rebellion, intergenerational class dynamics became unstable and failed to converge. A paradox emerges from the aggregate data: while overall intergenerational mobility increased over the full period studied, this aggregate increase masked a simultaneous decline in upward mobility alongside a rise in downward mobility — a divergence that the authors argue is consistent with theoretical models linking distributional mobility patterns to conditions of political instability. The findings contribute to the historical economics literature on long-run inequality dynamics and provide evidence on the social consequences of dynastic collapse and external military shocks. They also speak to broader debates about whether greater mobility reflects genuine improvements in opportunity or is better understood as increased downward risk for incumbent classes during periods of political disruption.
