# China Economics Papers — 2026-07-17

## 1. Do Industrial Policies Improve Efficiency? Evidence From China's Credit Policies
**Authors:** Kaiji Chen (Emory University), Yuxuan Huang, Xuewen Liu (Hong Kong University of Science and Technology), Zhikun Lu, Yong Wang
**Date:** 2026 (Journal of International Economics, Vol. 161, Article 104238; VoxChina, July 14, 2026)
**Source:** https://voxchina.org/show-3-467.html
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether industrial policies that selectively direct preferential credit toward high-markup sectors enhance or distort aggregate economic efficiency in China. Using firm-level data spanning 2009–2020 and a quantitative multi-sector general equilibrium model, the authors assess the allocative consequences of preferential credit targeting. The central finding is that credit policies reallocate capital toward underfunded high-markup activities, improving aggregate efficiency even as they weaken firm selection — a finding that stands in contrast to the standard view that credit subsidies primarily distort resource allocation. Counterfactual analysis shows that removing preferential credit policies would nearly double aggregate productivity losses, from approximately 2.9 percent to 5.5 percent of GDP, because the sectors benefiting most from credit support are precisely those where private credit markets underinvest. The mechanism runs through markup dispersion: in the presence of persistent markup heterogeneity across sectors, targeted credit policies can raise TFP by directing resources toward sectors where social returns to capital exceed private returns. The results suggest that evaluating China's industrial credit policies requires attention to the interaction between credit market imperfections and product-market distortions, and that blanket removal of preferential lending could reduce rather than improve aggregate efficiency in an economy with pervasive markup heterogeneity.

## 2. Heterogeneous Diffusion of Electric Vehicles in China: Demand, Learning, Product Entry, and the Incidence of Industrial Policy
**Authors:** Yu (Jasmine) Hao (Peking University), Jinge Li
**Date:** June 26, 2026 (arXiv:2606.27924)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27924
**Abstract:** This paper studies China's dramatic expansion in electric vehicle adoption — from approximately 1 percent of new car sales in 2015 to 45 percent in 2024 — using an equilibrium structural model that integrates consumer demand, dynamic learning-by-doing, endogenous product entry, and government subsidy policy. The authors exploit rich variation in EV prices, model availability, and subsidy generosity across Chinese cities and over time to identify and decompose the sources of EV diffusion. Decomposition of the aggregate sales growth reveals that product-quality improvements account for the largest share of EV adoption (approximately 45 percent), followed by expansion of the product choice set (nearly 15 percent) and battery-cost decline (around 8 percent). Subsidy policies themselves account for a smaller share of adoption, but the subsidy incidence analysis finds that each yuan of 2024 subsidies generated 3.38 yuan in private consumer surplus — substantially above one — reflecting the subsidy's role in overcoming informational barriers and network externalities that would otherwise suppress adoption. The welfare gains from subsidies are geographically heterogeneous, with less affluent and less urbanized regions experiencing smaller surplus gains relative to subsidy cost, raising questions about the distributional consequences of EV industrial policy in China. The paper contributes a unified framework for evaluating the relative importance of demand-side, supply-side, and policy factors in China's EV transition.

## 3. Beyond Mean Exposure: How Exposure Variance Shapes Air Pollution Mortality Risk
**Authors:** Bissan Ghaddar, Kimberly Singer Babiarz, Hongbin Li (Stanford University), Lingsheng Meng, Lynn Hildemann, Han Hong, Alvise Scarabosio, Meili Wang, Aprajit Mahajan, Peng Yin, Maigeng Zhou, Grant Miller
**Date:** July 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35428)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35428
**Abstract:** Standard epidemiological studies of air pollution health effects rely on temporally aggregated exposure measures — typically daily or weekly average PM2.5 concentrations — in regression models of mortality outcomes. This paper demonstrates that such aggregation obscures an important dimension of exposure: the variance of within-period pollution fluctuations. Using hourly air pollution data matched with daily vital statistics from 251 Chinese cities over four years, the authors document that mortality risk depends not only on mean exposure levels but on the distribution of exposure across hours within an aggregation window. The relationship between pollution variance and mortality follows a nonlinear pattern that depends on the mean exposure level: at low average concentrations (where the dose-response curve is locally convex), higher within-period variance increases predicted mortality; at high average concentrations (where the curve is locally concave), greater variance is associated with reduced mortality risk. The practical magnitude of these variance effects is substantial — at a 7-day mean concentration of 150 μg/m³, predicted mortality varies more across different plausible hourly distributions than across a 100 μg/m³ difference in mean exposure — a difference large enough to affect both the estimation of health damages from air pollution and the design of air quality regulatory standards. The findings imply that temporal aggregation of pollution data can substantially bias health damage estimates, and that pollution control strategies targeting peak hourly concentrations can generate welfare gains beyond what mean-level reductions alone would achieve.

## 4. Exposure Without Persuasion: Ideology, Regulation, and Audience Choice in Chinese Television
**Authors:** Bei Qin (Academia Sinica), Steven Pai Xu (University of Hong Kong)
**Date:** January 2026 (Working Paper; presented at NBER Chinese Economy Working Group Meeting, Spring 2026, March 19–20, 2026)
**Source:** https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f232404/f232404.pdf
**Abstract:** This paper studies how government regulations shape ideological content in commercial media markets and how audiences respond, using Chinese television dramas before and after the tightening of content regulation that began in 2014. The authors use machine-learning methods to construct two compatible measures of ideology — political alignment and commercial orientation — and combine them with detailed viewership data to distinguish between ideology exposure (what audiences are offered) and ideology intake (exposure weighted by actual viewing). Before 2014, television and online dramas were similar in their ideological profiles. After regulation tightened, television content shifted away from commercial orientation and toward political alignment relative to online platforms, with the two ideological dimensions moving together rather than offsetting each other. These changes substantially altered what viewers were offered but not what they chose to consume: exposure to political ideology rose by about 4 percent while exposure to commercial ideology fell by a similar amount, yet ideology intake dropped sharply — by roughly 18 percent for commercial content and 16 percent for political content — reflecting widespread audience avoidance, particularly in wealthier prefectures. The paper further shows that regulatory tightening coincided with rising market concentration after 2013, as the number of producers declined and the market shares of leading and government-affiliated investors stabilized. Government investors, particularly television networks, supplied systematically more political content, reinforcing ideological control through market power rather than persuasion. The results suggest that state media regulation in China has succeeded in altering the ideological supply of television content but has not translated this supply-side shift into commensurate changes in audience consumption, calling into question the persuasive effectiveness of China's television content control strategy.

## 5. The Geopolitics of Industrial Policy: Evidence from Export Credits
**Authors:** Adrien Matray (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta; CEPR), Karsten Müller (National University of Singapore; CEPR), Zekai Shen (Bocconi University), Chenzi Xu (University of California, Berkeley; CEPR)
**Date:** 2026 (Working Paper; presented at NBER Chinese Economy Working Group Meeting, Spring 2026, March 19–20, 2026)
**Source:** https://chenzi-xu.com/
**Abstract:** This paper examines how governments use officially supported export credits — subsidized lending to foreign buyers of domestic exports — as instruments of economic statecraft and industrial policy. Export credits are among the most widely deployed tools of modern industrial policy, yet their geopolitical determinants have received limited empirical scrutiny. Using a comprehensive dataset on official export credits extended by OECD members and China, the authors document that geopolitical alignment is a powerful predictor of credit allocation: countries receiving more export credits from a given lender are systematically more aligned with that lender geopolitically, even after controlling for trade flows, borrower creditworthiness, and exporter industry composition. A quasi-experimental design exploiting quasi-random rotations of countries onto the United Nations Security Council confirms a causal geopolitical channel: recipient countries experience sharp inflows of export credits from permanent Security Council members upon rotating onto the Council. China's position in this market has become especially prominent: China has provided more export credits than all OECD countries combined in recent years, and China's credit allocation exhibits geopolitical targeting patterns that are at least as strong as those of OECD lenders. The authors document that geopolitical motives among OECD export credit agencies have increased over time and now approach the intensity observed among China's state-directed lenders. The findings characterize officially supported export credits as a first-order tool of economic statecraft in the modern geoeconomic competition between China and the West, with implications for international trade finance, the design of industrial policies, and the evolving fragmentation of the global financial system.
