## 1. China's Global Ownership

**Authors:** Jennie Bai, Luc Laeven, Yaojun Ke, and Hong Ru
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper No. 35106 — https://www.nber.org/papers/w35106
**Abstract:** This paper examines Chinese corporate investments abroad using a comprehensive micro-level dataset of 161,773 firms across 159 countries from 2012–2021. The authors reconstruct multi-layered ownership chains through offshore tax havens to identify ultimate beneficial owners, revealing a global footprint substantially larger than traditional FDI statistics suggest. Chinese-controlled foreign assets grew at 20% annually, totaling $2.1 trillion — approximately 3% of global corporate assets — by 2021. State-owned enterprises strategically acquired R&D-intensive and supply-chain-linked firms. Following acquisition, target companies expanded capital stock and R&D spending but experienced stagnant patent output and declining profitability. An "innovation spillback" occurred: Chinese parent companies saw accelerated patent growth following developed-economy acquisitions, while target firms showed no improvement. Increased Chinese ownership also crowded out R&D at competing non-target firms. The authors conclude that China represents a "state-driven model" prioritizing long-term technological capacity building over immediate performance gains at acquired entities.

## 2. Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy

**Authors:** Ignacio Banares-Sanchez, Robin Burgess, Dávid László, Pol Simpson, John Van Reenen, and Yifan Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper No. 34893 — https://www.nber.org/papers/w34893
**Abstract:** This paper asks whether industrial policies that promote clean energy can simultaneously increase a country's growth and welfare while reducing carbon emissions. The authors examine Chinese solar subsidies implemented by city-regions from 2004–2020, exploiting new city- and firm-level panel data on solar policies, patenting, and output. They find that production and innovation subsidies were more effective than demand-side (installation) subsidies in generating large and persistent increases in local innovation, net entry, production, and exports. A structural model reveals that local policy effects explain 40–50% of aggregate changes in solar innovation, global solar prices, and revenues. Social benefits to Chinese citizens exceeded subsidy costs by 65%, doubling when environmental benefits are included. Innovation subsidies are found to be the most cost-effective instrument overall. The paper provides among the first credible empirical evidence that green industrial policy can deliver growth and welfare gains alongside emissions reductions.

## 3. Decoding China's Industrial Policies

**Authors:** Hanming Fang, Ming Li, and Guangli Lu
**Date:** May 2025 (revised 2026)
**Source:** NBER Working Paper No. 33814 — https://www.nber.org/papers/w33814
**Abstract:** This paper constructs a novel large-scale dataset of China's industrial policies by applying large language models (LLMs) to approximately 3 million official documents issued by central, provincial, and municipal governments from 2000 to 2022. The methodology classifies policy documents and extracts structured information on policy objectives, targeted industries, policy tones (supportive vs. regulatory/suppressive), policy tools, implementation mechanisms, and intergovernmental relationships. Combining this policy dataset with firm-level business data, the paper addresses four questions: the economic and political foundations of targeted industries; how policy tools vary across government levels, regions, and industry development phases; the impacts of policies on firm behavior (entry, production, productivity growth); and the political economy dimensions of industrial policy, including top-down transmission, policy persistence, and regional diffusion. The paper documents potential inefficiencies including excess industrial capacity and identifies systematic differences in how central and local governments deploy policy instruments.

## 4. The Chinese Current Account Imbalances: Puzzles, Patterns, and Possible Causes

**Authors:** Chang Ma and Shang-Jin Wei
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper No. 35056 — https://www.nber.org/papers/w35056
**Abstract:** This paper examines why China maintains a substantial and persistent current account surplus despite trading partner pressure and concerns about global imbalances. While sector-specific policies create imbalances in targeted industries, the authors find they contribute minimally to the overall current account surplus. Instead, the research identifies structural factors — including demographic trends and differential financing access across firm types — as primary drivers of the surplus. The authors note that macroeconomic stimulus can boost imports and reduce the surplus in the short run, but argue that sustainable solutions require addressing the underlying structural challenges. The paper uses Chinese firm and macro data to decompose the sources of the surplus and to evaluate the effectiveness of various proposed policy remedies.

## 5. AI Patents in the United States and China: Measurement, Organization, and Knowledge Flows

**Authors:** Hanming Fang, Xian Gu, Hanyin Yan, and Wu Zhu
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper No. 35022 — https://www.nber.org/papers/w35022
**Abstract:** This paper develops an improved machine-learning classifier to identify artificial intelligence patents, achieving 97.0% precision, 91.3% recall, and a 94.0% F1 score. Analyzing U.S. patents from 1976–2023 and Chinese patents from 2010–2023, the authors document rapid AI patenting growth in both nations with similar intensities and subfields, though China recently surpassed the U.S. in annual patent counts. However, the organizational structures of innovation differ significantly: American AI innovation concentrates among large private corporations and established technology hubs, while Chinese innovation displays greater geographic dispersion involving universities and state enterprises. AI patents command market-value premiums for listed firms in both countries. Cross-border citation patterns indicate ongoing technological interdependence rather than separation, with Chinese inventors drawing more heavily on U.S. developments than the reverse. The findings suggest decoupling in AI knowledge flows remains incomplete despite geopolitical tensions.
