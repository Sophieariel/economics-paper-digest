# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-04-30

## 1. Gender-Specific Economic Shocks and Household Bargaining Power [Gender]
**Authors:** Rania Gihleb, Osea Giuntella, and Dor Morag
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35118
**Abstract:** This paper studies how gender-specific labor market shocks reshape intra-household allocation, by combining machine learning with product-level consumption data on single men and single women to construct a new index that "quantifies the 'gendered' nature of consumer goods." Using this index, the authors examine spending responses within heterosexual households to two large shocks with opposite gender incidence: industrial robot adoption, which worsened men's relative economic position, and the expansion of fracking, which boosted demand for young and less-skilled men. They find that robot exposure shifted household consumption toward products predominantly purchased by single women, while fracking yields "suggestive but less robust evidence of increased spending on goods favored by single men." Although neither shock significantly altered total spending on children's products, robot exposure led to a reallocation of spending toward goods more commonly purchased for daughters than for sons. The results provide novel revealed-preference evidence that local labor-demand shocks alter bargaining power within households and have gendered downstream effects on the consumption of women, men, and children — directly relevant to the literatures on the gender wage gap, household economics, and the labor-market implications of automation. NBER Working Paper No. 35118. Programs: Children and Families; Labor Studies.

## 2. Early Childcare Attendance and Cognitive Skills in Adolescence [Education]
**Authors:** Ingvild Almås, Nina Drange, Costas Meghir, and Henrik D. Zachrisson
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35109
**Abstract:** This paper estimates the long-run effects of starting publicly subsidized childcare one year earlier on children's academic skills in adolescence, exploiting a 2003 Norwegian reform that lowered the age threshold for guaranteed childcare access at ages 1–2 and that staggered the rollout of new slots across municipalities. Linking the policy variation to administrative registry data on test scores in grades 5 and 9, the authors find that "starting childcare one year earlier increases math scores by 9.7% of a standard deviation (SD) in grade 9." The gains are highly concentrated among disadvantaged children: those whose mothers do not hold a high school diploma benefit by 28% of a SD in grade 9, "reducing the math achievement gap from children of higher-educated mothers by about one third," and children of immigrants also show strong improvements. The findings contribute new long-horizon evidence on returns to early human-capital investment, school readiness, and the design of universal childcare and early-childhood policy in advanced economies. NBER Working Paper No. 35109. Programs: Children and Families; Economics of Education; Public Economics.

## 3. The Timing of School Exclusions and Its Consequences for Peers' Outcomes [Education]
**Authors:** Richard Dorsett, Veruska Oppedisano, Dave Thomson, and Min Zhang
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18599
**Abstract:** This paper studies how the timing of permanent school exclusions of disruptive pupils affects the educational and labor-market outcomes of their classmates, using English administrative data linking pupil-level school records to subsequent education and employment outcomes. Removing disruptive pupils may benefit classmates, but "delays in exclusion can impose costs" through prolonged peer exposure to disruption. The authors exploit exogenous variation in local capacity constraints as an instrument for exclusion timing within schools. They find that Year 9 exclusions generate the largest negative peer spillovers: "an additional excluded pupil per 1,000 reduces GCSE Maths and English scores by 0.024 and 0.044 standard deviations" respectively, lowers Level 2 and Level 3 attainment by roughly 0.6 percentage points, and raises the probability of being NEET at age 21 by 0.62 percentage points. Earlier exclusions are associated with improved labor-market outcomes for peers, while later exclusions impose broader losses concentrated among disadvantaged pupils, with the mechanism linked to accumulated disruption prior to exclusion. The findings speak directly to debates over school discipline, peer effects, and human-capital formation. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18599. JEL: C36, I2, I20, J24.

## 4. Effects of Expanding Contraceptive Choice: New Evidence from Virginia's Contraceptive Access Initiative [Fertility]
**Authors:** Jessica H. Kiser, Analisa Packham, Janelle Anthony, Evelyn Escobar, and Emily Yeatts
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35108
**Abstract:** This paper evaluates the fertility and contraceptive-take-up effects of Virginia's 2018 Contraceptive Access Initiative (CAI), a state-level program designed to expand access to long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs). The authors combine encounter-level data on contraceptive choice in participating CAI clinics with county-level natality records from 2014–2021 to estimate "relative changes in LARC take-up and childbearing rates before and after the CAI." Using a difference-in-differences design comparing participating and non-participating counties, they find that "the CAI reduced birth rates in participating counties by approximately 3 percent, or less than half of the effect size of other similar, state-level programs." The smaller-than-expected effect is attributed to relatively high pre-existing LARC take-up and substantial substitution from other contraceptive methods. The paper contributes empirical evidence on the design, costs, and marginal effectiveness of public contraceptive-access programs, with direct implications for fertility decisions, female labor supply, and the role of reproductive-health policy in shaping women's labor-market trajectories. NBER Working Paper No. 35108. Programs: Children and Families; Economics of Health.

## 5. Does Employment Slow Cognitive Decline? Evidence from Labor Market Shocks [Health]
**Authors:** Noah Arman Kouchekinia, David Neumark, and Tim A. Bruckner
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35117
**Abstract:** This paper provides causal evidence on whether continued employment slows the pace of cognitive decline among older U.S. workers — an outcome of growing importance as life expectancy rises and "the population share of disability due to cognitive decline and dementia has substantially increased." Many older Americans leave the workforce well before age 65, and prior correlational evidence has linked earlier exits to faster cognitive aging. Using Health and Retirement Study (HRS) data linked to local labor-market conditions, the authors construct a Bartik-style instrument that exploits "plausibly exogenous shifts in labor demand in local labor markets" to identify variation in employment that is unrelated to individual health. They find "substantial declines over time in cognitive scores stemming from negative labor demand shocks." The estimated effects are concentrated among men aged 51–64, "whose employment decisions and outcomes may be more sensitive to local labor market conditions than are these decisions or outcomes for women or for older men." The evidence extends past work focused on the retirement-age window and supports the view that working to older ages may delay age-related cognitive decline, with direct implications for retirement policy, disability insurance, and the design of labor-market interventions for older workers. NBER Working Paper No. 35117. Programs: Economics of Aging; Economics of Health; Labor Studies; Public Economics.
