# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-01

## 1. Missing Men and Women's Demand for Political Representation [Gender]
**Authors:** Barbara Boelmann and Carola Stapper
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18587
**Abstract:** This paper provides causal evidence on how the absence of men shapes women's demand for political representation, exploiting the male conscription shock of World War I in Germany as a natural experiment. The authors construct a novel revealed-preference measure of women's political engagement based on the local presence and persistence of suffragette clubs, and link this to administratively recorded male conscription rates and women's wartime relief activity. They find that "women were more inclined to maintain organizational activity in regions experiencing greater male conscription," with effects strongest in localities where women took on substantial wartime relief responsibilities. Leadership experience accumulated during male absence emerges as a key channel: women who gained organizational and managerial roles during the war were more likely to push for enfranchisement. The effects extend well beyond the suffrage decision itself: in areas with sustained wartime club activity, women showed higher post-enfranchisement electoral participation and greater representation among political candidates. The paper contributes to the literatures on gender and leadership, the formation of political preferences, and the long-run labor-market and political consequences of large gender-specific shocks. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18587. JEL: J16, N44, D71.

## 2. Economic Incentives or Social Norms? Labor Supply Differentials Between East and West German Mothers [Gender]
**Authors:** Bastien Chabé-Ferret, Zainab Iftikhar, and JungJae Park
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18600
**Abstract:** This paper quantifies the relative contributions of social norms and economic incentives to the persistent 350-hour annual gap in maternal labor supply between East and West Germany, more than three decades after reunification. The authors build a structural model of family formation and female labor supply estimated on GSOEP household data from 2000–2017, in which East- and West-German mothers face different wage profiles, child-care conditions, and gender-role norms. Decomposing the gap, they find that "the working-mother stigma accounts for 73 percent of the gap," while higher Western wages partially offset the stigma effect by drawing some West-German mothers into the labor market. Counterfactual policy simulations show that subsidizing maternal employment or expanding child-care access can widen East-West differences in the short run by interacting with diverging norms, and that such policies become largely ineffective once the stigma fades. The paper contributes empirical and quantitative evidence to the literatures on the gender wage gap, maternal labor supply, the cultural transmission of gender norms, and the design of family policy. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18600. JEL: J01, J08, J12, J13, J22.

## 3. Diversity and Access in Academic Finance Seminars [Gender]
**Authors:** Marina Gertsberg
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18603
**Abstract:** This paper provides new empirical evidence on gender and access to elite networks in economics-adjacent academia by analyzing 8,744 external research seminars across 74 U.S. finance departments from 2010 to 2024. Using hand-collected and scraped data linked to authors' publication and citation records, the author documents that "female representation increased from 10% to 25%" of seminar speakers over the sample period, outpacing the rate at which women entered finance faculty. At the same time, seminar opportunities remain highly selective and concentrated: presenters have substantially more publications, top-3 publications, and citations than same-institution faculty, and "the top 10% of presenters [account] for 43% of all talks." Seminar matching follows a strong hierarchical structure in which elite-affiliated and senior researchers reach broader geographic audiences, while access for early-career and non-elite-affiliated scholars is far more constrained. The paper speaks directly to the literatures on gender and leadership, occupational segregation in high-skill labor markets, and the role of professional networks in academic career advancement. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18603. JEL: I23, J16, J44, J71.

## 4. Undersupply or Lack of Demand? Evaluating Measures of Child Care Access [Fertility]
**Authors:** Jessica H. Brown
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18597
**Abstract:** This paper provides new empirical evidence on whether widely used measures of child-care access actually identify markets with genuine supply shortages, with direct implications for fertility decisions, maternal labor supply, and the design of child-care subsidies. Combining administrative provider-level data on licensed slots, enrollment, and vacancies with detailed local demographic information, the author tests whether "child-care deserts" — typically defined as census tracts with three or more young children per licensed slot — predict tight access conditions on the ground. She finds that desert status only weakly predicts low vacancy rates, while alternative measures that adjust for local demand patterns and household demographics correlate substantially more strongly with vacancies. The results imply that some areas genuinely face market frictions in child-care supply, but that conventional desert metrics misclassify many high-demand low-supply markets and over-flag others where parents simply do not seek formal care. The paper contributes to the literatures on early-childhood policy, female labor supply, and the targeting of child-care subsidies and supply-side interventions. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18597. JEL: J13, H75, H54.

## 5. Neighborhood Disorder and Dementia Risk in U.S. Older Adults: The Role of Cardiometabolic Risk [Health]
**Authors:** Jiao Yu, Yi Wang, Thomas M. Gill, and Xi Chen
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18581
**Abstract:** This paper provides new empirical evidence on how the social and physical environment shapes cognitive aging among older Americans, an outcome of growing importance for late-career labor supply, retirement, and disability policy. Using longitudinal Health and Retirement Study (HRS) data from 2006–2020 linked to interviewer-rated and administrative measures of neighborhood disorder, the authors estimate the effect of exposure to visible signs of community decay — vandalism, vacant buildings, and litter — on the subsequent incidence of dementia. They find that "exposure to visible neighborhood disorder is associated with higher risk of dementia (Hazard Ratio: 1.37; 95% CI: 1.08–1.74)," with effects robust to extensive controls for individual demographics, socio-economic status, and baseline health. A formal mediation analysis identifies cardiometabolic dysregulation as a key biological pathway, accounting for roughly 16% of the disorder–dementia link. The findings imply that "community interventions that simultaneously reduce visible signs of neighborhood decay and address cardiometabolic risk may yield dementia-prevention dividends," with direct implications for late-life labor supply, disability insurance, and the spatial distribution of health and human-capital outcomes. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18581. JEL: I12, I14, J14, R23.
