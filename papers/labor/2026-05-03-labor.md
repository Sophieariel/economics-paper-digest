# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-03

## 1. Gender-Specific Economic Shocks and Household Bargaining Power [Gender]
**Authors:** Rania Gihleb, Osea Giuntella, Dor Morag
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35118
**Abstract:** This paper uses machine learning on product-level consumption data from single men and women to construct an index measuring the gendered nature of consumer goods, then examines how gender-specific labor market shocks reshape household spending patterns. Industrial robot adoption — which hurt men's relative economic position — shifted household consumption toward products predominantly purchased by single women, consistent with a bargaining-power channel. Fracking expansion, which improved the relative position of young less-skilled men, yields suggestive but less robust evidence of increased spending on male-favored goods. Neither shock substantially altered children's product spending overall, though robot exposure reallocated resources toward daughter-focused goods. The identification strategy leverages the geographic rollout of robotics and fracking as natural experiments, using local labor market exposure as an instrument for gender-specific wage shocks. The findings provide causal evidence that within-household resource allocation responds to shifts in relative earnings, with implications for the welfare of women and children in deindustrializing labor markets. NBER Working Paper No. 35118. JEL: J16, D12, J22.

## 2. Effects of Expanding Contraceptive Choice: New Evidence from Virginia's Contraceptive Access Initiative [Fertility]
**Authors:** Jessica H. Kiser, Analisa Packham, Janelle Anthony, Evelyn Escobar, Emily Yeatts
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35108
**Abstract:** This paper evaluates Virginia's 2018 Contraceptive Access Initiative (CAI), which removed cost and visit barriers to long-acting reversible contraception (LARC) at participating Title X clinics, using a difference-in-differences design that compares encounter-level clinic data and county natality records across participating and non-participating counties from 2014 to 2021. The CAI reduced birth rates in participating counties by approximately 3 percent. The effect is driven primarily by substitution from less effective to more effective contraceptive methods among existing users rather than by net increases in contraceptive uptake, reflecting already-high baseline adoption rates at Title X facilities. The results underscore that even in settings with broad contraceptive access, reducing friction around the most effective methods can meaningfully lower fertility rates. The paper contributes to the literatures on contraceptive policy, fertility, and women's reproductive autonomy, and carries direct policy relevance for the design of publicly funded family planning programs. NBER Working Paper No. 35108. JEL: J13, I18, H75.

## 3. Early Childcare Attendance and Cognitive Skills in Adolescence [Education]
**Authors:** Ingvild Almås, Nina Drange, Costas Meghir, Henrik D. Zachrisson
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35109
**Abstract:** This paper estimates the long-run cognitive returns to early childcare enrollment using a natural experiment generated by a 2003 Norwegian policy expansion that created quasi-random variation in childcare slot availability for one- and two-year-olds. Beginning childcare one year earlier raises grade 9 math scores by 9.7 percent of a standard deviation. Heterogeneity analysis reveals especially large gains among the most disadvantaged children: children of mothers without a high school diploma see scores improve by 28 percent of a standard deviation, narrowing the socioeconomic achievement gap by roughly one-third, and children of immigrants also demonstrate substantial gains. Effects are weaker in grade 5, suggesting that early childcare benefits compound over time. The findings provide strong causal evidence for the long-run human capital returns to universal early childhood education, with particular implications for intergenerational mobility and the design of early childhood policy in developed economies. NBER Working Paper No. 35109. JEL: I21, J13, J24.

## 4. Does Employment Slow Cognitive Decline? Evidence from Labor Market Shocks [Health]
**Authors:** Noah Arman Kouchekinia, David Neumark, Tim A. Bruckner
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35117
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether continued workforce participation delays cognitive decline among older Americans, using plausibly exogenous shifts in labor demand derived from a Bartik shift-share instrument to instrument for individual employment in U.S. Health and Retirement Study data. Negative local labor demand shocks generate substantial and persistent declines in cognitive test scores, particularly among men aged 51–64, consistent with the hypothesis that employment provides cognitively stimulating activity that retards decline. The effects are concentrated in respondents with below-median cognitive reserve at baseline, suggesting complementarity between employment and cognitive maintenance for more vulnerable workers. The paper contributes to a growing literature on the non-pecuniary costs of job loss and the health consequences of labor demand fluctuations, with implications for retirement policy, disability insurance, and the labor market consequences of automation and trade shocks. NBER Working Paper No. 35117. JEL: I12, J14, J26.

## 5. The Welfare Effects of Protecting Older Workers [Health]
**Authors:** Todd Morris, Stefan Staubli, Benoit Dostie
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35111
**Abstract:** This paper estimates the welfare effects of mandatory retirement bans using the staggered provincial adoption of age-discrimination legislation across Canadian provinces between 2005 and 2009 as a natural experiment, exploiting linked employer-employee tax data matched to mortality records. The bans sharply reduce retirements at age 65, raising employment and earnings by at least 14 percent post-65. The fiscal effects are positive: extended employment boosts tax revenues and pension contributions while reducing public transfer receipt. Workers show no elevated mortality, and younger workers experience no displacement, allaying two key concerns about such policies. The paper provides the most comprehensive welfare accounting of mandatory retirement bans to date, with direct implications for pension system sustainability, age-discrimination law, and the design of late-career labor market institutions in ageing economies. NBER Working Paper No. 35111. JEL: J14, J26, H55.
