# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-04

## 1. Gender-Specific Economic Shocks and Household Bargaining Power [Gender]

**Authors:** Rania Gihleb, Osea Giuntella, Dor Morag
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper 35118
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35118
**Method:** Natural experiments (industrial robot adoption and fracking expansion as exogenous labor demand shocks)
**NBER Programs:** Children and Families; Labor Studies

**Abstract:** This paper constructs an index measuring the degree to which consumer goods are gender-typed, using machine learning and product-level data. The authors examine how gender-specific labor market shocks affect household consumption patterns as a window into household bargaining power. They find that industrial robot adoption — which reduced men's relative economic position — shifted household spending toward products typically purchased by single women. Fracking expansion, which benefited young less-skilled men, showed weaker evidence of the opposite effect. Neither shock substantially changed total children's product spending, though robot exposure reallocated spending toward daughters' goods relative to sons'. The results are consistent with models in which improvements in women's outside options increase their bargaining power within the household.

## 2. Does Employment Slow Cognitive Decline? Evidence from Labor Market Shocks [Health]

**Authors:** Noah Arman Kouchekinia, David Neumark, Tim A. Bruckner
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper 35117
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35117
**Method:** Instrumental variables (Bartik shift-share instrument for local labor demand)
**NBER Programs:** Economics of Aging; Economics of Health; Labor Studies; Public Economics

**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether workforce participation delays cognitive deterioration. Using data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and exploiting plausibly exogenous shifts in labor demand in local labor markets as a Bartik instrument, the authors find that negative labor demand shocks are associated with substantial declines over time in cognitive scores. Effects concentrate among men aged 51–64, suggesting that working longer may postpone age-related cognitive decline. The findings have implications for retirement policy and highlight the non-pecuniary benefits of employment for older workers.

## 3. Early Childcare Attendance and Cognitive Skills in Adolescence [Education]

**Authors:** Ingvild Almås, Nina Drange, Costas Meghir, Henrik D. Zachrisson
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper 35109
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35109
**Method:** Natural experiment (quasi-random variation in childcare slot availability from a 2003 Norwegian policy expansion)
**NBER Programs:** Children and Families; Economics of Education; Public Economics

**Abstract:** This paper analyzes the long-run effect of early childcare attendance on academic performance in grades 5 and 9 in Norway. Exploiting a 2003 childcare expansion that created quasi-random variation in slot availability for children aged 1–2, the authors find that starting childcare one year earlier increases math scores by 9.7% of a standard deviation in grade 9. The benefits are particularly pronounced for disadvantaged groups: children of mothers without high school diplomas gain 28% of a standard deviation, reducing achievement gaps by roughly one-third. Similar positive effects appear for children of immigrants. The results suggest that high-quality universal childcare can be an effective early-life equalizer with lasting educational returns.

## 4. Effects of Expanding Contraceptive Choice: New Evidence from Virginia's Contraceptive Access Initiative [Fertility] [Health]

**Authors:** Jessica H. Kiser, Analisa Packham, Janelle Anthony, Evelyn Escobar, Emily Yeatts
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper 35108
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35108
**Method:** Difference-in-differences (comparing participating vs. non-participating counties before and after the 2018 CAI rollout)
**NBER Programs:** Children and Families; Economics of Health

**Abstract:** This paper examines Virginia's 2018 Contraceptive Access Initiative (CAI), a program designed to increase access to long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) in public health clinics. Using clinic encounter data and county natality statistics from 2014–2021, the authors employ a difference-in-differences design and find that the initiative reduced birth rates in participating counties by approximately 3 percent. The effect is attributed to high LARC take-up among existing contraceptive users (substitution) rather than expansion to previously unserved populations. The findings contribute to the literature on contraceptive access policy and its downstream effects on fertility outcomes.

## 5. Adolescent Behavior, Learning, and Knowledge Diffusion: Evidence from a Randomized Experiment [Education]

**Authors:** Sule Alan, Kumar Biswas, Christina S. Hauser, Shwetlena Sabarwal
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper 35160
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35160
**Method:** Randomized controlled trial (RCT) across 127 middle schools in Bangladesh
**NBER Programs:** Development Economics; Economics of Education

**Abstract:** This paper evaluates a school-based program in 127 Bangladesh middle schools reaching over 7,500 adolescents that shifts responsibility for behavioral norm-setting from teachers to students themselves. The intervention significantly improved classroom social climate, fostering stronger cooperation, better behavioral norms, and more supportive peer networks. High-performing students gained the most academically, and these gains persisted 1.5 years later, driven by enhanced academic support networks among higher-ability peers. The study documents knowledge diffusion through peer networks as a mechanism, providing evidence that student-led behavioral interventions can have durable effects on learning outcomes.
