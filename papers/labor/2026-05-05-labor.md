# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-05

## 1. High Schools and the Uneven Rise in American Opportunity [Education]
**Authors:** Alison Doxey, Ezra Karger, and Peter Nencka
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18580
**Abstract:** This paper exploits the fastest expansion of school access in U.S. history — the share of young Americans living in towns with high schools rose from 17% to 46% between 1850 and 1910 — to estimate the causal effects of secondary schooling on a broad range of economic outcomes. Using a difference-in-differences design that compares cohorts within counties before and after a high school opened, the authors link historical Census records to outcomes including school attendance, women's labor force participation, marriage timing, and intergenerational earnings. They find that opening a high school produced "sharp increases in school attendance rates for high school-aged children," with the expansion accounting for roughly one-third of women's labor force participation growth between 1870 and 1930 and contributing to delayed marriage among women. However, the gains were concentrated: effects were "largest for children from already-wealthy families" and did not materialize for Black children on average, so the High School Movement simultaneously narrowed gender gaps in the labor market while widening existing race- and class-based disparities. The paper contributes new long-run evidence on the returns to school access, the distributional consequences of education policy, and the historical roots of labor-market inequality. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18580.

## 2. Paying Disadvantaged Teenagers to Stay in School [Education]
**Authors:** Jack Britton, Nick Ridpath, Carmen Villa, and Ben Waltmann
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18575
**Abstract:** This paper provides long-run causal evidence on the labor-market effects of the Education Maintenance Allowance (EMA), a large conditional cash transfer scheme that paid low-income teenagers in England up to £30 per week to remain in education beyond the compulsory school-leaving age of 16. Exploiting the staggered geographic rollout of the EMA as a source of quasi-experimental variation and linking participants to administrative records on education, earnings, and criminal justice outcomes through age 31, the authors find "no significant overall effect of the policy on labour market outcomes or criminality." Heterogeneous effects tell a more nuanced story: high-attaining students raised their university attendance rates but did not complete degrees at higher rates, while low-attaining students "committed fewer crimes" after the program. A welfare analysis yields a Marginal Value of Public Funds of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.52–1.29), indicating that even at optimistic projections the program's benefits barely justified its costs. The findings contribute causal evidence on education-policy design, the returns to extended schooling for disadvantaged youth, and the broader social returns to conditional cash transfer programs. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18575. JEL: I21, I24, I38, J24.

## 3. Work from Home and Disability Employment [Health]
**Authors:** Nicholas Bloom, Gordon B. Dahl, and Dan-Olof Rooth
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18555
**Abstract:** There has been a dramatic rise in disability employment since the pandemic. At the same time, work from home (WFH) has risen four-fold. This paper asks whether the two are causally related. Using panel data on employment outcomes for workers with and without physical disabilities, combined with industry-level variation in the feasibility and adoption of remote work, the authors control for compositional changes and labor-market tightness to isolate the causal effect of WFH on disability employment. They find that "a 1 percentage point increase in WFH increases full-time employment by 1.0% for individuals with a physical disability," and that the postpandemic increase in working from home explains 68%–85% of the rise in full-time employment among this population. Wage data suggest that WFH increased the labor supply of workers with physical disabilities, "likely by reducing commuting costs and enabling better control of working conditions." The results have direct implications for disability and work policy, the design of workplace accommodation mandates, and the long-run consequences of the remote-work revolution for labor-market inclusion. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18555. JEL: J14, J42.

## 4. Climate Change and Workplace Injury [Health]
**Authors:** Qifan Fu and Masaru Sasaki
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18592
**Abstract:** This study comprehensively assesses the effects of exposure to temperature extremes on workplace health, safety, and economic outcomes. Using Japanese prefecture-level data on work-related injuries and fatalities from 2014–2019 combined with high-frequency weather records, the authors estimate that higher temperatures significantly increase work-related injuries and their associated social costs. A key finding is that when exposed to temperature extremes, workers neither reduce their working hours nor exit the labor force, implying that the health burden falls on workers without labor-supply adjustment serving as a buffer. Testing the compensating wage differential model reveals minimal wage increases for exposure to temperature extremes, suggesting that markets do not adequately price heat-related occupational risk. These findings highlight the need for effective policies — including heat-safety regulations, workplace cooling investments, and improved occupational health standards — to mitigate the adverse effects of temperature extremes in the workplace, and contribute to the literatures on climate and labor markets, occupational safety, and the economics of workplace health. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18592. JEL: J28, J81, Q54.

## 5. Beliefs, Attention, and Investments in Early Childhood [Education]
**Authors:** Flavio Cunha, Snejana Nihtianova, Jessica Rood, and Anja-Lize van der Merwe
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35150
**Abstract:** This paper develops and tests a model of parental investment in early childhood in which bandwidth-constrained parents hold distorted beliefs about the returns to responsive interaction with their children. When parents lack the cognitive capacity to meet their own aspirations, the model predicts that motivated reasoning provides relief: the parent distorts her working belief about the returns to responsive parenting downward, rationalizing low engagement. This distortion, in turn, prevents the accumulation of calibrating evidence that could correct the misperception, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of low beliefs, low investment, and underdevelopment of children's early human capital. The authors identify four distinct intervention channels — belief correction, attention restoration, aspiration reduction, and direct behavioral nudges — that can break this cycle, and they test the model's predictions using a randomized controlled trial. The RCT confirms significant treatment impacts on parental beliefs and responsive parenting behaviors while showing no effects on material-input or placebo outcomes, validating the model's mechanism. The paper contributes to the literatures on early-childhood investment, the formation and consequences of parental beliefs, and the design of early-childhood programs, with implications for education policy and human capital accumulation among disadvantaged children. NBER Working Paper No. 35150. Programs: Children and Families.
