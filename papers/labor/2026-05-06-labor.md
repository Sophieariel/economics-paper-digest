# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-06

## 1. Job Loss and Mental Health: The Role of Anticipation and Re-employment in Recovery Patterns [Health]
**Authors:** Olivier B. Bargain, Nicolas Herault, and Daniel Nettle
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18549
**Abstract:** This paper provides new long-run causal evidence on the mental health consequences of job loss and the role of re-employment in mitigating them, using 22 years of data (2001–2022) from the Household, Income and Labour Dynamics in Australia (HILDA) panel covering 14,195 individuals and 4,251 redundancy events. The authors exploit the panel structure to implement an event-study design that tracks mental health trajectories around involuntary job separations, comparing individuals who do and do not eventually regain employment. Three findings stand out. First, mental health declines significantly in the periods leading up to job loss—regardless of whether individuals anticipated the separation—suggesting that pre-displacement stress is a robust feature of the experience rather than a product of subjective uncertainty. Second, among those who find re-employment, mental health recovers quickly and fully, whereas those who remain jobless show persistent deterioration. Third, subjective anticipation of job loss does not significantly buffer the psychological impact, implying that pre-announcement of redundancies does little to reduce the mental health toll. These results directly inform the literature on health and labor market outcomes by documenting a strong, rapid, and path-dependent link between employment status and psychological well-being, and they have clear implications for displacement policy: early re-employment assistance, not forewarning, is the most effective lever for limiting the mental health damage of job loss. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18549. JEL: J63, I12, I31.

## 2. Migration Opportunities and Human Capital Investments [Education]
**Authors:** Esther Gehrke
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18570
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether the availability of low-skill emigration opportunities crowds out investment in domestic human capital, exploiting the staggered expansion of the U.S. Secure Communities (SC) program as an exogenous shock to migration costs for undocumented workers from Mexico. SC is a federal data-sharing initiative that substantially raised deportation risk for undocumented migrants in counties that adopted the program early; because adoption timing was determined by agreements between the Department of Homeland Security and county governments, it provides plausibly exogenous variation in the perceived return to out-migration. Linking county-level SC adoption dates to municipal-level school records in Mexico, the author finds that municipalities with stronger social-network ties to early-adopting U.S. counties experienced significant increases in school attendance, enrollment, and completed years of education following SC rollout. The results are consistent with a model in which low-skill migration and schooling are substitutes in origin communities: when the option to earn abroad as an undocumented worker becomes more costly, the expected return to domestic education rises and families shift investment toward schooling. The paper contributes causal evidence to the literature on migration and human capital formation, the returns to education in developing economies, and the unintended consequences of immigration enforcement for origin-country skill investment. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18570.

## 3. How an Unexpected Asylum Seeker Influx Affects Host Residents' Mental Health: Quasi-Experimental Evidence from South Korea [Health]
**Authors:** Seonho Shin
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18586
**Abstract:** This paper studies the mental health consequences for receiving communities of sudden, large-scale inflows of asylum seekers, providing evidence from a setting—South Korea—that has been largely absent from the English-language literature. The author exploits the quasi-random arrival of approximately 550 Yemeni asylum seekers on Jeju Island in 2018 as a natural experiment, applying a difference-in-differences design that compares Jeju Island residents to residents of comparable South Korean regions before and after the influx. Using nationally representative government health survey data, the study finds that the influx shock significantly worsened host residents' mental health outcomes, raising measured depression and anxiety rates and lowering life satisfaction. A mediation analysis identifies two channels: heightened public-safety concerns and reduced trust in government institutions. The mental health effects persist for at least two survey waves after the arrival and are concentrated among residents who reported the highest pre-event exposure to news coverage of the influx. The paper contributes to the emerging literature on the non-economic consequences of refugee and asylum-seeker arrivals for host populations, documents a mental health externality relevant to the broader literature on health and labor market outcomes (including sick leave, disability insurance take-up, and labor supply), and highlights that rapid, concentrated inflows may impose costs on receiving communities that do not appear in standard economic measures of immigration's labor market effects. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18586. JEL: I12, F22, J61.

## 4. Adolescent Behavior, Learning, and Knowledge Diffusion: Evidence from a Randomized Experiment [Education]
**Authors:** Sule Alan, Kumar Biswas, Christina S. Hauser, and Shwetlena Sabarwal
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35160
**Abstract:** This paper studies whether transferring responsibility for establishing classroom behavioral norms from teachers to students can improve learning outcomes, using a randomized controlled trial implemented across 127 middle schools in Bangladesh involving more than 7,500 students. The intervention—drawing on social-emotional learning principles—trains teachers to cede norm-setting authority to student peer groups, who then collectively establish and enforce shared behavioral standards. Treated classrooms exhibit measurable improvements in the social climate and in the density and quality of peer academic collaboration networks relative to control classrooms. A follow-up assessment 1.5 years after the program ends reveals that the improvements in classroom social environment decay, but that gains in mathematics and verbal test scores persist fully. Heterogeneous effects analysis shows that the academic gains are concentrated among higher-ability students, and the authors provide evidence that the mechanism is peer learning: high-ability students in treated classrooms form denser academic support ties with similarly skilled peers, facilitating knowledge diffusion that is absent in control schools. The results contribute to the literature on school quality and human capital formation, the design of low-cost scalable educational interventions in low- and middle-income countries, and the role of social networks in determining whether school-level inputs translate into durable learning gains. NBER Working Paper No. 35160. Programs: Development Economics; Economics of Education.

## 5. Geriatric Emergency Care, Hospitalization, and Mortality Among Older Adults in the United States [Health]
**Authors:** Yuting Qian, Cameron Gettel, Jasmine I-Shin Su, Elyssa F.L. Grogan, Inessa Cohen, Craig Rothenberg, Xi Chen, and Ula Hwang
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18556
**Abstract:** This paper provides causal evidence on whether specialized Geriatric Emergency Departments (GEDs)—emergency care units with adapted physical environments, dedicated staff training, and protocols designed for older patients—improve health outcomes relative to standard emergency departments, using Medicare claims linked to Health and Retirement Study (HRS) data on adults aged 65 and older. The authors exploit geographic variation in GED availability near older adults' residences to identify treatment effects. They find that patients who receive care at GEDs are 9.7 percentage points less likely to be subsequently hospitalized and 6.1 percentage points less likely to die within 30 days, effects that are large relative to the unconditional hospitalization and mortality rates in the sample. However, the benefits are unevenly distributed: gains are concentrated among non-Hispanic white patients and adults under age 80, while Black and Hispanic older adults show no statistically significant improvements—a pattern the authors attribute to differences in baseline access to primary care and chronic-disease management. The findings speak directly to the literature on health and labor market outcomes among older workers, where deteriorating health is a leading driver of early labor force exit and disability insurance take-up, and they have direct policy relevance for investments in age-appropriate health infrastructure as a mechanism for supporting healthy aging, prolonged labor force participation, and reduced disability claims. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18556.
