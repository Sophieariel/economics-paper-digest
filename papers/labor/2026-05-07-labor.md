# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-07

## 1. Economic Incentives or Social Norms? Labor Supply Differentials Between East and West German Mothers [Gender]
**Authors:** Bastien Chabé-Ferret, Zainab Iftikhar, JungJae Park
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18600
**Abstract:** This research examines the 350-hour annual disparity in maternal labor supply between East and West Germany. Using a collective family model estimated with GSOEP panel data spanning 2000–2017, the authors determine that the working-mother stigma accounts for 73 percent of the gap. Economic considerations provide partial counterbalance: elevated Western wages increase the opportunity cost of non-employment, such that wage equalization would substantially expand the regional difference rather than close it. The analysis reveals that conventional policy interventions may intensify regional divergence and that their success depends critically on the presence of social norms—once eliminated, such policies prove largely ineffective. The findings highlight the interplay between economic incentives and gender norms in explaining persistent maternal labor supply gaps.

## 2. Beliefs, Attention, and Investments in Early Childhood [Education]
**Authors:** Flavio Cunha, Snejana Nihtianova, Jessica Rood, Anja-Lize van der Merwe
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35150
**Abstract:** The researchers develop a theoretical framework examining how parents with limited cognitive resources form distorted beliefs about the value of responsive interaction with young children. When parents experience capacity constraints, motivated reasoning provides relief: the parent distorts her working belief downward, rationalizing low engagement. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle where limited parental engagement prevents the accumulation of evidence that would correct these false beliefs. The study identifies intervention channels that interrupt this pattern and tests model predictions through a randomized controlled trial. Results suggest that informational interventions targeting parental beliefs about the returns to engagement can improve early childhood investment and, in turn, children's developmental outcomes.

## 3. Undersupply or Lack of Demand? Evaluating Measures of Child Care Access [Fertility]
**Authors:** Jessica H. Brown
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18597
**Abstract:** The paper examines whether "child care deserts"—areas with three or more young children per licensed slot—accurately indicate supply shortages or instead reflect weak demand for formal care. The author employs center-based provider vacancy rates to assess families' ability to access care, testing correlations with desert status and two alternative metrics. The first alternative measure identifies deviations between actual and predicted supply in demographically comparable regions; the second compares licensed slots to young children with all parents working. Findings indicate that desert status weakly predicts low vacancy rates, while the alternative measures strongly predict low infant and toddler vacancy rates. Results suggest market frictions exist in child care markets and that effective policy targeting requires adjusting access thresholds for local population demographics and maternal employment rates.

## 4. How an Unexpected Asylum Seeker Influx Affects Host Residents' Mental Health: Quasi-Experimental Evidence from South Korea [Health]
**Authors:** Seonho Shin
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18586
**Abstract:** This research examines how sudden asylum seeker arrivals impact the psychological wellbeing of receiving communities, using the 2015 Yemeni asylum seeker influx to Jeju Island as a natural experiment. The study leverages South Korea's unique regional visa policies and movement restrictions to create distinct treatment and control groups. The findings indicate that the influx shock worsened host residents' mental health outcomes—with higher depression and anxiety and lower life satisfaction. The researcher identifies two key mechanisms: increased concerns about public safety and decreased confidence in governmental institutions. The analysis employs a difference-in-differences methodology applied to nationally representative government survey data, providing the first causal evidence on this topic from a non-Western context and contributing to the broader literature on immigration shocks and population mental health.

## 5. Adolescent Behavior, Learning, and Knowledge Diffusion: Evidence from a Randomized Experiment [Education]
**Authors:** Sule Alan, Kumar Biswas, Christina S. Hauser, Shwetlena Sabarwal
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35160
**Abstract:** The study evaluates a school-based program implemented in 127 Bangladesh middle schools (over 7,500 students) that shifts responsibility for behavioral norms from teachers to students. Results show improved classroom social climate and stronger peer cooperation. Higher-performing students gained significantly in math and verbal assessments. At a 1.5-year follow-up, social climate improvements fade while academic gains persist and expand to a broader set of students, particularly through enhanced academic support networks among high-ability peers. The randomized design allows for credible identification of treatment effects and heterogeneous impacts across student ability levels, contributing to the human capital literature on how peer norms and social structure shape learning outcomes in developing country contexts.
