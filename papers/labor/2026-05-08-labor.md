# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-08

## 1. Firm Pay, Amenities, and Inequality [Gender]
**Authors:** Sydnee Caldwell, Ingrid Haegele, Jörg Heining
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35149
**Abstract:** This paper estimates firm-specific amenity valuations using discrete choice experiments embedded in a large-scale survey of German workers linked to administrative records. Workers ranked hypothetical job offers with randomized wages, enabling estimation of willingness-to-pay for job amenities at the firm level. Key findings include substantial heterogeneity in amenity valuations across firms and worker demographics, and near-orthogonality between firm amenity levels and wage premia—meaning that better-paying firms do not systematically offer worse amenities, so amenities do not offset wage inequality. Importantly, gender differences in amenity preferences help explain a portion of observed gender wage gaps, with women placing higher value on certain non-wage job attributes. The paper contributes to understanding how compensating differentials and firm heterogeneity jointly shape labor market inequality by gender.

## 2. Does Employment Slow Cognitive Decline? Evidence from Labor Market Shocks [Health]
**Authors:** Noah Arman Kouchekinia, David Neumark, Tim A. Bruckner
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35117
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether continued employment protects against cognitive decline among older workers. Using longitudinal data from the Health and Retirement Study, the authors instrument for employment using local labor demand shocks, thereby addressing the endogeneity of retirement and job exit decisions. They find substantial declines over time in cognitive test scores stemming from negative labor demand shocks that push workers out of employment, with effects concentrated among men aged 51–64. The results suggest that working longer may help delay age-related cognitive decline beyond what would otherwise occur at or after typical retirement ages. The findings contribute to a growing literature on the relationship between productive engagement and health capital in the pre- and post-retirement period, with implications for policies that affect older workers' labor force attachment.

## 3. Effects of Expanding Contraceptive Choice: New Evidence from Virginia's Contraceptive Access Initiative [Fertility]
**Authors:** Jessica H. Kiser, Analisa Packham, Janelle Anthony, Evelyn Escobar, Emily Yeatts
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35108
**Abstract:** In 2018, Virginia's Department of Health launched the Contraceptive Access Initiative (CAI) to expand access to long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) through participating family-planning clinics. Using clinic encounter data and county-level natality records from 2014–2021, the authors exploit the staggered rollout of the program across counties in a difference-in-differences framework. They find that the CAI reduced birth rates in participating counties by approximately 3 percent. This effect is smaller than impacts documented for comparable state-level contraceptive access initiatives elsewhere, which the authors attribute to Virginia's pre-existing high baseline LARC take-up and substitution away from other contraceptive methods rather than net new contraceptive adoption. The paper provides new empirical evidence on how the margin of contraceptive expansion—rather than access alone—determines fertility effects of such programs.

## 4. Early Childcare Attendance and Cognitive Skills in Adolescence [Education]
**Authors:** Ingvild Almås, Nina Drange, Costas Meghir, Henrik D. Zachrisson
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35109
**Abstract:** This paper investigates the long-run effects of early childcare on cognitive skills in adolescence, using a 2003 Norwegian policy expansion that generated quasi-random variation in childcare slot availability for children aged 1–2. The authors link administrative childcare records to national school registry data covering math and reading test scores at grades 5 and 9. Starting childcare one year earlier raises math scores by 9.7 percent of a standard deviation by grade 9. Effects are substantially larger for disadvantaged groups: children of mothers without a high school diploma experience improvements of 28 percent of a standard deviation, closing roughly one-third of the achievement gap with children of more educated mothers. Children of immigrants also show strong positive outcomes. The results highlight early formal childcare as a powerful and lasting lever for human capital investment, particularly for children from less advantaged backgrounds.

## 5. Gender-Specific Economic Shocks and Household Bargaining Power [Gender]
**Authors:** Rania Gihleb, Osea Giuntella, Dor Morag
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35118
**Abstract:** This paper studies how gender-specific labor market shocks alter intrahousehold bargaining power, as revealed through household consumption patterns. The authors construct a "gender typicality" index for consumer goods using machine learning applied to scanner and product data, and then examine how shocks that differentially affect men's versus women's labor market standing shift household spending toward goods typical of the economically strengthened spouse. Industrial robot adoption—a shock that disproportionately harmed male employment—caused households to purchase significantly more female-typical goods, consistent with a shift in bargaining power toward women. Fracking expansion, which benefited young men, shows weaker evidence of movement toward male-typical goods. Neither shock substantially changed aggregate spending on children's goods, though robot exposure led to a reallocation favoring daughters' goods over sons' goods, suggesting gender-of-child effects in intra-household resource allocation. The findings illuminate how macro-level labor market shocks transmit into household decision-making through the channel of bargaining power.
