# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-09

## 1. Does Employment Slow Cognitive Decline? Evidence from Labor Market Shocks [Health]
**Authors:** Noah Arman Kouchekinia, David Neumark, Tim A. Bruckner
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35117
**Abstract:** The researchers investigate whether continued employment helps slow cognitive deterioration using Health and Retirement Survey data. They employ a Bartik instrument to analyze how local labor market disruptions affect cognitive outcomes across different regions. Their key finding indicates that substantial declines over time in cognitive scores stemming from negative labor demand shocks occur primarily among men aged 51–64. The authors note these individuals show greater employment sensitivity to local economic conditions compared to women or older men. Their evidence broadens understanding beyond traditional retirement-age considerations, suggesting that working to older ages may delay age-related cognitive decline.

## 2. Work from Home and Fertility [Fertility]
**Authors:** Steven J. Davis, Cevat Giray Aksoy, Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Katelyn Cranney, Mathias Dolls, Pablo Zarate
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34963
**Abstract:** This research examines the relationship between remote work arrangements and fertility outcomes using data from surveys conducted after the pandemic. The authors find that realized fertility from 2023 to 2025 and future planned fertility are higher among adults who work from home at least one day a week. When both partners in a couple work remotely, estimated lifetime fertility increases by approximately 0.32 children per woman compared to couples where neither partner works from home. The study also analyzes how work-from-home availability in occupations correlates with annual fertility rates using pre- and post-pandemic U.S. data. National fertility implications vary across countries based on differences in remote work adoption rates.

## 3. Expanding Paternity Leave: Effects on Beliefs, Norms, and Gender Gaps [Fertility]
**Authors:** Henrik Kleven, Camille Landais, Anne Sophie S. Lassen, Philip Rosenbaum, Herdis Steingrimsdottir, Jakob Egholt Søgaard
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34862
**Abstract:** The researchers examined a major Danish paternity leave expansion using a regression discontinuity design combined with survey data linked to administrative records. The reform substantially shifted leave allocation from mothers to fathers. Key findings include: parents became more supportive of paternity leave, with shifts toward progressive gender-role beliefs; perceived differences in childcare ability between parents narrowed; and gender gaps in earnings narrowed by 34 percentage points during the leave year and 2.8 percentage points afterward. The policy demonstrated that policy can meaningfully influence beliefs, norms, and gender inequality. However, the earmarked leave structure restricted families' flexibility and lowered leave satisfaction, highlighting inherent trade-offs in paternalistic policy design.

## 4. Progress or Backsliding? Changes in the Gender Wage Gap for Business Professionals [Gender]
**Authors:** Ann Harrison, Laura J. Kray, Noor Sethi
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34809
**Abstract:** This research examines earnings differences between male and female MBA graduates from a large public university across three decades. The study reveals substantial gender wage gaps that typically expand throughout careers. When comparing graduates at similar career stages over time, the researchers found the raw gender wage gap has decreased by 33 to 50 percent over the past 20 years. A significant shift in timing has emerged: previously, wage gaps developed gradually over time, but now measurable gaps appear immediately upon graduation. The convergence in working hours and changes in industry distribution account for much of this narrowing. However, unexplained wage gaps persist at the start of recent graduates' careers, indicating different underlying causes across generations. The findings suggest both meaningful advancement in wage equity and new concerns about early-career disparities.

## 5. Marginal Admission to Elite High Schools: Long-run Effects on Labor Market Outcomes [Education]
**Authors:** Francisco J. Cabrera-Hernández, Andrew Dustan, Daniel Osuna Gomez, María Padilla-Romo
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34706
**Abstract:** The researchers employed a regression discontinuity methodology to examine how admission to elite public high schools in Mexico City affects long-term employment and earnings. They compared students whose exam scores fell just above and just below the elite school threshold. Key findings include that marginally admitted students initially faced lower employment in the formal private sector and reduced wages at five and ten-year marks. However, these gaps disappeared after 15 years. The study also reveals that elite school attendance initially delayed formal labor market entry and appeared to channel students toward lower-productivity firms and industries in the short term, with longer-run gains emerging only after the student has had sufficient time to accumulate experience and credentials.
