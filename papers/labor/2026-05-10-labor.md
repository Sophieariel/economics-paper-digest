# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-10

## 1. Firm Pay, Amenities, and Inequality [Gender]
**Authors:** Sydnee Caldwell, Ingrid Haegele, Jörg Heining
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35149
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how workers value non-wage job attributes and what those valuations imply for wage inequality and the gender pay gap. Using a large-scale survey of German workers that embeds discrete choice experiments — in which respondents rank pairs of hypothetical job offers drawn from real firms with wages randomized across offers — the authors recover willingness-to-pay estimates for a comprehensive set of firm-specific amenities including work flexibility, job security, commuting distance, and workplace culture. They find that amenity valuations vary substantially across both firms and demographic groups, yet a single amenity index captures most of the cross-firm variation. Crucially, workers' valuations of amenities are approximately orthogonal to firm wage premiums: workers at high-wage firms do not, on average, place lower values on amenities, so compensating differentials do not meaningfully compress between-firm wage inequality. On gender: male–female differences in amenity valuations — women valuing flexibility and job security more highly, men valuing compensation and advancement — explain a meaningful share of the residual gender wage gap beyond what observable human capital and occupation account for. By linking survey responses directly to administrative employment and earnings records, the authors validate that stated preferences track actual sorting across firms. The paper contributes to the literatures on compensating wage differentials, firm heterogeneity and inequality, and the sources of the gender pay gap. NBER Working Paper No. 35149.

## 2. The Effects of School Phone Bans: National Evidence from Lockable Pouches [Education]
**Authors:** Hunt Allcott, E. Jason Baron, Thomas Dee, Angela L. Duckworth, Matthew Gentzkow, Brian Jacob
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35132
**Abstract:** This paper provides the first large-scale causal estimates of the effects of restricting student smartphone use during the school day, exploiting the staggered adoption of lockable phone pouches (Yondr) across thousands of U.S. schools as a natural experiment. The authors combine administrative school records, standardized test scores, GPS-based phone-activity data, and original student and teacher surveys, and estimate treatment effects using a staggered difference-in-differences design identified by the timing of pouch adoption relative to the pouch manufacturer's sales records. Pouch adoption substantially reduces in-school phone use — GPS pings and teacher reports both document sharp declines. In the short run, disciplinary incidents rise and self-reported student well-being dips, consistent with an adjustment period as students adapt to restricted access. These negative effects reverse in subsequent school years, with well-being recovering while disciplinary effects dissipate. Academic effects average near zero across the full sample: high schools show modest positive math gains while middle schools show small negative effects, suggesting heterogeneous responses by developmental stage. The analysis finds minimal effects on attendance, self-reported classroom attention, or perceived cyberbullying. The paper contributes to the literatures on education technology, school policy, and adolescent development, and provides actionable evidence for the rapidly expanding policy debate over student phone use. NBER Working Paper No. 35132.

## 3. Wind Turbine Proximity and Health: Longitudinal Evidence from U.S. Households [Health]
**Authors:** Niklas Rott, Douglas Almond, Osea Giuntella
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35131
**Abstract:** Concerns that living near wind turbines causes adverse health effects — including depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance, and cardiovascular stress — have generated substantial public controversy and have informed land-use regulations in several U.S. states. This paper provides the most comprehensive causal investigation of these claims to date, combining geocoded residential addresses for over 120,000 U.S. households observed continuously from 2011 to 2023 with the precise coordinates and commissioning dates of every utility-scale wind turbine in the country and consumer purchasing data on health-related products including prescription and over-the-counter medications, sleep aids, and mental-health supplements. The authors exploit within-household variation in turbine exposure over time — households that had no nearby turbines and then gained them as new installations came online — to estimate longitudinal treatment effects using an event-study design that flexibly controls for household fixed effects, county-by-year shocks, and observable household characteristics. Across all health indicators examined, including depression, anxiety, sleep disorders, and associated medication purchases, the authors find no statistically or economically detectable adverse health effects at typical turbine-to-residence distances. While the authors are careful to note they cannot rule out very small effects in tails of the exposure distribution, the findings indicate that fears about substantial population-level health impacts from turbine siting are not supported by data. The paper contributes to the literatures on environmental health economics, the non-market costs of energy infrastructure, and health-outcome measurement using consumer purchasing records. NBER Working Paper No. 35131.

## 4. Authority Figures and the Polarization of Gender Norms [Gender]
**Authors:** Ali Abboud, Samuel Bazzi, Serena Canaan, Antoine Deeb, Pierre Mouganie
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18611
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether exposure to female authority figures in higher education causally shifts gender attitudes among students, and whether any such shifts differ systematically by student gender. The authors exploit the quasi-random assignment of incoming first-year students to faculty mentors at a selective university in the Middle East, a design that generates exogenous variation in whether a student's primary academic advisor is male or female. Linking administrative assignment records to follow-up alumni surveys conducted over a 24-year horizon, the authors estimate long-run treatment effects on a battery of outcomes capturing views on women's economic participation, political representation, and household roles. Female students assigned to female mentors show persistently more progressive attitudes toward women's employment and political participation, effects strongest among observant women and those in male-dominated STEM programs where female faculty are least common. Male students assigned to female mentors, by contrast, develop more conservative gender views on average, with the largest backlash among religiously observant men and men in male-dominated fields. The authors interpret these diverging responses through a model in which female authority figures in counter-stereotypical roles simultaneously provide role-model information that updates women's aspirations and trigger identity-protective responses among men who feel their social position is threatened. The result is what the authors term a "progress paradox": expanding female representation in elite academic roles opens pathways for women while deepening gender-norm polarization between men and women. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18611.

## 5. Mothers, Schools, and the Making of American Human Capital Mobility [Education]
**Authors:** Lukas Althoff, Harriet M. Brookes Gray, Hugo Reichardt
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35152
**Abstract:** This paper revisits the historical origins of intergenerational mobility in the United States, arguing that standard accounts — which track father-to-son earnings persistence — miss the central role of mothers' education in transmitting human capital across generations. The authors build a novel longitudinal dataset spanning the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries by linking literacy records across decennial Census waves, recovering both maternal and paternal educational attainment for large samples of children and their eventual adult outcomes. This two-parent framework reveals that mothers' literacy was, in earlier cohorts, the dominant predictor of children's educational attainment and subsequent earnings mobility — stronger than fathers' education, neighborhood of origin, or observable economic resources. As publicly funded mass schooling expanded across U.S. counties from the mid-1800s onward, however, the importance of parental human capital as a determinant of children's outcomes declined sharply: school availability attenuated the intergenerational transmission of both mothers' and fathers' education while simultaneously raising average mobility across the board. The authors use the staggered timing of county-level school openings and expansions as a source of identifying variation in an event-study and difference-in-differences framework applied to the linked Census records. Their findings imply that the United States' historical reputation as a mobile society is explained in large part by its nineteenth-century investment in public education, which effectively substituted public human capital investment for the private transmission advantage of educated mothers. The paper contributes to the literatures on intergenerational mobility, the economics of education, and the historical role of public schooling in shaping inequality. NBER Working Paper No. 35152.
