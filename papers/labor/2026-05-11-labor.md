# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-11

## 1. Gender Mix and Team Performance: Evidence from Obstetrics [Gender]
**Authors:** Ambar La Forgia and Manasvini Singh
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35084
**Abstract:** This paper provides causal evidence on how the gender composition of expert teams affects high-stakes performance outcomes, using childbirth as the empirical setting. Leveraging quasi-random assignment of patients to two-person obstetric teams across 2.5 million Florida births, the authors estimate the effect of team gender mix on severe maternal complications. They find that "female-only teams achieve the best maternal outcomes, whereas male-only teams have the worst": severe maternal complications are 15.8% higher in all-male teams and 7.1–10.8% higher in mixed-gender teams relative to all-female teams. Patient sorting and physician preferences cannot explain the pattern, pointing instead to gender dynamics within teams as the operative mechanism. Female-led mixed teams perform particularly poorly in high-acuity cases, a result the authors attribute to the inversion of conventional gender hierarchies generating coordination frictions. A notable distributional finding is that all-female teams eliminate the racial disparity in maternal outcomes for Black women that characterizes all other team compositions. The paper contributes to the literatures on gender and workplace performance, discrimination, occupational segregation, and the economics of healthcare quality. NBER Working Paper No. 35084. Programs: Labor Studies, Health Economics.

## 2. Authority Figures and the Polarization of Gender Norms [Gender]
**Authors:** Ali Abboud, Samuel Bazzi, Serena Canaan, Antoine Deeb, and Pierre Mouganie
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18611
**Abstract:** This paper exploits the random assignment of first-year students to faculty advisors at a Middle Eastern university to identify the long-run causal effect of mentor gender on students' gender attitudes and labor market behavior. Tracking gender-norm beliefs through alumni surveys covering 24 years of graduates, the authors find that female advisors shift women's views in a more egalitarian direction — particularly regarding women's roles in politics and the labor market — while simultaneously shifting male students toward more conservative positions. Effects are largest among students from religious backgrounds and those enrolled in male-dominated STEM fields. A key mechanism finding is that simple exposure to high-achieving female peers produces minimal attitude change; what matters is contact with effective senior female authority figures. The study thus documents a polarization externality: policies that expand female representation in leadership roles simultaneously empower women and generate backlash among men, widening the gap in gender-norm beliefs across the student population. The paper contributes to the literatures on gender discrimination, the formation of gender norms, mentoring and leadership, and the labor-market consequences of gendered peer networks. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18611.

## 3. Does Employment Slow Cognitive Decline? Evidence from Labor Market Shocks [Health]
**Authors:** Noah Arman Kouchekinia, David Neumark, and Tim A. Bruckner
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35117
**Abstract:** This paper provides causal evidence on whether continued labor force participation protects cognitive function in older adults, a question with direct implications for retirement policy, disability insurance, and late-career labor supply. Using longitudinal Health and Retirement Study (HRS) data, the authors construct a Bartik shift-share instrument based on "plausibly exogenous shifts in labor demand in local labor markets" to isolate the effect of employment on cognitive test scores. They find "substantial declines over time in cognitive scores stemming from negative labor demand shocks," implying that job loss induced by local economic downturns accelerates cognitive aging. Effects are concentrated among men aged 51–64, whose employment rates respond most strongly to local labor market conditions, while estimates for women and older age groups are smaller and less precisely estimated. The analysis extends the cognitive-engagement hypothesis beyond voluntary retirement by showing that involuntary employment exits—driven by demand-side shocks—are sufficient to accelerate decline. The findings carry implications for policies that extend working lives, including delayed retirement incentives and occupational rehabilitation, as well as for the social costs of regional economic downturns. NBER Working Paper No. 35117. Programs: Economics of Aging, Health Economics, Labor Studies, Public Economics.

## 4. Dynamic Teacher Sorting [Education]
**Authors:** Rasmus H. Klokker and Nicolai Kristensen
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18604
**Abstract:** Sorting among school teachers—whereby high-qualified teachers systematically teach higher-achieving students—is well documented, but the dynamic process through which this sorting arises has received less attention. This paper uses Danish administrative records tracking 10 cohorts of teacher college graduates at biannual intervals over a 15-year horizon, combined with a semi-structural dynamic model, to uncover the career trajectories that generate observed teacher–school matching patterns. The authors find that high-performing teachers progressively reallocate toward high-achieving public schools and private schools over their careers, while the least-qualified teachers enter low-SES public schools and largely remain there. Teachers who exit the profession possess stable unobserved characteristics that are distinct from those who stay, indicating that selection out of teaching is not random and may further concentrate disadvantage in low-SES schools. The dynamic framework allows the authors to decompose sorting into initial placement, voluntary school transitions, and profession exit, revealing that career dynamics amplify the sorting gap that emerges at labor market entry. The findings have implications for education policy aimed at improving school quality in disadvantaged communities, including teacher incentive schemes, recruitment targeting, and the design of school assignment mechanisms. IZA Discussion Paper No. 18604. JEL: I21, I24, I28.

## 5. Work from Home and Fertility [Fertility]
**Authors:** Steven J. Davis, Cevat Giray Aksoy, Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Katelyn Cranney, Mathias Dolls, and Pablo Zarate
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34963
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether the large, pandemic-driven expansion of remote work has affected fertility rates and planned family size. Drawing on novel survey data collected from 2023 to 2025 across multiple countries, combined with pre- and post-pandemic U.S. administrative and census data, the authors document that "fertility from 2023 to 2025 and future planned fertility are higher among adults who WFH at least one day a week," with effects substantially larger when both partners in a couple work remotely. The authors estimate that "lifetime fertility is greater by 0.32 children per woman when both partners WFH one or more days per week" relative to couples in which neither partner works from home. Analysis of pre- versus post-pandemic variation in the United States confirms the positive association between remote work adoption and birth rates at the population level. The proposed mechanism is that remote work reduces the career cost of childbearing by improving schedule flexibility and reducing commuting burdens, lowering the effective price of fertility. Cross-country implications differ because WFH adoption rates vary markedly across national labor markets. The paper contributes to the literatures on fertility decisions, the child penalty, gender gaps in labor supply, and the long-run demographic consequences of the remote-work revolution. NBER Working Paper No. 34963.
