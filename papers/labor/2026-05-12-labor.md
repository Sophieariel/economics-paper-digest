# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-12

## 1. Authority Figures and the Polarization of Gender Norms [Gender]
**Authors:** Ali Abboud, Samuel Bazzi, Serena Canaan, Antoine Deeb, Pierre Mouganie
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18611
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how authority figures in higher education shape gender norms over the long run. Using the random assignment of first-year students to faculty advisors at an elite Middle Eastern university, and combining administrative data with alumni surveys spanning up to 24 years, the authors find that women assigned to female advisors develop more egalitarian perspectives on politics and employment, while men assigned to female advisors adopt more conservative positions. Effects are most pronounced among religious students and those in male-dominated STEM disciplines. The paper documents a "progress paradox" whereby gains in female representation in elite authority expand opportunities for women while intensifying backlash among men. The theoretical framework combines belief updating and identity-based status threat to explain these divergent patterns.

## 2. Economic Incentives or Social Norms? Labor Supply Differentials Between East and West German Mothers [Fertility]
**Authors:** Bastien Chabé-Ferret, Zainab Iftikhar, JungJae Park
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18600
**Abstract:** This paper examines a 350-hour annual difference in maternal work hours between East and West Germany. Estimating a collective model of family formation and labor supply using GSOEP panel data spanning 2000–2017, the authors decompose the contributions of social norms versus economic incentives to observed labor supply differences. They find that the working-mother stigma accounts for 73 percent of the East-West gap, while higher Western wages partially counterbalance this cultural effect. The analysis further demonstrates that standard policy reforms may actually widen the regional disparity, with their effectiveness critically depending on whether social norms remain in place. The results highlight the importance of accounting for cultural factors when designing family and labor market policies.

## 3. Peer Effects in Adolescent Mental Health [Health]
**Authors:** Hussain Hadah
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18623
**Abstract:** This paper examines how peers' mental health influences adolescents' overall wellbeing, school performance, and behavioral patterns. Using friends-of-friends as an instrumental variable for peer mental health, the author finds that a one standard deviation increase in friends' mental unwellness corresponds with a 0.67 standard deviation increase in an individual's own mental unwellness. Results further show that peers' therapy participation significantly reduces individual mental unwellness while increasing the likelihood of therapy participation. The study documents substantial adverse effects of peer mental unwellness on personal mental health, academic achievement, physical health, and risky behavior, establishing that adolescent mental health functions as a social contagion phenomenon with important implications for school-based mental health interventions.

## 4. The Impact of Preschool Entry Age on Low-Income Children's Use of Health and Social Services [Education]
**Authors:** Maya Rossin-Slater, Adrienne Sabety, Aileen Wu
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18627
**Abstract:** This paper demonstrates that public preschools serve as a gateway to health and social services for low-income children. Using a regression discontinuity design based on state school-entry cutoff dates, the authors find that children from low-income families born shortly before the cutoff accessed school-based services — including speech-language pathology, occupational or physical therapy, and vision and dental services — more frequently at ages three and four compared to those born just after the cutoff. Additionally, these children showed higher rates of receiving Supplemental Security Income benefits. The findings indicate that preschool enrollment serves as a critical connector linking low-income children to broader health and social service systems beyond purely educational settings, with implications for the design of early childhood programs.

## 5. The Labor Market and Health Impacts of Reducing Cesarean Section Deliveries [Health]
**Authors:** Sarah Miller, Petra Persson, Maya Rossin-Slater, Laura Wherry
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18629
**Abstract:** This paper examines an intervention designed to lower cesarean delivery rates among low-risk first-time mothers, using California birth records matched with earnings information. Exploiting the quasi-experimental variation generated by the intervention, the authors find that exposed mothers were 8 percent less likely to have a c-section, with no adverse health effects. The study identifies suggestive evidence that these mothers were more inclined to return to their original employer and achieved higher earnings rankings within their firms during the quarter following birth, though these labor market advantages diminished over time. Mothers who subsequently had a second child demonstrated reduced rates of both cesarean sections and preterm deliveries, suggesting the measured benefits from avoiding an initial c-section may represent conservative estimates of overall gains. The findings contribute to the literature on how obstetric practices shape maternal labor market trajectories.
