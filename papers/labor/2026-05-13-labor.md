# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-13

## 1. The Labor Market and Health Impacts of Reducing Cesarean Section Deliveries [Health]
**Authors:** Sarah Miller, Petra Persson, Maya Rossin-Slater, Laura R. Wherry
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18629
**Abstract:** This paper examines an intervention designed to lower cesarean delivery rates among low-risk first-time mothers, using California birth records linked to earnings data. Exposed mothers experienced an 8% reduction in c-section rates without adverse health consequences. The study finds suggestive evidence that these mothers were more likely to return to their pre-birth employer and had higher within-firm earnings rankings in the quarter following delivery, though these labor market advantages diminished over longer horizons. Mothers who went on to have a second child also showed reduced rates of both cesarean and preterm delivery, suggesting the measured benefits from avoiding an initial c-section are conservative estimates of broader advantages. The analysis provides new causal evidence on how obstetric practices shape both short- and long-run maternal labor market and health outcomes.

## 2. Supporting Mothers Back to Work: Experimental Evidence on Employment, Fertility, and Child Outcomes [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniela Del Boca, Luca Favero, Chiara D. Pronzato
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18630
**Abstract:** This paper uses experimental evidence from an intervention targeting mothers who reduced employment due to childcare responsibilities to examine how work-family reconciliation policies affect maternal labor market trajectories, family investments, and reproductive decisions. The study finds that improving work-family reconciliation supports mothers' labor market reintegration, promotes investments in existing children, and, under conditions of greater economic stability, strengthens fertility desires. The results connect three interconnected outcomes — maternal labor force participation, child investments, and future childbearing — framing policy-supported reintegration as a lever for addressing both demographic sustainability challenges and gender gaps in employment in advanced economies.

## 3. Does Field of Study Shape the Gender Wage Gap? The Role of Migration Background [Gender]
**Authors:** Louise Devos, François Rycx, Thomas Senterre, Mélanie Volral
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18640
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how gender differences in wage returns to fields of study vary by migration background among more than 62,000 master's graduates. Law, economics, management, and science fields generate the highest wage returns, while women consistently earn less than men across all fields, with larger wage gaps concentrated in high-paying disciplines. First-generation immigrants from developing countries experience the lowest returns regardless of field choice, while second-generation immigrants approach but do not fully align with native earnings. Field of study accounts for a substantial share of wage inequality among natives and second-generation immigrants, whereas broader structural disadvantages dominate for first-generation immigrants. Results vary meaningfully by parental origin and age at arrival, highlighting the interaction between gender, educational sorting, and immigration background as compounding sources of labor market inequality.

## 4. Gender Representation and Collective Decision-Making in Expert Committees [Gender]
**Authors:** Rigissa Megalokonomou
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18639
**Abstract:** This paper uses quasi-random assignment of judges to panels across 3,700 cases in Greece's highest administrative court to examine how increased female representation shapes group deliberation and decision outcomes. Panels with a majority of women judges (three or more of five members) reject more appeals and refer fewer cases onward, while modest diversity below this threshold has minimal impact. Panels with higher shares of female judges require substantially more time for decisions, particularly on complex cases — a pattern consistent with more thorough deliberation rather than coordination costs. The findings suggest that diversity's effectiveness depends on achieving sufficient critical mass to reshape how groups engage with problems, and challenge policy approaches that target only incremental increases in female representation without altering the deliberative composition of the group itself.

## 5. The Impact of Preschool Entry Age on Low-Income Children's Use of Health and Social Services [Education]
**Authors:** Maya Rossin-Slater, Adrienne Sabety, Aileen Wu
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18627
**Abstract:** This paper demonstrates that public preschools serve as a gateway to health and social services for low-income children. Using a regression discontinuity design that exploits school-entry age cutoff dates across states as a natural experiment, the study shows that children from low-income families born just before the cutoff enroll in preschool earlier and subsequently gain greater access to school-based services — including speech-language pathology, therapy, and vision and dental care — at ages three and four. These children also show higher rates of receiving Supplemental Security Income assistance. The findings reveal an important and underappreciated channel through which preschool enrollment connects disadvantaged children to broader health and social service systems, with implications for evaluating the full returns to early childhood education investments.
