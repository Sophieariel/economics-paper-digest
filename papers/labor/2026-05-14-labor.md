# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-14

## 1. Gender Mix and Team Performance: Evidence from Obstetrics [Gender]
**Authors:** Ambar La Forgia, Manasvini Singh
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35084
**Abstract:** This research examines how team gender composition influences performance in obstetrics using data from 2.5 million births. The authors found that female-only teams achieve the best maternal outcomes, whereas male-only teams have the worst. Specifically, severe maternal complications were 15.8% higher in all-male teams and 7.1–10.8% higher in mixed-gender teams compared to all-female teams. The study ruled out alternative explanations involving patient selection or physician preferences. Instead, the researchers attribute performance differences to how gender dynamics affect team decision-making and resilience. Notably, female-only teams demonstrated superior outcomes for Black women and were the only team type showing no racial disparities in maternal outcomes.

## 2. High Schools and the Uneven Rise in American Opportunity [Education]
**Authors:** Alison Doxey, Ezra Karger, Peter Nencka
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35068
**Abstract:** This research examines how high school expansion between 1850 and 1910 reshaped economic prospects across America. The study documents that when towns established high schools, nearby children significantly increased their enrollment rates. The findings reveal that high schools substantially boosted women's workforce engagement and career opportunities while delaying marriage and childbearing. Women's labor force participation growth between 1870 and 1930 can be attributed one-third to expanded high school access. However, the benefits were unevenly distributed. Children from wealthy families experienced the largest gains, while Black students saw no measurable benefits on average. Although high schools narrowed gender-based economic disparities, they simultaneously amplified existing inequalities based on race and class status.

## 3. The Impact of Maternal Education on Early Childhood Development [Education]
**Authors:** Moriam Khanam, Mohammad Hajizadeh, Casey Warman
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35075
**Abstract:** This investigation examines how maternal schooling affects young children's developmental progress by utilizing an educational stipend initiative targeting female students in rural Bangladesh. Drawing on 2019 survey data, the researchers find that five years of program eligibility raised mothers' education by approximately one year. The analysis demonstrates that each additional year of maternal schooling improves developmental scores by 0.5 points (on a 0-10 scale), with meaningful improvements in readiness, literacy-numeracy abilities, and physical development. The study also identifies mechanisms through which education operates, including enhanced child nutrition, enriched home learning settings, improved parenting approaches, and greater early education participation. Results underscore the significance of expanding educational opportunities for women in developing regions.

## 4. Exploring Trends in Severe Maternal Morbidity in the U.S. [Health]
**Authors:** Pinka Chatterji, Fangning Li, Sara Markowitz
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35062
**Abstract:** The study investigates rising severe maternal morbidity (SMM) incidence in the United States, with particularly sharp increases among Black mothers. Rather than examining isolated variables, the researchers evaluated a large set of variables over a long time span to let the data inform what factors may be most influential. Using inpatient hospital data from 1998–2019, they analyzed maternal, hospital, and county-level characteristics associated with SMM trends and racial disparities. Key findings indicate that observed characteristics explain minimal overall SMM variation but account for substantial portions of the Black-White gap. Medical and pregnancy-related comorbid conditions are the largest drivers of the observed variations. The authors suggest that investigating patients with comorbidities and hospital environment features may help identify policy-relevant SMM drivers and disparities.

## 5. Work from Home and Fertility [Fertility]
**Authors:** Steven J. Davis, Cevat Giray Aksoy, Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Katelyn Cranney, Mathias Dolls, Pablo Zarate
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34963
**Abstract:** The researchers examine connections between remote work and fertility patterns using survey data from the post-pandemic period. They find that realized fertility from 2023 to 2025 and future planned fertility are higher among adults who work from home at least one day a week. The estimated lifetime increase is substantial: approximately 0.32 more children per woman when both partners work from home one or more days weekly compared to neither partner doing so. The analysis reveals that national fertility implications vary considerably across countries, primarily due to differing WFH adoption rates. Supplementary analysis of U.S. data from before and after the pandemic demonstrates that fertility rates rise in connection with increased remote work opportunities. Note: this paper is dated March 2026, slightly outside the 30-day window; it is the most recent available NBER paper on fertility and labor supply.
