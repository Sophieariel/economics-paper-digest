# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-15

## 1. Does Field of Study Shape the Gender Wage Gap? The Role of Migration Background [Gender]
**Authors:** Louise Devos, François Rycx, Thomas Senterre, Mélanie Volral
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18640
**Abstract:** The study analyzes over 62,000 master's graduates using matched employer-employee data to investigate how gender differences in wage returns to fields of study vary by migration background. Key findings include wide variation in returns across fields, with law, economics, management, and science performing best. Women consistently earn less than men within all fields, particularly in higher-paying ones. First-generation immigrants from developing nations experience the lowest returns regardless of field, while second-generation immigrants approach native levels. Educational specialization explains substantial wage inequality for natives and second-generation immigrants, but broader disadvantages dominate for first-generation immigrants.

## 2. Supporting Mothers Back to Work: Experimental Evidence on Employment, Fertility, and Child Outcomes [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniela Del Boca, Luca Favero, Chiara D. Pronzato
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18630
**Abstract:** Many advanced economies face persistently low fertility alongside rapid population ageing, raising concerns about economic sustainability and demographic balance. Addressing these challenges requires both sustained labor market participation among the working-age population and conditions that support childbearing. These objectives place women, and particularly mothers, at the center of the demographic debate, as motherhood remains a key turning point in employment trajectories and family formation. Using experimental evidence from an intervention targeting mothers who curtailed employment due to childcare responsibilities, the paper finds that improving work–family reconciliation can support mothers' labor market reintegration, promote investments in existing children, and, under conditions of greater stability, strengthen fertility desires.

## 3. The Labor Market and Health Impacts of Reducing Cesarean Section Deliveries [Health]
**Authors:** Sarah Miller, Petra Persson, Maya Rossin-Slater, Laura R. Wherry
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18629
**Abstract:** The researchers examined an intervention reducing cesarean deliveries among low-risk first-time mothers using California birth records linked to earnings data. Mothers in the intervention group were 8% less likely to have a c-section, with no adverse health effects. The study found suggestive evidence that these mothers were more likely to return to their employer and had higher earnings rankings shortly after birth, though these advantages diminished over time. Additionally, mothers who subsequently had a second child showed reduced rates of both c-sections and preterm delivery, suggesting the full benefits of avoiding an initial cesarean may be underestimated.

## 4. Does Employment Slow Cognitive Decline? Evidence from Labor Market Shocks [Health]
**Authors:** Noah Arman Kouchekinia, David Neumark, Tim A. Bruckner
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35117
**Abstract:** The study examines whether continued workforce participation slows cognitive decline using data from the Health and Retirement Study. The researchers employ labor demand shifts as an instrumental variable to establish causality. Their findings show substantial declines over time in cognitive scores stemming from negative labor demand shocks, particularly affecting men aged 51–64. The evidence suggests that working longer may help delay age-related cognitive decline, extending prior research beyond traditional retirement-age analysis and supporting the hypothesis that employment to older ages may preserve cognitive function.

## 5. The Working Life of a Teacher: Lessons from the Danish Matched Student-Teacher Data [Education]
**Authors:** Maria Knoth Humlum, Helena Skyt Nielsen, Marianne Simonsen
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18645
**Abstract:** This research examines teacher workforce characteristics and working conditions using matched student-teacher data from Danish public schools. The paper documents gender segregation across subjects and grades, with female teachers dominating Danish language instruction while male educators are more prevalent in advanced mathematics. The study reveals that educators tend to select teaching fields aligned with their subject-specific expertise, though some lack strong foundational skills. More skilled educators cluster in schools with equally competent colleagues, creating sorting patterns with implications for student outcomes and educational inequality. These findings offer insights for understanding educational production functions and for designing strategies to enhance teacher retention and professional wellbeing.
