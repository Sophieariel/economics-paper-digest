# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-17

## 1. Does Field of Study Shape the Gender Wage Gap? The Role of Migration Background [Gender]
**Authors:** Louise Devos, François Rycx, Thomas Senterre, Mélanie Volral
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18640
**Abstract:** Using matched employer-employee data on more than 62,000 master's graduates, this paper examines how gender differences in wage returns to fields of study vary by migration background and how educational specialisation contributes to the gender wage gap. We estimate wage regressions and apply a decomposition approach to separate sorting across fields from differences in pay within fields. Returns vary widely, with law, economics and management, and science yielding the highest returns, and women earning less than men within all fields, especially in high-paying ones. First-generation immigrants from developing countries obtain the lowest returns regardless of field of study, while second-generation immigrants approach but do not fully match natives. Fields of study explain a substantial share of gender wage inequality among natives and second-generation immigrants, whereas among first-generation immigrants broader wage disadvantages dominate. Results further vary with the number of parents originating from developing countries and with age at arrival.

## 2. Adolescent Behavior, Learning, and Knowledge Diffusion: Evidence from a Randomized Experiment [Education]
**Authors:** Sule Alan, Kumar Biswas, Christina S. Hauser, Shwetlena Sabarwal
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35160
**Abstract:** Improving classroom behavior is a persistent challenge in low-resource education systems, where disruptive environments often derail instruction and limit learning. This paper evaluates a program that transferred behavioral norm-setting responsibility from teachers to students across 127 middle schools in Bangladesh with over 7,500 adolescents. The program strengthened classroom social climate and peer cooperation. High-performing students showed significant math and verbal gains. A follow-up assessment 1.5 years later found that while social climate improvements diminished, academic gains persisted and expanded to additional students, particularly among higher-ability peers. A key mechanism is enhanced academic support networks among high-ability students, facilitating peer learning and knowledge diffusion within this group.

## 3. Supporting Mothers Back to Work: Experimental Evidence on Employment, Fertility, and Child Outcomes [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniela Del Boca, Luca Favero, Chiara D. Pronzato
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18630
**Abstract:** This paper presents an experimental intervention study addressing how work-family reconciliation policies affect mothers' labor market reintegration. The findings indicate that improving work–family reconciliation can support mothers' labor market reintegration, promote investments in existing children, and, under conditions of greater stability, strengthen fertility desires. The study addresses persistent demographic challenges in advanced economies, where low fertility rates coincide with aging populations, positioning mothers' employment trajectories as central to understanding both economic participation and family formation patterns. The results suggest that family-friendly policy design can simultaneously advance labor market participation and demographic goals.

## 4. The Labor Market and Health Impacts of Reducing Cesarean Section Deliveries [Health]
**Authors:** Sarah Miller, Petra Persson, Maya Rossin-Slater, Laura R. Wherry
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18629
**Abstract:** This paper examines an intervention that reduced cesarean deliveries among low-risk first-time mothers, using California birth records linked to earnings data. Exposed mothers were 8% less likely to have a c-section, with no adverse health effects. Evidence suggests these mothers were more likely to return to their pre-birth employer and had higher within-firm earnings rankings in the quarter after birth, though labor market gains diminished over time. Mothers who subsequently had a second child showed lower rates of both c-section and preterm delivery, suggesting the estimated effects from avoiding a first c-section may be lower bounds on the total gains. The findings highlight how obstetric care decisions intersect with maternal labor market trajectories.

## 5. The Long-Run Effects of the Affordable Care Act: Evidence from a Partially Pre-Committed Research Design Over the COVID-19 Recession and Recovery [Health]
**Authors:** Jeffrey Clemens, Anwita Mahajan, Joseph J. Sabia
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35190
**Abstract:** Adjustment frictions can cause the long-run effects of social insurance reforms to differ from their short-run effects. Using pre-committed extensions of event study specifications applied previously for short-run analyses, we test the hypothesis that the Affordable Care Act's (ACA) impacts on insurance coverage and employment would increase following the substantial churn generated by the COVID-19 pandemic. Contrary to the hypothesis, the ACA's impacts remained stable through the pandemic. Long-run effects on employment and employer coverage were modest and much smaller than initially projected. Our study illustrates how partially pre-committed designs can yield informative long-run estimates while limiting specification search.
