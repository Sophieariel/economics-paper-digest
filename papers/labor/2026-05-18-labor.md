# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-18

## 1. Authority Figures and the Polarization of Gender Norms [Gender]
**Authors:** Ali Abboud, Samuel Bazzi, Serena Canaan, Antoine Deeb, Pierre Mouganie
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35174
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how senior faculty shape perspectives on gender through long-term effects. Utilizing random assignment of undergraduates to faculty mentors at a prestigious Middle Eastern institution, the authors tracked alumni attitudes spanning nearly two decades. Female students mentored by women embraced more progressive stances regarding employment and civic participation, while male students moved toward traditional viewpoints. The impact intensified among religiously observant students and in technical disciplines where female leadership represents an unconventional presence. The largest impacts come from senior and high-value-added female advisors rather than exposure to accomplished female peers. The authors' framework suggests this pattern reflects identity-based status concerns combined with belief revision, revealing a progress paradox whereby gains in female representation in elite authority expand opportunities for women while intensifying backlash among men.

## 2. Building Opportunity: The Intergenerational Effects of Chilean School Construction [Education]
**Authors:** Adrienne Lucas, Patrick McEwan
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35042
**Abstract:** Between 1965–1966, Chile constructed and staffed thousands of new primary classrooms in communities facing supply shortages. The authors employ a quasi-experimental methodology with large census datasets to demonstrate that childhood exposure to this school construction initiative produced substantial improvements in adult educational attainment and employment outcomes. The policy notably eliminated a longstanding gender gap in schooling completion. When mothers benefited from the initiative, their children experienced meaningful positive effects on timely grade advancement and overall educational completion. The analysis indicates the policy generated significant returns, with a marginal public value of 13, accounting for both immediate adult benefits and subsequent generational advantages.

## 3. Breaking the Early Bell: Lessons from the First Statewide Mandate on School Start Times [Health]
**Authors:** Jialu (Gloria) Dou, Rania Gihleb, Osea Giuntella, Jakub Lonsky
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35184
**Abstract:** This study investigates California's Senate Bill 328, which established the first statewide requirement for delayed school start times in middle and high schools. Utilizing difference-in-differences and event study methodologies across five datasets, the authors find that SB 328 increased the share of students sleeping at least 8 hours per night by 13%. The analysis reveals meaningful reductions in negative mental health indicators among boys and concentrated gains in academic performance for eighth-grade mathematics and English—roughly 0.08–0.10 standard deviations—particularly benefiting Hispanic and economically disadvantaged learners. Results remain consistent when employing teachers' commute patterns as an alternative measure of initial start times. The findings suggest direct cognitive improvements from synchronizing school schedules with adolescent circadian biology rather than attendance-related mechanisms.

## 4. The Long-Run Effects of the Affordable Care Act: Evidence from a Partially Pre-Committed Research Design Over the COVID-19 Recession and Recovery [Health]
**Authors:** Jeffrey Clemens, Anwita Mahajan, Joseph J. Sabia
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35190
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether adjustment frictions would cause the Affordable Care Act's long-term effects to diverge from short-term impacts. Using pre-committed extensions of event study specifications, the authors test whether the pandemic's insurance market disruption would amplify the ACA's effects on coverage and employment. The findings reveal that the ACA's impacts remained stable through the pandemic and that long-run effects on employment and employer coverage were modest and much smaller than initially projected. This evidence helps resolve debates about the magnitude of labor market distortions attributable to the ACA's insurance market reforms and their persistence over the business cycle.

## 5. Bachelors Without Bachelor's: Gender Gaps in Education and Declining Marriage Rates [Gender]
**Authors:** Clara Chambers, Benjamin Goldman, Joseph Winkelmann
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35179
**Abstract:** This paper examines how shifts in college enrollment—from predominantly male to predominantly female over fifty years—correlate with marriage market changes. As college-educated men became scarcer, college-educated women maintained stable marriage rates by partnering with high-earning non-college men. This pattern, combined with economic decline among non-college men, has significantly reduced eligible partners for non-college women. The share of non-college men earning above the national median and unmarried to college women has fallen by more than 50%. Geographic analysis reveals smaller education-based marriage gaps where non-college men experience lower joblessness and incarceration rates, suggesting that deteriorating male economic outcomes primarily explain declining marriage rates among non-college women.
