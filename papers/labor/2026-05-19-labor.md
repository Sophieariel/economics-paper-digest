# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-19

## 1. Does Field of Study Shape the Gender Wage Gap? The Role of Migration Background [Gender]
**Authors:** Louise Devos, François Rycx, Thomas Senterre, Mélanie Volral
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18640
**Abstract:** This research examines wage disparities between genders across different educational specializations, analyzing how migration background influences these patterns. Using matched employer-employee data on more than 62,000 master's graduates and applying wage regression and Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition methods, the study finds that fields such as law, economics, management, and science yield the highest financial returns. Women earn less than men across all fields studied, with larger gaps concentrated in high-paying disciplines. First-generation immigrants from developing nations experience the lowest returns regardless of field selection, while second-generation immigrants approach but do not fully match native earnings. Educational specialization accounts for a significant portion of gender wage inequality among native and second-generation immigrant graduates. For first-generation immigrants, however, broader wage disadvantages overshadow field-of-study effects. Outcomes also correlate with parental origin and age at arrival, highlighting how migration background interacts with occupational sorting to shape earnings inequality by gender.

## 2. Supporting Mothers Back to Work: Experimental Evidence on Employment, Fertility, and Child Outcomes [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniela Del Boca, Luca Favero, Chiara D. Pronzato
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18630
**Abstract:** Many advanced economies face persistently low fertility alongside rapid population ageing, raising concerns about economic sustainability and demographic balance. This paper uses experimental data from an intervention targeting mothers who had reduced their labor market attachment due to childcare obligations. The findings show that improving work-family reconciliation can support mothers' labor market reintegration, promote investment in existing children, and, under conditions of greater economic stability, strengthen fertility desires. The study identifies a positive feedback loop between labor market participation and family formation intentions, offering evidence that policies simultaneously addressing demographic challenges and workforce participation can succeed when both dimensions are targeted. Results point to the importance of program design features—particularly stability guarantees and childcare support—for achieving sustained employment effects and measurable changes in fertility intentions.

## 3. The Long-Run Effects of the Affordable Care Act: Evidence from a Partially Pre-Committed Research Design Over the COVID-19 Recession and Recovery [Health]
**Authors:** Jeffrey Clemens, Anwita Mahajan, Joseph J. Sabia
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35190
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether the Affordable Care Act's effects on insurance coverage and employment would intensify following the pandemic-era disruptions to labor and insurance markets. Using pre-committed extensions of event study specifications previously applied in short-run analyses, the authors test the hypothesis that long-run impacts would exceed initial projections. The evidence contradicts this expectation: the ACA's impacts on insurance coverage and employment remained stable throughout the COVID-19 recession and recovery. Long-run effects on employment and employer-sponsored coverage were modest and substantially smaller than initially projected. The paper also demonstrates how partially pre-committed research designs can yield informative long-run causal estimates while limiting researcher degrees of freedom in specification search, offering a methodological contribution to the evaluation of major health policy interventions and their labor market consequences.

## 4. The Impacts of Medicaid's Managed Long-Term Services and Supports on Health Outcomes in Medicare [Health]
**Authors:** Ajin Lee, Maya Rossin-Slater, Becky Staiger, Amanda Su
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18628
**Abstract:** An aging U.S. population has raised important questions regarding the organization, delivery, and funding of long-term services and supports (LTSS), prompting many states to transition toward managed care delivery models. This paper analyzes the effects of shifting to Managed LTSS (MLTSS) on health outcomes among dual-eligible beneficiaries aged 65 and over in Florida and New York. Using Medicare claims data with a differences-in-differences methodology that exploits staggered county-level program rollouts, the authors find that MLTSS increased hospitalizations by 4.2 percent in Florida but produced no significant change in New York. Declining flu vaccination rates in Florida may explain increased respiratory hospitalizations under the managed care model. The findings reveal heterogeneous impacts across states that depend on implementation context and the specific managed care contracts in place, with implications for ongoing federal and state policy debates about the appropriate delivery structure for long-term care services.

## 5. Dynamic Teacher Sorting [Education]
**Authors:** Rasmus H. Klokker, Nicolai Kristensen
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18604
**Abstract:** This paper examines how teacher sorting across schools emerges and evolves over the career cycle, using Danish administrative data covering ten teacher cohorts followed for up to fifteen years. The authors formulate and estimate a semi-structural dynamic model of teacher career choice that captures transitions between school types, private-sector employment, and exit from teaching. The central finding is that high-qualified teachers systematically migrate toward high-achieving schools or private institutions over time, while lower-qualified teachers remain concentrated in low-socioeconomic-status public schools. Teachers who exit the profession entirely share certain time-constant unobserved characteristics that predict their departure. These patterns of dynamic sorting imply that the distribution of teacher quality across schools diverges substantially from the initial allocation at career entry, with long-run consequences for educational inequality and the accumulation of human capital in disadvantaged communities.
