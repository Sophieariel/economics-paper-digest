# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-20

## 1. Targeting Attitudes to Combat Sexual Harassment: A Randomized Intervention in the Norwegian Military [Gender]
**Authors:** Olle Folke, Torbjørn Hansen, Åshild Johnsen, Andreas Kotsadam, Johanna Rickne
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18637
**Abstract:** The authors conduct a field experiment with Norwegian military recruits to evaluate an information-based workplace harassment prevention program. Treated recruits received information about peers' beliefs that sexualized jokes constitute sexual harassment and data establishing equal job performance between male and female soldiers. The intervention produced durable improvements in knowledge about harassment definitions and women's occupational capabilities. Effects on actual harassment prevalence were directionally negative but statistically insignificant, a pattern the authors attribute to measurement challenges in workplace harassment surveys. Evidence from hypothetical harassment scenarios supports the view that behavior was influenced despite null main effects on reported incidence. The paper is the first field experiment to evaluate whether information-provision interventions reduce harassment in an institutional workplace setting, and it offers methodological guidance for subsequent research measuring behavioral change in this domain.

## 2. Does Field of Study Shape the Gender Wage Gap? The Role of Migration Background [Education]
**Authors:** Louise Devos, François Rycx, Thomas Senterre, Mélanie Volral
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18640
**Abstract:** Using matched employer-employee data on more than 62,000 master's graduates, the paper examines how gender differences in wage returns to fields of study vary by migration background and how educational specialization contributes to the gender wage gap. Returns vary widely across disciplines, with law, economics and management, and science yielding the highest premia; within every field, women earn less than men, with the gap widest in high-paying specializations. First-generation immigrants from developing countries receive the lowest returns regardless of field, while second-generation immigrants converge toward native earnings levels. Educational specialization explains a substantial share of the gender wage gap among natives and second-generation immigrants, though for first-generation immigrants broader wage disadvantages dominate. The analysis further distinguishes outcomes by parental origin and age at arrival, revealing that both generational status and migration timing shape how much educational choices can reduce gender earnings disparities.

## 3. Supporting Mothers Back to Work: Experimental Evidence on Employment, Fertility, and Child Outcomes [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniela Del Boca, Luca Favero, Chiara D. Pronzato
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18630
**Abstract:** The paper evaluates a randomized intervention providing work-family reconciliation support to mothers who reduced employment following childbirth. Treated mothers received assistance designed to facilitate labor market reintegration. The experiment demonstrates that improving work-family reconciliation significantly raises maternal employment rates. Beyond labor market effects, the intervention spurred investment in existing children and, in households achieving greater post-treatment stability, strengthened stated preferences for additional children. The findings establish a positive spillover from employment-support policies to both child development investments and fertility intentions, suggesting that reducing barriers to maternal labor market return can simultaneously address concerns about declining fertility and child human capital formation. The experimental design enables credible identification of causal effects across employment, fertility, and child outcome margins.

## 4. The Labor Market and Health Impacts of Reducing Cesarean Section Deliveries [Health]
**Authors:** Sarah Miller, Petra Persson, Maya Rossin-Slater, Laura R. Wherry
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18629
**Abstract:** The researchers study the consequences of an intervention that reduced cesarean delivery rates among first-time mothers classified as low medical risk, using California administrative birth records linked to quarterly earnings data. Exposed mothers were approximately 8 percent less likely to deliver by cesarean section, with no detectable adverse maternal or infant health effects. Evidence indicates that these mothers showed higher likelihood of employment and of returning to their pre-birth employer in the quarter following delivery, though labor market advantages attenuated over subsequent quarters. Among mothers who went on to have additional children, exposure to the initial intervention reduced both repeat cesarean rates and preterm birth risk, suggesting that the documented effects on long-run maternal and child health may exceed the immediate estimates. The results contribute to a growing literature on how mode of delivery shapes mothers' trajectories in both health and labor market dimensions.

## 5. The Emergence of the Poverty–Mental Health Gradient and the Great Pakistan Earthquake of 2005 [Health]
**Authors:** Tahir Andrabi, Benjamin Daniels, Jishnu Das
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35060
**Abstract:** The paper investigates why the correlation between poverty and mental health varies substantially across populations and contexts. Using survey data collected four years after the 2005 Pakistan earthquake from 126 villages spanning varying distances from the activated fault line, the authors document that in unaffected areas consumption poverty bears no relationship to mental health status. In villages closest to the epicenter, however, a one log-unit increase in consumption is associated with a 0.12 standard deviation improvement in mental health, a gradient that disappears beyond approximately 20 kilometers. The design exploits geographic distance from the fault line as a natural experiment, generating quasi-random variation in exposure to mass adversity. The findings indicate that large-scale shocks can activate poverty-mental health linkages that are otherwise dormant, with implications for understanding when and where antipoverty policies will generate mental health co-benefits and for targeting mental health interventions in post-disaster recovery contexts.
