# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-21

## 1. Does Field of Study Shape the Gender Wage Gap? The Role of Migration Background [Gender]
**Authors:** Louise Devos, François Rycx, Thomas Senterre, Mélanie Volral
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18640
**Abstract:** Using employer-employee matched data on over 62,000 master's degree holders, this paper examines how field of study interacts with gender and migration background to shape wage outcomes. Returns vary widely across fields, with law, economics and management, and science yielding the highest returns. Women consistently earn less than men across all fields, with gaps largest in the most lucrative disciplines. First-generation immigrants from developing countries face the lowest wage returns regardless of field, while second-generation immigrants partially but incompletely close the gap with native-born workers. Educational specialization explains a substantial share of gender wage differences among natives and second-generation immigrants, though broader disadvantage overshadows field-of-study effects for first-generation immigrants. Outcomes also vary by parental origin and age at immigration.

## 2. Targeting Attitudes to Combat Sexual Harassment: A Randomized Intervention in the Norwegian Military [Gender]
**Authors:** Olle Folke, Torbjørn Hansen, Åshild Johnsen, Andreas Kotsadam, Johanna Rickne
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18637
**Abstract:** This paper presents the first field experiment evaluating whether an information-based prevention program reduces the prevalence of sexual harassment in a workplace setting. The randomized control trial was conducted among military recruits in Norwegian boot camp. Treated recruits received two pieces of information: that sexualized jokes can constitute sexual harassment, and that female soldiers perform equally to male soldiers on military skill assessments. The intervention produced lasting improvements in knowledge about what constitutes sexual harassment and about women's job performance. Effects on actual harassment incidents were directionally negative but statistically insignificant. Survey-based evidence on responses to harassment scenarios suggests the intervention likely influenced behavior, with measurement limitations constraining precision. The paper provides guidance for the design and evaluation of future workplace harassment prevention programs.

## 3. Supporting Mothers Back to Work: Experimental Evidence on Employment, Fertility, and Child Outcomes [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniela Del Boca, Luca Favero, Chiara D. Pronzato
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18630
**Abstract:** This paper studies how work–family reconciliation policies affect mothers who reduced or exited employment following childbirth. Using experimental variation in access to reconciliation support, the study finds that improving work–family reconciliation can support mothers' labor market reintegration, promote investments in existing children, and strengthen fertility aspirations when the policy environment provides greater stability. The findings speak to the challenges posed by persistently low fertility rates and population aging in advanced economies, positioning the joint determination of maternal employment and childbearing as central to economic and demographic sustainability. Results suggest that well-designed reconciliation policies can generate joint gains for labor supply, child development, and fertility.

## 4. The Labor Market and Health Impacts of Reducing Cesarean Section Deliveries [Health]
**Authors:** Sarah Miller, Petra Persson, Maya Rossin-Slater, Laura R. Wherry
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18629
**Abstract:** This paper examines the labor market and health consequences of an intervention that reduced cesarean section deliveries among low-risk first-time mothers. Using California birth records linked to earnings data, the study finds that exposed mothers were 8% less likely to have a c-section, with no adverse health effects. Mothers who avoided c-sections showed suggestive evidence of increased likelihood of returning to their pre-birth employer and had higher within-firm earnings rankings in the quarter after birth, though these gains diminished subsequently. Mothers with subsequent pregnancies experienced lower rates of cesarean sections and preterm delivery, suggesting that the total benefits of reducing unnecessary c-sections may exceed initial estimates. The paper provides new evidence on how delivery mode affects the joint health and labor market trajectories of new mothers.

## 5. The Working Life of a Teacher: Lessons from the Danish Matched Student-Teacher Data [Education]
**Authors:** Maria Knoth Humlum, Helena Skyt Nielsen, Marianne Simonsen
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18645
**Abstract:** This paper examines the careers and working conditions of Danish public school teachers using matched administrative register data linking students and their teachers. The analysis reveals pronounced gender sorting by subject: women concentrate in language instruction while men are overrepresented in advanced mathematics. Teachers tend to select subjects aligned with their own academic strengths, but a non-trivial share teach subjects in which they have limited foundational knowledge. Many teachers specialize in particular age groups while managing multiple disciplines simultaneously. Teachers with stronger qualifications sort into schools employing similarly accomplished colleagues, raising questions about school quality inequality. The findings offer empirical grounding for education policy discussions around teacher satisfaction, retention, subject-specific qualifications, and the distribution of teaching talent across schools.
