# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-22

## 1. Science on the Move: How Experiential Pedagogy Shapes Human Capital [Education]
**Authors:** Nitin Bharti, Samreen Malik, Abhiroop Mukhopadhyay, Nishith Prakash
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18677
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a cluster-randomized controlled trial across 132 public schools in Uttar Pradesh, India, examining two discovery-based science teaching approaches. The high-intensity Mobile Science Lab (MSL) improves motivational beliefs and self-confidence by 0.15–0.18 standard deviations, reduces perceived barriers to education by 0.23 standard deviations, raises engagement by 0.17–0.22 standard deviations, and increases standardized test scores by 0.22–0.34 standard deviations across all subjects. The lower-intensity Lab on Bike variant showed more limited benefits, with improvements primarily among students attending all sessions. The findings underscore that pedagogical design and delivery intensity are critical determinants of multidimensional human capital formation, and that discovery-based pedagogy can shift motivational beliefs, engagement, and achievement in low-capacity public school systems.

## 2. Are Gender Norms Shaped by Who Earns More? [Gender]
**Authors:** Hanna Brosch, Elisabeth Grewenig, Philipp Lergetporer, Katharina Werner, Helen Zeidler
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18661
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a large-scale representative vignette experiment in Germany with over 10,000 participants to investigate whether parental earnings influence gender attitudes toward childcare roles. They randomly varied pre-childbirth earnings and measured recommendations about which parent should stay home with a child. Without specifying earnings, 90% recommend that the mother stay home; when the mother earned less, this figure rose to 93%. However, when she earned more, recommendations that the mother stay home dropped to 47%. The authors conclude that economic circumstances do shape gender attitudes, but deeply rooted norms persist even when they conflict with financial incentives, rejecting a purely income-based explanation for gender preferences about parental labor allocation.

## 3. Supporting Mothers Back to Work: Experimental Evidence on Employment, Fertility, and Child Outcomes [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniela Del Boca, Luca Favero, Chiara D. Pronzato
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18630
**Abstract:** The paper examines how work-family reconciliation policies affect mothers who curtailed employment due to childcare responsibilities. Using a randomized controlled trial, the authors find that improving work–family reconciliation can support mothers' labor market reintegration, promote investments in existing children, and, under conditions of greater stability, strengthen fertility desires. The study demonstrates that targeted interventions addressing practical barriers to maternal employment can produce simultaneous gains in labor supply, child development, and fertility intentions, with implications for the design of family-friendly policies in countries with low maternal employment rates.

## 4. Stepping Stone or Exit Path: Experimental Evidence on Training the Long-Term Unemployed [Health]
**Authors:** Jacob Nielsen Arendt, Iben Bolvig
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18618
**Abstract:** The study evaluates an employment initiative targeting economically disadvantaged jobless individuals through a randomized controlled trial. The program emphasized on-the-job training, contracting the unemployed for paid work hours as a stepping stone into the labour market. Results indicated the intervention expedited transitions to part-time positions, yet produced an unexpected consequence: it permanently increased the share of participants receiving disability pensions among the most disadvantaged groups. The authors propose that while training boosted productivity for some participants, it simultaneously generated employability information leveraged in disability pension assessments. The findings highlight an important tension in active labour market policy: interventions designed to promote employment may inadvertently accelerate exits from the labor force into disability for the most vulnerable workers.

## 5. Parenthood and Productivity [Fertility]
**Authors:** Diogo Britto, Caio De Holanda, Bruno Ferman, Alexandre Fonseca, Breno Sampaio, Lucas Warwar
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18658
**Abstract:** The researchers investigate whether becoming a parent affects workers' job performance, particularly regarding employment penalties experienced by mothers. They studied judges because this profession allows precise measurement of output, provides data before and after childbirth as judges rarely leave the profession, and features balanced workload distribution. Using a difference-in-differences methodology, they found no evidence that mothers' — and fathers' — output declines during pregnancy or after they return from parental leave. The analysis examined both quantity and quality metrics of judicial work, with similar findings among self-employed labor attorneys. The study concludes that motherhood does not necessarily reduce workplace productivity, suggesting that child penalties in employment may not be driven by lasting declines in on-the-job productivity in professional contexts where output is measurable and workload is distributed.
