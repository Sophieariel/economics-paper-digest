# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-23

## 1. Gender Mix and Team Performance: Evidence from Obstetrics [Gender]
**Authors:** Ambar La Forgia, Manasvini Singh
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35084
**Abstract:** The researchers examine how team composition by gender influences performance in high-stakes medical settings. Using data on 2.5 million births, they analyze outcomes when patients are assigned to two-member obstetrician teams with different gender configurations. Female-only teams achieve the best maternal outcomes, whereas male-only teams have the worst: severe maternal complications are 15.8% higher in male-only teams and 7.1–10.8% higher in mixed-gender teams relative to female-only teams. The study identifies gender norms as the underlying mechanism — female-only teams demonstrate superior collaborative decision-making and team resilience. Notably, female-only teams are the only team type to eliminate racial disparities in maternal outcomes for Black women. The research suggests these patterns reflect how gender composition directly shapes team dynamics and clinical decisions rather than differences in physician skill or patient selection, with implications for managing diverse professional workplaces and designing clinical staffing policies.

## 2. Are Gender Norms Shaped by Who Earns More? [Gender]
**Authors:** Hanna Brosch, Elisabeth Grewenig, Philipp Lergetporer, Katharina Werner, Helen Zeidler
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18661
**Abstract:** The researchers conduct a large-scale representative vignette experiment in Germany (N > 10,000) to investigate whether people's gender attitudes regarding parental labor allocation are driven by earnings differences. Without earnings information specified, 90% of respondents recommend mothers stay home with children; when mothers earn less, 93% make the same recommendation. When mothers earn more, this drops to 47% — yet nearly half still recommend mothers stay home. The study concludes that economic circumstances shape gender attitudes, but deeply rooted norms persist even when they conflict with financial incentives. The asymmetric response pattern rejects a purely income-based explanation for gender norms surrounding work and caregiving responsibilities, with implications for understanding the persistence of the gender pay gap and occupational segregation.

## 3. Class Peers as Competitors and Educators: The Consequences of Rank-Based Academic Rewards [Education]
**Authors:** Mark R. Rosenzweig, Bing Xu
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18676
**Abstract:** This research investigates how competitive ranking systems in schools influence student behavior and classroom dynamics in settings where peers function simultaneously as rivals and collaborative learning partners. Using U.S. high school panel data and refugee placement records as a source of quasi-experimental variation in peer ability, the authors explore how shifts in peer ability levels impact student conduct under various competition structures. Their theoretical tournament model predicts that when higher-achieving students enter a cohort, existing high performers will reduce their homework commitment and curtail academic assistance to classmates — particularly in rank-based reward environments. The empirical findings validate these predictions, demonstrating that high-performing students in competitive schools spend less time on homework and forgo collaborative learning benefits when facing stronger competition. The results have implications for school design, academic reward structures, and human capital accumulation in environments with heterogeneous student ability.

## 4. Parenthood and Productivity [Fertility]
**Authors:** Diogo Britto, Caio De Holanda, Bruno Ferman, Alexandre Fonseca, Breno Sampaio, Lucas Warwar
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18658
**Abstract:** The researchers investigate whether becoming a parent affects workers' job performance and whether any productivity decline explains observed employment penalties for mothers. They study judges — a population that allows precise output measurement and continuous employment records regardless of parental status — using a difference-in-differences methodology. They find no evidence that mothers' or fathers' output declines during pregnancy or after returning from parental leave, and credibly rule out moderate productivity losses across multiple measures of judicial work quality and quantity. Comparable findings emerge among self-employed labor attorneys. The study concludes that motherhood does not inherently reduce on-the-job productivity, suggesting that documented employment and wage penalties for mothers stem from factors other than lasting declines in workplace performance — such as employer discrimination, statistical discrimination, or constraints on job flexibility — rather than from actual productivity differences.

## 5. Does Employment Slow Cognitive Decline? Evidence from Labor Market Shocks [Health]
**Authors:** Noah Arman Kouchekinia, David Neumark, Tim A. Bruckner
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35117
**Abstract:** The researchers investigate whether workforce participation helps preserve cognitive function in older adults. Using data from the Health and Retirement Study, they employ local labor market shocks as an instrumental variable for employment to establish causality between work and cognitive trajectories. Their key finding is that substantial declines over time in cognitive scores stem from negative labor demand shocks. The effect concentrates among men aged 51–64, whose employment is more responsive to local labor market conditions than that of older workers. The study extends prior research beyond the traditional retirement-age window and provides causal evidence suggesting that working to older ages may delay age-related cognitive decline. The results have implications for disability policy, retirement age policy, and the design of programs that promote workforce attachment among older workers.
