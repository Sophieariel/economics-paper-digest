# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-24

## 1. Medical Innovation and Racial Health Disparities: Evidence from a Breakthrough Treatment [Health]
**Authors:** D. Mark Anderson, Kerwin Kofi Charles, Daniel I. Rees
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35210
**Abstract:** The researchers investigate whether medical breakthroughs help or harm efforts to reduce racial disparities in health outcomes. Their case study examines surfactant replacement therapy (SRT), an FDA-approved treatment in 1989 for respiratory disorders in premature infants. Before SRT's introduction, Black premature infants had lower respiratory-related mortality rates than White infants. However, following FDA approval, this gap narrowed dramatically within years. Using mortality data from 1980–2000 and a difference-in-differences event-study design centered on the FDA approval date, the authors find both groups benefited from SRT, yet White neonates saw larger and more immediate reductions in mortality: by 1993, SRT had reduced respiratory-related mortality among White neonates by 46 percent, compared to 30 percent for Black neonates. The researchers ruled out healthcare access disparities as the explanation, suggesting instead that racial differences in fetal pulmonary maturation likely account for the unequal treatment benefits. The findings contribute to the broader health economics literature on technology diffusion, racial health gaps, and the distributional consequences of medical innovation for labor-market-relevant health outcomes.

## 2. The Long-Run Effects of the Affordable Care Act: Evidence from a Partially Pre-Committed Research Design Over the COVID-19 Recession and Recovery [Health]
**Authors:** Jeffrey Clemens, Anwita Mahajan, Joseph J. Sabia
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35190
**Abstract:** The researchers examine whether adjustment delays would cause the ACA's long-run effects on health insurance coverage and employment to exceed its short-run effects. They extended a set of pre-committed event-study specifications through the COVID-19 recession and recovery — a period expected to amplify coverage and employment impacts via delayed behavioral adjustment. Contrary to the hypothesis, they find that the ACA's estimated impacts remained stable through the pandemic: long-run employment and employer-sponsored coverage effects were modest and significantly smaller than initially anticipated. The paper demonstrates how partially pre-committed research designs, in which the empirical specifications are fixed before new data become available, generate reliable long-run evidence while constraining specification-searching. The findings have direct implications for assessing the employment consequences of health insurance tied to jobs — a central question in the health-insurance-and-employment literature — and illustrate the value of credible research-design discipline in evaluating major policy interventions.

## 3. Supporting Mothers Back to Work: Experimental Evidence on Employment, Fertility, and Child Outcomes [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniela Del Boca, Luca Favero, Chiara D. Pronzato
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18630
**Abstract:** The paper addresses demographic challenges facing advanced economies — persistently low fertility rates and population aging — by evaluating a randomized intervention designed to help mothers who had reduced their employment due to childcare duties re-enter the labor market. The experimental design allows the authors to credibly identify the causal effects of work-family reconciliation support on mothers' labor market outcomes, investments in existing children, and fertility intentions. Findings indicate that improving work-family reconciliation can support mothers' labor market reintegration and promote human-capital investments in existing children, and that under conditions of greater maternal employment stability, fertility desires strengthen. The study positions mothers at the center of demographic policy analysis, documenting how motherhood represents a critical juncture that shapes subsequent employment and family-formation decisions. The results contribute to the literatures on maternity leave, childcare policy, fertility decisions, and the labor market consequences of childbearing in low-fertility advanced economies.

## 4. Does Field of Study Shape the Gender Wage Gap? The Role of Migration Background [Gender]
**Authors:** Louise Devos, François Rycx, Thomas Senterre, Mélanie Volral
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18640
**Abstract:** The researchers analyze data on over 62,000 master's graduates to examine how wage returns to educational fields differ by migration status and contribute to gender wage inequality. Key findings indicate that returns vary widely across fields, with law, economics and management, and science yielding the highest returns, and that women earn considerably less than men across all fields of study. First-generation immigrants from developing nations experience the lowest wage returns regardless of field, while second-generation immigrants partially close but do not eliminate the native-immigrant gap. The study reveals that educational field choice explains a substantial share of gender wage inequality among natives and second-generation immigrants, but that broader structural disadvantages predominate for first-generation immigrants. Wage penalties for women and immigrants also fluctuate based on parental origin countries and age at immigration. The findings contribute to the literatures on gender wage gaps, returns to education, occupational segregation, and the intersection of gender and migration in determining labor market outcomes, with implications for educational guidance policy and anti-discrimination enforcement.

## 5. The Gender Gap in Digital Skills at Work [Gender]
**Authors:** Adele Whelan, Luke Brosnan, Seamus McGuinness
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18675
**Abstract:** The researchers examine gender differences in digital skills usage across European workplaces, uncovering a notable disparity in which women perform advanced digital tasks at substantially lower rates than men. The raw 16 percentage-point gap between genders is only partially explained by conventional factors such as education level or occupation, and using detailed decomposition methods the unexplained component widens significantly among workers in highly digital-intensive roles. While educational and occupational sorting accounts for some differences at higher digital-intensity levels, persistent unexplained gaps suggest the presence of structural, cultural, or organizational barriers to women's full utilization of digital skills on the job. Notably, younger women already experience larger gaps than their older counterparts, indicating this is an ongoing and potentially worsening phenomenon rather than a legacy of past educational disadvantage. The findings contribute to the literatures on gender discrimination, occupational segregation, and the evolving role of technology in shaping gender-based labor market inequality, with implications for firm-level human resource practices and labor market policy in digitizing economies.
