# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-25

## 1. Science on the Move: How Experiential Pedagogy Shapes Human Capital [Education]
**Authors:** Nitin Bharti, Samreen Malik, Abhiroop Mukhopadhyay, Nishith Prakash
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18677
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a randomized controlled trial across 132 public schools in Uttar Pradesh, India, to assess two discovery-based science teaching approaches: a high-intensity Mobile Science Lab and a lower-intensity Lab on Bike program. The high-intensity intervention produced substantial improvements, raising motivational beliefs and self-confidence by 0.15–0.18 standard deviations, reducing educational barriers, and boosting test scores by 0.22–0.34 standard deviations. The lower-intensity Lab on Bike program yielded more modest outcomes, with benefits concentrated among fully participating students. The study concludes that pedagogical design quality and implementation intensity significantly shape human capital development in resource-limited school settings, with implications for education policy targeting public schools in low- and middle-income countries.

## 2. The Gender Gap in Digital Skills at Work [Gender]
**Authors:** Adele Whelan, Luke Brosnan, Seamus McGuinness
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18675
**Abstract:** This study examines gender disparities in digital skill utilization across European workplaces using linked employer–employee data. The authors document a raw gender gap of approximately 16 percentage points in advanced digital task performance, with only 30 percent explained by measurable worker and job characteristics. The gap intensifies at higher levels of digital intensity, where women remain notably underrepresented even after controlling for education and occupational sorting. Persistent unexplained gaps across specifications point to organizational and structural impediments rather than differences in observable qualifications. Notably, younger women already face larger gaps than older cohorts, indicating that the challenge is ongoing rather than historical, and raising concerns about the career and earnings implications of the gender divide in digital skill use as technology-intensive work expands.

## 3. Are Gender Norms Shaped by Who Earns More? [Gender]
**Authors:** Hanna Brosch, Elisabeth Grewenig, Philipp Lergetporer, Katharina Werner, Helen Zeidler
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18661
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a large-scale vignette experiment with over 10,000 participants in Germany to test whether earnings information influences gender attitudes about parental work arrangements. When no earnings information was provided, 90 percent of respondents recommended that mothers remain at home. This preference held at 93 percent when mothers earned less, but dropped sharply to 47 percent when mothers earned more than fathers. Despite this sensitivity to economic information, nearly half of respondents continued to favor maternal caregiving roles even when financial incentives favored the opposite. The findings indicate that economic circumstances shape gender attitudes at the margin, but deeply rooted norms persist even in conflict with material incentives, implying that wage equalization alone cannot fully eliminate gendered labor supply expectations or close maternal employment gaps.

## 4. Parenthood and Productivity [Fertility]
**Authors:** Diogo Britto, Caio De Holanda, Bruno Ferman, Alexandre Fonseca, Breno Sampaio, Lucas Warwar
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18658
**Abstract:** This paper tests whether becoming a parent reduces on-the-job productivity, a mechanism often invoked to explain employment penalties experienced by mothers. The authors study Brazilian judges, whose output is directly measurable, whose workload is quasi-randomly assigned, and who rarely exit the profession. Using an event-study design comparing judicial output before and after childbirth, the study finds no evidence that mothers'—or fathers'—output declines during pregnancy or following return from parental leave. Results are robust across multiple measures of both the quantity and quality of judicial decisions, and are replicated among self-employed attorneys. The findings suggest that child penalties in employment are not driven by lasting declines in on-the-job productivity, pointing instead to demand-side factors such as employer discrimination or statistical discrimination as the operative mechanisms.

## 5. The Long-Run Effects of the Affordable Care Act: Evidence from a Partially Pre-Committed Research Design Over the COVID-19 Recession and Recovery [Health]
**Authors:** Jeffrey Clemens, Anwita Mahajan, Joseph J. Sabia
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35190
**Abstract:** This paper examines the long-run effects of the Affordable Care Act (ACA) on health insurance coverage and employment, using a partially pre-committed event-study design intended to reduce specification bias. The authors tested whether substantial labor market churn during the COVID-19 pandemic and recovery would amplify ACA effects through delayed adjustment mechanisms. Contrary to this hypothesis, estimated effects remained stable throughout the pandemic period. Long-run effects on employment and employer-sponsored coverage were modest and substantially smaller than initially projected in prior literature. The findings illustrate how pre-committed research designs can generate credible long-run estimates in settings subject to evolving macroeconomic conditions, and contribute to ongoing debates about the labor market consequences of major health insurance expansions.
