# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-26

## 1. Are Gender Norms Shaped by Who Earns More? [Gender]
**Authors:** Hanna Brosch, Elisabeth Grewenig, Philipp Lergetporer, Katharina Werner, Helen Zeidler
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18661
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a large-scale vignette experiment in Germany (N > 10,000) to explore how parental earnings influence gender attitudes about work and childcare. Participants were presented with hypothetical couples differing in relative earnings and asked to recommend which partner should reduce labor supply after having a child. Without specifying earnings, 90% recommend that the mother stay home. When the mother earned more, this dropped to 47%—yet nearly half of respondents still recommend that the mother stay home despite financial incentives favoring alternative arrangements. The study demonstrates that while economic circumstances shift gender attitudes in a progressive direction, deeply rooted norms persist even when they conflict with financial incentives. The experimental design allows clean causal identification of the role of earnings information in shaping expressed preferences, providing new evidence on the malleability—and stickiness—of gender norms around market work and childcare.

## 2. The (Lack of) Efficacy of Social Belonging and Growth Mindset Interventions Among College Students [Education]
**Authors:** Abid N. Alam, Philip Oreopoulos, Uros Petronijevic
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35230
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted four large-scale randomized controlled trials across two Canadian universities involving nearly 12,000 students to assess the impact of social belonging and growth mindset interventions—widely adopted programs designed to improve academic persistence and performance. They found no immediate or dynamic effects on student grades and no effect on persistence through university from either type of intervention. Using machine learning methods to examine heterogeneous treatment effects, they found no evidence of meaningful variation in treatment effects across student subgroups defined by gender, race, first-generation status, or prior academic performance. The null results, obtained at scale and with careful experimental design, challenge the external validity of earlier positive findings from smaller trials and raise questions about the scalability of brief psychological interventions as tools for improving higher-education outcomes.

## 3. Parenthood and Productivity [Fertility]
**Authors:** Diogo Britto, Caio De Holanda, Bruno Ferman, Alexandre Fonseca, Breno Sampaio, Lucas Warwar
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18658
**Abstract:** The researchers investigate whether becoming a parent reduces on-the-job productivity, a mechanism often proposed to explain the well-documented child earnings penalty. They exploit quasi-experimental variation among Brazilian judges—whose output (cases decided) can be measured objectively, workloads are randomly assigned, and employment records are complete—using a difference-in-differences design that compares output before and after childbirth. They find no evidence that mothers' or fathers' output declines during pregnancy or after they return from parental leave, and document similar null results among self-employed labor lawyers. The findings suggest that the large and persistent earnings penalties associated with parenthood, particularly for mothers, are unlikely to arise from reduced on-the-job performance, and instead likely reflect employer discrimination, reduced hours, or career interruptions rather than actual productivity losses.

## 4. The Long-Run Effects of the Affordable Care Act: Evidence from a Partially Pre-Committed Research Design Over the COVID-19 Recession and Recovery [Health]
**Authors:** Jeffrey Clemens, Anwita Mahajan, Joseph J. Sabia
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35190
**Abstract:** The researchers examine whether adjustment frictions and time would cause the ACA's long-run impacts on employment and health insurance coverage to diverge from short-term estimates. Using pre-committed extensions of event study specifications previously applied for short-run analyses, they tested whether pandemic-related insurance churn and labor market disruptions would amplify the law's measured long-run effects. Findings show that the ACA's effects on coverage remained stable through the pandemic period, and that long-run effects on employment and employer-sponsored coverage were modest and much smaller than initially projected by proponents and opponents alike. The pre-committed design provides credible identification by locking in the empirical specification prior to observing outcomes, reducing concerns about specification search and lending additional credibility to the null employment effects.

## 5. The Gender Gap in Digital Skills at Work [Gender]
**Authors:** Adele Whelan, Luke Brosnan, Seamus McGuinness
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18675
**Abstract:** The research examines gender disparities in digital skill utilization across European workplaces using task-based analysis and Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition. Women are significantly less likely to perform advanced digital tasks, with a raw gap of approximately 16 percentage points. Only 30% of this gap relates to measurable differences in education, occupation, or industry; the majority remains unexplained and likely reflects structural or organizational barriers. The gap intensifies at higher levels of digital work intensity, where women remain substantially underrepresented. Notably, younger women already experience larger unexplained gaps than older cohorts, indicating this is an ongoing rather than fading generational concern. The findings carry important implications for labor market inequality in an era of increasing digitalization, suggesting that policies targeting occupational sorting and educational attainment alone will be insufficient to close the emerging digital skills gender gap.
