# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-28

## 1. Science on the Move: How Experiential Pedagogy Shapes Human Capital [Education]
**Authors:** Nitin Bharti, Samreen Malik, Abhiroop Mukhopadhyay, Nishith Prakash
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18677
**Abstract:** This paper evaluates two discovery-based science teaching programs — a high-intensity Mobile Science Lab (MSL) and a lower-intensity Lab on Bike (LoB) — through a randomized controlled trial spanning 132 public schools in Uttar Pradesh, India. The MSL assigns a fully equipped mobile laboratory to schools for several days at a time, allowing students to conduct hands-on experiments; the LoB delivers a smaller kit carried by bicycle to remote classrooms. Comparing treated and control classrooms across a rich set of outcomes, the authors find that the high-intensity MSL improved students' motivational beliefs and self-confidence by 0.15–0.18 standard deviations and raised standardized science test scores by 0.22–0.34 standard deviations. The lower-intensity LoB produced more limited gains, concentrated among students who attended all sessions. Results demonstrate that pedagogical design quality and implementation intensity are first-order determinants of human capital accumulation: intermittent, low-dose experiential interventions generate little learning, while sustained, well-resourced programs can meaningfully improve achievement and non-cognitive skills in under-resourced public school systems. The findings have direct implications for education policy, particularly the design of scalable inquiry-based science initiatives in low- and middle-income countries.

## 2. Does Field of Study Shape the Gender Wage Gap? The Role of Migration Background [Gender]
**Authors:** Louise Devos, François Rycx, Thomas Senterre, Mélanie Volral
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18640
**Abstract:** This paper examines how gender differences in wage returns to fields of study vary by migration background using a large matched employer-employee dataset covering more than 62,000 master's degree graduates in Belgium. The authors estimate wage equations that interact gender, field of study, and immigrant generation, allowing the gender wage gap to differ across disciplines and by nativity. Women earn less than men across all fields of study, but the gap is particularly pronounced in high-paying disciplines such as engineering, economics, and the natural sciences. First-generation immigrants from developing countries face the lowest wage returns regardless of field choice, placing them at a double disadvantage relative to native-born graduates in competitive majors. Second-generation immigrants' outcomes fall between those of natives and first-generation immigrants, consistent with a pattern of gradual assimilation. The analysis reveals that sorting into fields of study accounts for a meaningful share of the overall gender wage gap but leaves a large, field-specific residual unexplained, pointing to within-field discrimination or unobserved skill differentials. The findings underscore that gender wage disparities in graduate labor markets are shaped by an interaction of field selection, migration background, and employer-level pay practices, with implications for both anti-discrimination policy and targeted support for immigrant workers.

## 3. Supporting Mothers Back to Work: Experimental Evidence on Employment, Fertility, and Child Outcomes [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniela Del Boca, Luca Favero, Chiara D. Pronzato
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18630
**Abstract:** Against the backdrop of persistently low fertility rates and population aging in advanced economies, this paper uses a randomized field experiment to evaluate an intervention designed to help mothers who had reduced their working hours due to childcare demands reintegrate into the labor market. The treated group received personalized job coaching, childcare information, and access to flexible work arrangements through partnerships with local employers. The authors track outcomes on maternal employment, hours worked, earnings, investments in existing children, and stated fertility intentions over a two-year follow-up period. Results show that strengthening work-family balance through the bundled intervention significantly raises the probability of maternal reemployment and increases investments in current children's education and health. Importantly, under conditions of greater employment stability, the program also strengthens mothers' fertility desires, suggesting that family-friendly reconciliation policies can simultaneously address labor force participation shortfalls and demographic sustainability concerns. The experimental design allows clean identification of causal effects, and the multi-dimensional outcome analysis demonstrates that policies targeting maternal employment need not trade off child welfare against women's labor market attachment. The findings have direct implications for the design of parental leave and childcare policy in low-fertility countries.

## 4. The Labor Market and Health Impacts of Reducing Cesarean Section Deliveries [Health]
**Authors:** Sarah Miller, Petra Persson, Maya Rossin-Slater, Laura R. Wherry
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18629
**Abstract:** This paper studies the causal effects of reducing unnecessary cesarean section deliveries on maternal health and labor market outcomes using California administrative birth records linked to employer-employee earnings data. The authors exploit an intervention that reduced c-section rates among low-risk, first-time mothers to identify the effects of delivery mode on subsequent maternal outcomes. Mothers exposed to the intervention experienced an 8% reduction in cesarean deliveries with no evidence of adverse health consequences for mother or child. On the labor market side, treated mothers were significantly more likely to return to their original employer and achieved higher within-firm earnings rankings in the quarter immediately following delivery, consistent with faster recovery and earlier return to work. However, these labor market advantages dissipated within two to three years of birth. Among mothers who went on to have a second child, the intervention was also associated with reduced rates of repeat c-section and preterm delivery, suggesting the estimated benefits are a lower bound on the long-run gains from preventing an initial unnecessary cesarean. The results contribute to a growing literature on how childbirth practices shape maternal human capital trajectories and highlight a policy lever — reducing low-value obstetric procedures — that can simultaneously improve health outcomes and labor market attachment for new mothers.

## 5. Class Peers as Competitors and Educators: The Consequences of Rank-Based Academic Rewards [Education]
**Authors:** Mark R. Rosenzweig, Bing Xu
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18676
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how rank-based incentive structures in schools — where students compete for rewards tied to their ordinal position in the class distribution — affect individual study effort and peer-to-peer knowledge transmission. The authors note that classmates serve a dual role: they are simultaneously rivals for positional rewards and potential sources of academic support. Using high school panel data from the United States combined with exogenous variation in classroom peer composition from a refugee placement program, the paper identifies how shifts in the ability distribution of classmates affect effort and cooperation under different competition frameworks. A tournament model with endogenous peer teaching predicts that introducing higher-ability peers decreases effort and reduces cooperative peer teaching among top performers in rank-dependent systems, while the reverse holds when weaker peers arrive. Empirical results confirm these predictions: high-achieving students in rank-competitive environments reduce their study time and lose access to peer-teaching benefits when higher-ability classmates enter their cohort. The findings reveal an important unintended consequence of rank-based academic incentives — they can suppress the informal knowledge diffusion that would otherwise benefit lower-performing students — with implications for the design of grading schemes, classroom competition policies, and education systems in which peer learning is a key input to human capital formation.
