# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-29

## 1. Gender-Specific Economic Shocks and Household Bargaining Power [Gender]
**Authors:** Rania Gihleb, Osea Giuntella, Dor Morag
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35118
**Abstract:** This paper examines how labor market shocks that differentially affect men and women alter household bargaining power and spending decisions. The authors construct a machine-learning-based index of product "genderedness" using consumer purchasing data. They then study two natural experiments: industrial robot adoption, which reduced men's relative earnings, and fracking expansion, which benefited young male workers. Robot exposure shifts household consumption toward products predominantly purchased by single women, and reallocates spending toward goods more commonly purchased for daughters than sons, despite no significant change in total children's product spending. Fracking expansion shows weaker evidence of shifting spending toward male-preferred goods. The findings provide evidence that intra-household bargaining power responds to gender-specific labor demand shocks and shapes household consumption allocations.

## 2. Beliefs, Attention, and Investments in Early Childhood [Education]
**Authors:** Flavio Cunha, Snejana Nihtianova, Jessica Rood, Anja-Lize van der Merwe
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35150
**Abstract:** This paper presents a model of how resource-constrained parents form distorted beliefs about the value of responsive interaction with young children. When parents lack sufficient capacity to meet their goals, they engage in motivated reasoning to justify reduced engagement by downgrading their beliefs about potential developmental returns. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle in which inadequate parenting prevents accumulation of evidence that might challenge those beliefs. The paper identifies four intervention pathways that can disrupt this pattern and presents results from a randomized controlled trial supporting the model's predictions about parental beliefs, parenting behaviors, and child engagement. The findings have implications for the design of early childhood programs and the targeting of parental investments in human capital.

## 3. Science on the Move: How Experiential Pedagogy Shapes Human Capital [Education]
**Authors:** Nitin Bharti, Samreen Malik, Abhiroop Mukhopadhyay, Nishith Prakash
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18677
**Abstract:** This paper evaluates two guided discovery-based science teaching interventions using a cluster-randomized controlled trial across 132 public schools in Uttar Pradesh, India, involving thousands of students. A high-intensity Mobile Science Lab treatment produced improvements in motivational beliefs, self-confidence, and standardized test scores of 0.22–0.34 standard deviations, and reduced perceived educational barriers. A lower-intensity Lab on Bike treatment showed limited average effects, with gains concentrated among students attending all program sessions. The study demonstrates that pedagogical design and delivery intensity are critical determinants of multidimensional human capital formation, with implications for scaling educational interventions in low-income settings.

## 4. Parenthood and Productivity [Fertility]
**Authors:** Diogo Britto, Caio De Holanda, Bruno Ferman, Alexandre Fonseca, Breno Sampaio, Lucas Warwar
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18658
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether becoming a parent affects workplace output. The authors focus on Brazilian judges, an occupation in which output can be measured precisely and workers rarely exit the profession, allowing clean estimation of productivity effects. Using a difference-in-differences methodology, the paper finds no evidence that mothers' or fathers' output declines during pregnancy or after they return from parental leave. This null result holds across both quantity and quality metrics of judicial work, and comparable results are found among self-employed lawyers. The findings challenge the assumption that the "motherhood penalty" in earnings reflects reductions in on-the-job productivity, and suggest that other mechanisms—such as employer discrimination or hours constraints—are more likely drivers of the child penalty in wages.

## 5. Are Gender Norms Shaped by Who Earns More? [Gender]
**Authors:** Hanna Brosch, Elisabeth Grewenig, Philipp Lergetporer, Katharina Werner, Helen Zeidler
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18661
**Abstract:** This paper uses a large-scale vignette experiment with over 10,000 participants in Germany to examine how parental earnings shape gender attitudes about work and childcare arrangements. Without specifying earnings, 90 percent of respondents recommend that the mother stay home. When the mother earns more than the father, this share drops to 47 percent. The experiment documents that while economic incentives can shift stated gender norms, financial considerations alone do not fully account for persistent recommendations that mothers assume primary caregiving roles, even when this is economically inefficient. The findings suggest that deeply rooted cultural norms persist alongside economic considerations and may limit the effectiveness of wage-based policies aimed at closing gender gaps in labor force participation and the division of household labor.
