# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-30

## 1. Supporting Mothers Back to Work: Experimental Evidence on Employment, Fertility, and Child Outcomes [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniela Del Boca, Luca Favero, Chiara D. Pronzato
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18630
**Abstract:** This paper examines how advanced economies facing low fertility and population aging can simultaneously sustain maternal labor force participation. The authors use experimental evidence from an intervention targeting mothers who had reduced their working hours due to childcare constraints, randomly assigning access to work-family reconciliation support. They find that the intervention promotes mothers' return to employment, increases investment in children's human capital development, and—in households that achieve greater economic stability—strengthens fertility intentions. The results suggest that work-family balance policies can jointly address labor supply and demographic goals, with implications for the design of childcare and employment support programs.

## 2. The Gender Gap in Digital Skills at Work [Gender]
**Authors:** Adele Whelan, Luke Brosnan, Seamus McGuinness
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18675
**Abstract:** This paper documents a substantial gender gap in digital skill utilization across European workplaces. Using harmonized cross-country data and Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition methods, the authors find a raw gap of 16 percentage points in advanced digital task performance, with women significantly underrepresented in high-intensity digital roles. Only about 30 percent of the gap is explained by observable characteristics such as education, occupation, or sector. The gap widens at higher levels of digital task intensity, and younger women already face larger disparities than older cohorts, suggesting the gap reflects structural and organizational barriers rather than a transitional generational phenomenon. The findings point to labor market inefficiencies that may deepen as automation and digitalization reshape occupational demand.

## 3. Science on the Move: How Experiential Pedagogy Shapes Human Capital [Education]
**Authors:** Nitin Bharti, Samreen Malik, Abhiroop Mukhopadhyay, Nishith Prakash
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18677
**Abstract:** This paper evaluates two guided discovery-based science teaching interventions using a cluster-randomized controlled trial across 132 public schools in Uttar Pradesh, India. A high-intensity Mobile Science Lab treatment produces improvements in motivational beliefs and self-confidence of 0.15–0.18 standard deviations, reduces perceived barriers to education by 0.23 standard deviations, and raises standardized test scores by 0.22–0.34 standard deviations across subjects. A lower-intensity Lab on Bike program shows limited average effects, with gains concentrated among students who participate fully. The results demonstrate that pedagogical design and delivery intensity are critical determinants of multidimensional human capital formation, and that experiential learning can raise both cognitive achievement and non-cognitive skills in under-resourced school systems.

## 4. The Long-Run Effects of the Affordable Care Act: Evidence from a Partially Pre-Committed Research Design Over the COVID-19 Recession and Recovery [Health]
**Authors:** Jeffrey Clemens, Anwita Mahajan, Joseph J. Sabia
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35190
**Abstract:** This paper examines the long-run labor market and insurance coverage effects of the Affordable Care Act (ACA), using a partially pre-committed event study design that spans the COVID-19 recession and recovery. The authors test whether pandemic-driven insurance market disruptions intensified the ACA's effects on employment and employer-sponsored coverage. They find that the ACA's impacts on both margins remained stable through the pandemic, and that long-run effects on employment and employer coverage were modest and substantially smaller than initial projections. The paper also demonstrates how partially pre-committed research designs constrain analytical flexibility and yield more credible long-run causal estimates, contributing to best practices in empirical health economics.

## 5. The (Lack of) Efficacy of Social Belonging and Growth Mindset Interventions Among College Students [Education]
**Authors:** Abid N. Alam, Philip Oreopoulos, Uros Petronijevic
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35230
**Abstract:** This paper reports results from four large-scale randomized controlled trials involving nearly 12,000 students across two Canadian universities, evaluating the causal effects of social belonging and growth mindset interventions on academic outcomes. The authors find no immediate or dynamic effects on student grades and no effect on persistence through university. Machine learning analysis uncovers no evidence of meaningful heterogeneity in treatment effects across student subgroups—including by gender, race, or prior academic achievement. The null results challenge the generalizability of earlier small-sample findings and suggest that interventions shown to be effective in narrow contexts may not scale. The findings have direct implications for education policy investments in social-psychological interventions designed to close gaps in college attainment.
