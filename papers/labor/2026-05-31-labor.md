# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-05-31

## 1. Firm Pay, Amenities, and Inequality [Gender]
**Authors:** Sydnee Caldwell, Ingrid Haegele, and Jörg Heining
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35149
**Abstract:** This paper estimates firm-specific amenity valuations using discrete choice experiments embedded in a large-scale survey of German workers linked to administrative records. Workers evaluated hypothetical job offers from actual firms with randomized wages, allowing the authors to recover money-metric valuations of workplace amenities. The findings reveal substantial variation in amenity valuations across firms and demographic groups, with a single index capturing most of the cross-firm variation. Notably, amenity valuations are largely independent of firm wage premiums, meaning that amenity provision does not reduce between-firm wage inequality. However, gender differences in amenity preferences are pronounced and account for a meaningful share of the observed gender wage gap, providing new microeconomic evidence on the sources of earnings disparity between men and women.

## 2. The (Lack of) Efficacy of Social Belonging and Growth Mindset Interventions Among College Students [Education]
**Authors:** Abid N. Alam, Philip Oreopoulos, and Uros Petronijevic
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35230
**Abstract:** This study conducts four large-scale randomized controlled trials across two major Canadian universities involving nearly 12,000 students to evaluate whether growth mindset and social belonging interventions improve academic performance and institutional persistence. The authors find no immediate or dynamic effects on student grades and no effect on persistence through university, with machine learning analysis uncovering no evidence of meaningful variation in treatment effects across student subgroups including first-generation students, underrepresented minorities, and low-income students. These null results stand in contrast to smaller prior studies and suggest that low-cost scalable psychological interventions of this type do not reliably translate into academic gains when tested at scale in authentic postsecondary settings.

## 3. Expanding Paternity Leave: Effects on Beliefs, Norms, and Gender Gaps [Fertility]
**Authors:** Henrik Kleven, Camille Landais, Anne Sophie S. Lassen, Philip Rosenbaum, Herdis Steingrimsdottir, and Jakob Egholt Søgaard
**Date:** February 2026 (revised March 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34862
**Abstract:** This paper exploits a major Danish paternity leave expansion as a quasi-experimental shock to examine whether policy interventions can reshape beliefs, gender norms, and labor market outcomes. Using rich administrative data combined with survey evidence, the authors find that the reform substantially increased fathers' take-up of parental leave and generated measurable attitudinal shifts: parents became more supportive of paternity leave policies, adopted more egalitarian views on gender roles, and reduced their perceived differences in parenting capabilities between mothers and fathers. On labor market outcomes, the intervention narrowed gender earnings gaps by 34 percentage points during the leave period and by 2.8 percentage points in the years following. The paper also documents that the earmarking requirement — which constrained household allocation of leave — reduced overall leave satisfaction, revealing a fundamental policy tradeoff inherent in directive family leave designs.

## 4. The Effects of GLP-1 Use on Mental Health, Self-rated Health, Employment and Marriage [Health]
**Authors:** Robert Kaestner and Cuiping Schiman
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35198
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether GLP-1 receptor agonist drugs — which have produced well-documented and substantial weight loss among users — translate into improvements in mental health, self-rated health, employment, and marital status. Leveraging the rapid recent expansion of GLP-1 adoption among diabetic individuals, the authors employ a longitudinal within-person regression approach to isolate the effects of GLP-1 use from underlying health trends and selection into treatment. Despite the large documented effects on body weight, the study finds that GLP-1 use is not meaningfully associated with improvements in mental health, self-rated health, employment outcomes, or marriage, providing important evidence on the boundaries of pharmacological obesity treatment effects on broader socioeconomic outcomes.

## 5. The Long-Run Effects of the Affordable Care Act: Evidence from a Partially Pre-Committed Research Design Over the COVID-19 Recession and Recovery [Health]
**Authors:** Jeffrey Clemens, Anwita Mahajan, and Joseph J. Sabia
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35190
**Abstract:** This paper extends prior short-run event study analyses of the Affordable Care Act (ACA) to assess long-run effects on insurance coverage and employment, with particular attention to the disruptions introduced by the COVID-19 recession and recovery period. Employing a partially pre-committed research design that limits specification search by extending previously published event study specifications, the authors test whether delayed economic adjustment amplifies the ACA's measured long-run impacts. Their findings indicate that long-run effects on employment and employer-sponsored coverage were modest and substantially smaller than initial projections, suggesting that the ACA's steady-state labor market consequences are limited even after accounting for the large macroeconomic shocks of the pandemic era.
