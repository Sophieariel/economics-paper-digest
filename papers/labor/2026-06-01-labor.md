# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-01

## 1. Does Field of Study Shape the Gender Wage Gap? The Role of Migration Background [Gender]
**Authors:** Louise Devos, François Rycx, Thomas Senterre, Mélanie Volral
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18640
**Abstract:** This paper uses matched employer-employee data spanning over 62,000 master's degree recipients to investigate how the gender wage gap varies across academic disciplines and by workers' migration background. Employing wage regressions and Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition methods, the authors find that women consistently earn less than men across all fields of study, with the gap particularly pronounced in high-return disciplines such as law, economics and management, and science. Approximately 30 percent of the raw gap is explained by observable sorting into fields and occupations, but substantial within-field penalties for women persist after conditioning on controls. First-generation immigrants from developing nations face the lowest wage returns regardless of field, experiencing wage disadvantages that compound and largely overshadow field-specific effects. Educational specialization accounts for a meaningful share of gender wage disparities among native-born workers and second-generation immigrants, but proves insufficient to explain the broader pay disadvantages facing first-generation immigrant women. The findings highlight how intersecting dimensions of gender and migration status shape occupational wage returns and point to structural barriers within fields rather than selection between them.

## 2. How do Workers Learn? Theory and Evidence on the Roots of Lifecycle Human Capital Accumulation [Education]
**Authors:** Xiao Ma, Alejandro Nakab, Daniela Vidart
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35199
**Abstract:** This paper examines how the sources of human capital accumulation shift across a worker's career and how those dynamics shape lifecycle earnings profiles. Drawing on administrative linked employer-employee data from Germany and the United States, the authors document that internal learning from coworkers declines with labor market experience while external learning through employer-provided training follows a distinct lifecycle pattern. To quantify these relationships, the paper develops and estimates a job search model incorporating multiple knowledge acquisition channels with experience-varying returns. The model reveals that interactions between internal and external learning sources critically shape lifetime earnings trajectories. A key policy-relevant finding concerns remote work: shifting to remote arrangements substantially hampers peer-based learning, particularly in early career stages when coworker spillovers are most productive. Formal training programs can partially compensate for this loss, but the net effect on human capital accumulation remains negative, raising concerns about the long-run wage consequences of hybrid and remote work arrangements for younger workers.

## 3. The Geography of Lifecycle Human Capital Accumulation [Education]
**Authors:** Ben Sprung-Keyser, Sonya Porter
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35203
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether the places that produce the strongest childhood human capital outcomes are also the places that generate the greatest adult human capital accumulation through labor market exposure. Using variation in adult labor market conditions across U.S. commuting zones, estimated from earnings trajectories of interstate movers, and age-at-move variation in childhood relocations to identify childhood environment effects — with Hurricane Katrina displacement as an additional validation exercise — the authors decompose local wage effects into three components: location-specific wage premia, adult human capital returns from local labor market experience, and childhood human capital development. They uncover a fundamental tradeoff: each one-rank improvement in adult labor market earnings from a location trades off with approximately a half-rank reduction in childhood human capital benefits. Larger cities systematically favor adult accumulation but offer weaker childhood environments, while smaller cities display the opposite profile. These divergent trajectories are correlated with differences in urban density, social networks, and the structure of peer interactions at different life stages, with implications for place-based education and workforce development policies.

## 4. Mortality Rates by Race and Ethnicity Among People with Disabilities [Health]
**Authors:** Madeline S. Helfer, Becky Staiger, Jessica Van Parys
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35193
**Abstract:** This paper examines racial and ethnic disparities in mid-life mortality among low-income adults with disabilities, using Medicaid claims data linked to Supplemental Security Income (SSI) records for over five million individuals from 2017 to 2021. Among this population of adults whose labor market participation is severely constrained by disability, the authors document substantial variation in age-adjusted mortality rates: American Indian and Alaska Native and White SSI recipients face the highest rates (2.9 and 2.6 percent respectively), while Asian recipients exhibit the lowest at 1.6 percent. The analysis decomposes racial disparities into contributions from differences in clinical diagnoses, chronic conditions, and substance use disorders. Controlling for diagnosed health conditions substantially attenuates White–Hispanic and White–Asian mortality differentials, suggesting that observed health differences mediate those gaps. By contrast, the White–Black mortality differential is not explained by clinical diagnoses, indicating that other mechanisms — including differential healthcare utilization, social determinants, or unmeasured health status — sustain this disparity. The findings have direct implications for Medicaid policy and for programs targeting labor market reintegration of individuals with disabilities.

## 5. The Effects of GLP-1 Use on Mental Health, Self-rated Health, Employment and Marriage [Health]
**Authors:** Robert Kaestner, Cuiping Schiman
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35198
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether GLP-1 receptor agonist drugs — which produce well-documented weight loss among users — generate downstream improvements in mental health, self-assessed health, employment, and marital status. Leveraging the rapid and recent diffusion of GLP-1 adoption among individuals with diabetes as a source of quasi-experimental variation, the authors employ a longitudinal within-person regression approach to isolate the causal effects of GLP-1 initiation from underlying health trends and selection into treatment. Despite large effects on body weight, the study finds that GLP-1 use is not meaningfully associated with improvements in any of the four labor market and wellbeing outcomes examined. The null results are robust across alternative specifications and subgroups and contribute important evidence on the boundaries of pharmacological obesity treatment: while these drugs alter body composition, they do not appear to translate into the broader socioeconomic gains that might be expected if weight loss improved labor productivity, mental health, or relationship stability. The findings suggest that policies premised on weight reduction alone may be insufficient to materially improve employment or family outcomes for diabetic individuals.
