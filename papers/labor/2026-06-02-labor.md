# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-02

## 1. The (Lack of) Efficacy of Social Belonging and Growth Mindset Interventions Among College Students [Education]
**Authors:** Abid N. Alam, Philip Oreopoulos, Uros Petronijevic
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35230
**Abstract:** Using four large-scale experiments across two major Canadian universities, we experimentally evaluate the effects of growth mindset and social belonging interventions on student outcomes. In a sample of nearly 12,000 students, we find no immediate or dynamic effects on student grades and no effect on persistence through university. We further combine survey and administrative data with machine learning methods to explore treatment effect heterogeneity, finding no evidence of meaningful variation in treatment effects across student subgroups. Despite the recent promise of these light-touch interventions, our findings indicate further research is required to identify the contexts in which their benefits generalize.

## 2. Are Gender Norms Shaped by Who Earns More? [Gender]
**Authors:** Hanna Brosch, Elisabeth Grewenig, Philipp Lergetporer, Katharina Werner, Helen Zeidler
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18661
**Abstract:** We investigate whether parental earnings shape gender attitudes toward childcare responsibilities through a large-scale randomized survey experiment involving over 10,000 participants in Germany. We present respondents with scenarios in which mothers and fathers have varying pre-childbirth earnings and ask who should stay home with a child. Without specifying earnings, 90% recommend that the mother stay home. This share remains high when we specify that the mother earns less (93%). When she earns more, the share drops sharply to 47%, yet nearly half of respondents still recommend that the mother stay home. We conclude that while financial circumstances affect gender attitudes, deeply rooted traditional norms persist even when economically disadvantageous, rejecting a purely income-based explanation for gendered parental labor allocation.

## 3. The Long-Run Effects of the Affordable Care Act: Evidence from a Partially Pre-Committed Research Design Over the COVID-19 Recession and Recovery [Health]
**Authors:** Jeffrey Clemens, Anwita Mahajan, Joseph J. Sabia
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35190
**Abstract:** Adjustment frictions can cause the long-run effects of social insurance reforms to differ from their short-run effects. Using pre-committed extensions of event study specifications applied previously for short-run analyses, we test the hypothesis that the Affordable Care Act's (ACA) impacts on insurance coverage and employment would increase following the substantial churn generated by the COVID-19 pandemic. Contrary to the hypothesis, the ACA's impacts remained stable through the pandemic. Long-run effects on employment and employer coverage were modest and much smaller than initially projected. Our study illustrates how partially pre-committed designs can yield informative long-run estimates while limiting specification search.

## 4. Science on the Move: How Experiential Pedagogy Shapes Human Capital [Education]
**Authors:** Nitin Bharti, Samreen Malik, Abhiroop Mukhopadhyay, Nishith Prakash
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18677
**Abstract:** We evaluate discovery-based science instruction in Indian public schools using a cluster-randomized controlled trial across 132 institutions in Uttar Pradesh, testing two program variations: a high-intensity Mobile Science Lab (MSL) and a lower-intensity Lab on Bike (LoB). MSL improves motivational beliefs and self-confidence by 0.15–0.18 standard deviations, reduces perceived barriers to education by 0.23 standard deviations, raises engagement by 0.17–0.22 standard deviations, and increases standardized test scores by 0.22–0.34 standard deviations across all subjects. LoB shows more modest results, with measurable gains primarily among participants who attended all sessions. Pedagogical methodology and program intensity substantially influence human capital development outcomes, and discovery-based pedagogy can shift motivational beliefs, engagement, and achievement even in low-capacity public school systems.

## 5. Son Preference, Intergenerational Household Dynamics, and Women's Mental Health in India [Health]
**Authors:** S Anukriti, Catalina Herrera-Almanza, Shahadat Hossain, Mahesh Karra
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18653
**Abstract:** We examine how son preference influences women's mental health across generations using survey data from extended households in rural India. Exploiting exogenous variation in the sex of the firstborn child, we find that mothers-in-law whose co-resident daughters-in-law had firstborn sons experienced a 15 percent lower risk of anxiety or depression. Daughters-in-law show no average mental health effects from firstborn child sex, though older women facing reproductive time constraints experience adverse outcomes without sons. Additionally, a firstborn son prompts mothers-in-law to reallocate time toward childcare and increases their support for daughters-in-law's employment during early childhood, enhancing younger women's labor force participation. These results illuminate pathways connecting son preference to women's psychological well-being, household dynamics, and economic participation beyond fertility decisions alone.
