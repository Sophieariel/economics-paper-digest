# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-03

## 1. Science on the Move: How Experiential Pedagogy Shapes Human Capital [Education]
**Authors:** Nitin Bharti, Samreen Malik, Abhiroop Mukhopadhyay, Nishith Prakash
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18677
**Abstract:** This paper conducts a cluster-randomized controlled trial across 132 public schools in Uttar Pradesh, India, to evaluate discovery-based science instruction at two implementation intensities. The high-intensity Mobile Science Lab intervention — a fully equipped mobile laboratory staffed by trained science educators — improves students' motivational beliefs and self-confidence by 0.15–0.18 standard deviations and increases standardized test scores by 0.22–0.34 standard deviations across all subjects. The lower-intensity Lab on Bike intervention produces more modest improvements, with measurable benefits concentrated among students who participated in all sessions. The findings highlight that pedagogical design and delivery intensity are critical determinants of multidimensional human capital formation in low-capacity public school settings, with implications for the design of scalable educational interventions in low- and middle-income countries.

## 2. The Gender Gap in Digital Skills at Work [Gender]
**Authors:** Adele Whelan, Luke Brosnan, Seamus McGuinness
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18675
**Abstract:** This paper examines gender disparities in digital skill application across European workplaces using representative employer and worker survey data. The authors document a baseline gap of approximately 16 percentage points in the likelihood of performing sophisticated digital tasks, of which only 30 percent is attributable to observable characteristics such as education, occupation, and industry. Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition reveals that gendered educational and occupational sorting contributes substantially to unequal access to highly digital roles, while unexplained gaps persist at intermediate levels of digital complexity, pointing to structural, cultural, or organizational barriers. Notably, younger women currently experience larger advanced digital skill gaps than older cohorts, indicating an emerging rather than receding challenge. The results suggest that addressing gender gaps in digital work requires interventions beyond credential parity, targeting the organizational conditions that channel women away from high-complexity digital roles.

## 3. Are Gender Norms Shaped by Who Earns More? [Gender]
**Authors:** Hanna Brosch, Elisabeth Grewenig, Philipp Lergetporer, Katharina Werner, Helen Zeidler
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18661
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether attitudes about parental labor allocation respond to economic incentives by conducting a large-scale survey experiment with over 10,000 German respondents. Participants were randomly assigned vignettes varying which parent earned more prior to having children, and then asked which parent should reduce employment upon childbirth. Without specifying earnings, 90 percent of respondents recommend the mother stay home. When the mother earned less, support for maternal caregiving remained at 93 percent. However, when the mother earned more, support fell to 47 percent — yet approximately half of respondents still recommended the mother remain home despite this reversal of financial incentives. The authors conclude that while economic circumstances do shift gender norm endorsement at the margin, deeply embedded prescriptive gender norms persist even in the face of competing financial considerations, with implications for policy evaluations of parental leave design and incentive-based family policies.

## 4. Parenthood and Productivity [Fertility]
**Authors:** Diogo Britto, Caio De Holanda, Bruno Ferman, Alexandre Fonseca, Breno Sampaio, Lucas Warwar
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18658
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether parenthood reduces on-the-job productivity by studying Brazilian judges, a profession that enables precise measurement of work output through court rulings and case-processing records. Using event-study methods around childbirth and leveraging the measurability of judicial output, the authors find no evidence that mothers' or fathers' productivity declines during pregnancy or following return from parental leave across multiple dimensions of judicial quality and quantity. Comparable null results appear among self-employed labor attorneys. The findings suggest that observed employment and earnings penalties for mothers do not stem from diminished workplace performance, but rather from other channels such as employer discrimination, reduced hours, or changes in job assignment. These results have direct implications for the interpretation of child penalties in the labor market and for the design of family policies aimed at closing gender gaps in career outcomes.

## 5. The Labor Market and Health Impacts of Reducing Cesarean Section Deliveries [Health]
**Authors:** Sarah Miller, Petra Persson, Maya Rossin-Slater, Laura R. Wherry
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18629
**Abstract:** This paper evaluates a California hospital initiative designed to reduce rates of primary cesarean delivery among low-risk first-time mothers, using linked birth records and earnings data in a quasi-experimental framework. Mothers affected by the initiative were 8 percent less likely to deliver by cesarean section, with no detectable adverse health effects on either mother or infant. Turning to labor market outcomes, exposed mothers show higher likelihoods of returning to their original employer and stronger within-firm earnings rankings in the immediate postpartum period, though these relative advantages attenuate over subsequent years. Among mothers who give birth a second time, the intervention also reduced rates of both repeat cesarean and preterm delivery. The findings provide evidence that reducing medically unnecessary surgical deliveries can have positive spillovers on maternal labor market reattachment while improving downstream obstetric outcomes, with implications for both health policy and the economics of childbirth and women's careers.
