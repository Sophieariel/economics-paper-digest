# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-05

## 1. Where There's Smoke: Stochastic Caregiving Shocks and Mothers' Labor Market Outcomes [Gender]
**Authors:** Joshua S. Graff Zivin, Seunghoon Lee
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35264
**Abstract:** The researchers investigate how unexpected caregiving demands affect mothers' employment, using wildfire smoke exposure as a natural experiment. They find that smoke triggers school closures and increased student absenteeism, creating sudden childcare needs. Their analysis reveals these intermittent shocks substantially reduce employment, particularly among mothers working in male-dominated fields. Notably, "employment losses are fully offset among mothers working outside male-dominated industries," suggesting that workplace flexibility matters significantly in moderating the impact of unpredictable caregiving requirements. The findings help explain why formal childcare expansions often produce limited effects on maternal labor force participation.

## 2. Persistent Effects of Early Academic Rank on Cognitive and Noncognitive Outcomes [Education]
**Authors:** Eunsik Chang, María Padilla-Romo, Cecilia Peluffo
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35267
**Abstract:** This study examines how a student's early academic standing affects later educational outcomes. Using Mexican school records, researchers compared students with comparable third-grade achievement but different class rankings. They found that a higher third-grade rank increases performance on a high-stakes high school admission exam, with effects from both classroom and broader cohort rank. Students with higher elementary ranks selected more competitive high schools and demonstrated improved self-perception, academic aspirations, classroom responsibility, learning strategies, and teamwork attitudes by ninth grade. The research also documents positive effects on high school placement outcomes.

## 3. Frontier Knowledge in College and Student Success [Education]
**Authors:** Barbara Biasi, Song Ma
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35269
**Abstract:** This research examines how exposure to cutting-edge research affects student outcomes. Using syllabi from seven Texas public universities alongside publication data and student records, the authors measured each course's connection to recent versus established research. Their findings show that courses emphasizing frontier knowledge lead to improved completion rates, higher GPAs, increased graduate school enrollment, and better earnings, while also reducing time spent earning degrees. Benefits in completion and academic performance are widespread across student groups, though graduate school and earnings advantages are concentrated among students with stronger academic backgrounds and greater family resources. The researchers identify two key drivers: frontier-focused content enhances student motivation, and consistent exposure develops marketable professional abilities.

## 4. How Efficient was the Affordable Care Act at Reducing Uninsured Rates? [Health]
**Authors:** Anuj Gangopadhyaya, Robert Kaestner
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35263
**Abstract:** This study measures ACA efficiency by examining how gains in publicly supported health insurance affected uninsured rates. Using American Community Survey data from 2008–2024, the researchers analyzed trends across income groups defined by poverty level ratios. They found that for adults below 150% of the federal poverty line, increases in public insurance coverage were associated with one-for-one decrease in uninsured and no change in employer-sponsored insurance. For those earning 151–400% of poverty level, each percentage point increase in public coverage correlated with approximately 0.6 percentage point reductions in uninsured rates and 0.4 percentage point decreases in employer-sponsored insurance. The analysis suggests that expanding public insurance for low-income groups proves more efficient than subsidizing coverage for higher-income populations.

## 5. College Affirmative Action and Students' Outcomes: A Matter of the Dose [Education]
**Authors:** Yi Chen, Chao Fu, Hongbin Li, Teng Li, Siyan Tang
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35281
**Abstract:** The research examines how varying levels of affirmative action policy affect beneficiary outcomes by analyzing China's ethnic minority bonus-point system in college admissions. Using exogenous variation in policy intensity, the authors find that higher affirmative action doses increase recipients' admissions to colleges and elite colleges, but lower their academic rankings and reduce major earnings potential. Crucially, the study reveals non-monotonic effects: modest affirmative action boosts long-term consumption by 19%, while substantial doses reduce it by 25%. These patterns persist across expenditure types, including child-related spending. Students from stronger academic backgrounds demonstrate greater resilience to negative effects. The findings suggest that careful calibration of affirmative action policies is crucial for achieving intended objectives, indicating that policy effectiveness depends significantly on dosage levels.
