# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-07

## 1. The Labor Market and Health Impacts of Reducing Cesarean Section Deliveries [Health]
**Authors:** Sarah Miller, Petra Persson, Maya Rossin-Slater, Laura R. Wherry
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18629
**Abstract:** This paper examines an intervention that reduced cesarean section deliveries among low-risk first-time mothers in California, using linked birth and earnings administrative records. Mothers exposed to the intervention were 8% less likely to have a c-section, with no adverse health effects detected. The authors find suggestive evidence that exposed mothers more readily returned to their previous employers and achieved higher earnings rankings post-birth, though these advantages diminished over time. Notably, mothers with subsequent pregnancies experienced reduced rates of both c-sections and preterm deliveries, indicating that estimates of the intervention's total benefits may be conservative. The results speak to how obstetric practice patterns can have downstream consequences for maternal labor market reintegration and long-run career trajectories.

## 2. Supporting Mothers Back to Work: Experimental Evidence on Employment, Fertility, and Child Outcomes [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniela Del Boca, Luca Favero, Chiara D. Pronzato
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18630
**Abstract:** This paper evaluates a targeted intervention designed to support mothers who had reduced labor market participation due to childcare constraints. Using a randomized experimental design, the authors show that improving work–family reconciliation can support mothers' labor market reintegration, promote investments in existing children, and, under conditions of greater economic stability, strengthen fertility desires. The study finds evidence of positive spillovers across multiple domains: labor supply, child human capital, and stated intentions regarding future childbearing. The findings underscore the potential of family-friendly workplace policies to simultaneously address low fertility rates and women's underemployment, with implications for policymakers designing parental support schemes.

## 3. Does Field of Study Shape the Gender Wage Gap? The Role of Migration Background [Gender]
**Authors:** Louise Devos, François Rycx, Thomas Senterre, Mélanie Volral
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18640
**Abstract:** This paper analyzes matched employer-employee data for more than 62,000 master's degree graduates to investigate wage disparities between men and women across fields of study, with an additional focus on the role of migration background. Key findings indicate that returns to education vary significantly by discipline, with law, economics, management, and the natural sciences offering the highest wage premia. Women consistently earn less than men within every field examined, and this penalty is largest in the highest-paying disciplines. First-generation immigrants from developing countries experience the lowest returns regardless of field, while second-generation immigrants approach but do not fully close the gap with native workers. Educational specialization accounts for a substantial share of gender wage inequality among natives and second-generation immigrants, though first-generation immigrants face compounding wage disadvantages beyond field selection alone.

## 4. Science on the Move: How Experiential Pedagogy Shapes Human Capital [Education]
**Authors:** Nitin Bharti, Samreen Malik, Abhiroop Mukhopadhyay, Nishith Prakash
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18677
**Abstract:** This paper presents evidence from a cluster-randomized controlled trial conducted across 132 public schools in Uttar Pradesh, India. The trial evaluates two variants of guided, discovery-based science pedagogy: a high-intensity Mobile Science Lab intervention and a lower-intensity Lab on Bike program. The high-intensity treatment improved students' motivational beliefs and self-confidence by 0.15–0.18 standard deviations, reduced perceived educational barriers by 0.23 standard deviations, and raised science test scores by 0.22–0.34 standard deviations. The lower-intensity intervention produced more modest gains, concentrated among students with full participation. The results demonstrate that experiential pedagogy can meaningfully raise human capital formation in under-resourced public schools, and that program intensity is a key mediator of effectiveness.

## 5. Policy or Pandemic? Explaining the Rise in Young Adults' Private Health Insurance Take-Up in Australia [Health]
**Authors:** Nathan Kettlewell, Tiannan Gao
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18708
**Abstract:** This paper investigates the effect of Australia's 2019 age-based discount policy on private health insurance (PHI) enrollment among young adults. The policy offered perpetual premium reductions of 2–10% for individuals who purchased coverage before age 30. Exploiting administrative enrollment data and a synthetic difference-in-differences design, the authors find that the discount policy produced modest positive effects on PHI take-up prior to the pandemic during an otherwise declining membership period. However, enrollment increased substantially in the treated cohort during the COVID-19 period, when aggregate demand for health insurance rose sharply. The central finding is that the relative increase in demand among young adults is predominantly driven by pandemic-era factors rather than the policy incentive itself. The paper contributes to the literature on behavioral responses to health insurance subsidies and the role of health shocks in shaping insurance markets.
