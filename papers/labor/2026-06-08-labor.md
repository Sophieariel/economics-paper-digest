# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-08

## 1. Dissecting the Gender Gap in Intergenerational Transfers: The Case of Germany [Gender]
**Authors:** Karan Singhal, Markus M. Grabka, Eva Sierminska
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18706
**Abstract:** This study examines gender disparities in wealth transfers across Germany over four decades, analyzing both inheritances and inter vivos gifts using Socio-Economic Panel data. The research reveals persistent gaps: women are in some cases more likely to report receiving an inheritance, while men are more likely to receive gifts and obtain larger overall transfer amounts. Key findings include regional variation, with younger West German women facing significant disadvantages in gift receipt, while East Germany shows no systematic gaps. The analysis demonstrates that transfers—particularly gifts—meaningfully contribute to overall wealth inequality between genders. Using decomposition methods, the authors conclude that despite gender-neutral inheritance law, testamentary freedom and persistent social norms continue to generate unequal outcomes.

## 2. Caring Dads? Universal Childcare, Paternity Leave, and Fathers' Labor Supply [Fertility]
**Authors:** Mathias Huebener, Malin K. Mahlbacher, Sophia Schmitz
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18696
**Abstract:** The researchers examined how expansions of publicly subsidized childcare influence labor allocation between household members during early childhood, emphasizing paternal responses. Using data on childcare expansion across German counties, they found that increased childcare availability accelerates childcare entry and maternal return to work. Notably, fathers also respond by taking more parental leave and reducing full-time employment, though weekday caregiving increases were minimal. The study concludes that childcare policy reforms can modify household labor dynamics, producing smaller overall labor supply gains than maternal employment alone would suggest.

## 3. Policy or Pandemic? Explaining the Rise in Young Adults' Private Health Insurance Take-Up in Australia [Health]
**Authors:** Nathan Kettlewell, Tiannan Gao
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18708
**Abstract:** The research examines an Australian age-based discount policy from 2019 offering a perpetual premium discount of 2%–10% for people who purchased insurance before age 30. Using administrative data and synthetic difference-in-differences analysis, the authors found the policy produced only modest positive effects on insurance adoption during the pre-pandemic period of declining membership. However, uptake among the treatment group increased substantially during the COVID-19 pandemic when overall private health insurance demand rose. The study concludes the expansion in young adult enrollment was predominantly driven by factors other than the discount policy, highlighting the limits of price incentives in reshaping insurance take-up behavior.

## 4. Indoor Environment, Student Satisfaction and Performance in Higher Education [Education]
**Authors:** Stefan Flagner, Piet Eichholtz, Nils Kok, Rick Kramer, Steffen Künn, Wouter van Marken-Lichtenbelt, Guy Plasqui, Xudong Sun
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18697
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a randomized field experiment with 1,258 first-year undergraduates at Maastricht University. Students were assigned to tutorial groups in either a recently renovated building with enhanced indoor environmental conditions or a conventional control building. Key findings indicate that students in the treatment building reported significantly higher satisfaction with indoor environmental quality and the overall learning environment. However, the improvements in environmental conditions did not produce measurable impacts on academic performance, study effort, or evaluations from tutors. The results suggest that school building quality affects student well-being and perceived learning environment but may not translate directly into measurable achievement gains.

## 5. Understanding Occupational Wage Growth [Education]
**Authors:** Adrian Adermon, Simon Ek, Georg Graetz, Yaroslav Yakymovych
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18710
**Abstract:** The researchers analyzed Swedish worker data spanning 1996–2013 to examine growth in occupational wage premiums and life-cycle earnings profiles. They found a substantial increase in between-occupation wage inequality due to differential growth in premia, though workforce composition shifts partially offset this trend. The study indicates demand-side factors predominantly drove premium growth, with wage growth from occupation-specific skills being more dispersed early in the period. Results suggest Swedish labor institutions have accommodated technological and demand shifts through wage and employment adjustments, providing new evidence on how human capital investment in occupation-specific skills shapes long-run wage inequality.
