# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-10

## 1. Caring Dads? Universal Childcare, Paternity Leave, and Fathers' Labor Supply [Fertility]
**Authors:** Mathias Huebener, Malin K. Mahlbacher, Sophia Schmitz
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18696/caring-dads-universal-childcare-paternity-leave-and-fathers-labor-supply
**Abstract:** This paper examines how expansions of publicly subsidized childcare affect the intra-household allocation of labor supply during early childhood, with a particular focus on fathers. Using a difference-in-differences design that exploits variation in the rollout of childcare places for children under age three across German counties, the authors find that increased childcare availability accelerates maternal employment returns after childbirth. However, fathers respond by taking additional parental leave and reducing full-time work, while weekday childcare participation by fathers does not rise substantially. The findings suggest that childcare policies reshape labor supply within households in ways that partially offset one another, leading to smaller aggregate labor supply effects than would be inferred from maternal responses alone. The results highlight the importance of accounting for paternal labor supply responses when evaluating family policy, and have implications for the design of universal childcare schemes aiming to close gender employment gaps.

## 2. Indoor Environment, Student Satisfaction and Performance in Higher Education [Education]
**Authors:** Stefan Flagner, Piet Eichholtz, Nils Kok, Rick Kramer, Steffen Künn, Wouter van Marken-Lichtenbelt, Guy Plasqui, Xudong Sun
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18697
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how improvements in indoor environmental quality affect student satisfaction and academic performance in higher education. The authors implement a randomized field experiment with 1,258 first-year undergraduates at Maastricht University, randomly assigning tutorial groups to either a newly renovated building featuring enhanced air quality, lighting, and thermal comfort, or to a conventional control building. Students assigned to the upgraded facility report significantly higher satisfaction with the learning environment and with indoor environmental conditions. However, the randomized improvements do not produce measurable impacts on actual academic performance, study effort, or instructor evaluations. The null results on performance suggest that environmental amenities in higher education influence student experience and well-being without translating into achievement gains, raising questions about the returns to building quality investments as a policy lever for human capital formation.

## 3. De-biasing or Backlash? Confronting Prejudice Among Police Officers in India [Gender]
**Authors:** Sofia Amaral, Kim Chaney, Victoria Kaiser, Nishith Prakash, Abhilasha Sahay
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18699
**Abstract:** This paper examines how police officers respond when confronted with evidence of their own biased handling of gender-based violence cases. The authors conduct a lab-in-the-field experiment with 323 officers in Madhya Pradesh, India, presenting participants with a fictitious gender-based violence scenario and measuring subsequent changes in the prioritization of victim statements and stereotype assignment. The intervention produces no average treatment effect, but masks sharp heterogeneity by officer gender. Female officers improve their prioritization of victim statements by 23 percentage points — a 27 percent increase over baseline — one week after the confrontation. Male officers, by contrast, exhibit significant backlash: they deprioritize victim statements and assign more negative stereotypes to gender-based violence victims following the intervention. The findings reveal that de-biasing programs targeting institutional gender discrimination can generate divergent responses depending on the baseline level of bias and the gender of the recipient, with important implications for organizational diversity initiatives and the design of anti-discrimination training.

## 4. Dissecting the Gender Gap in Intergenerational Transfers: The Case of Germany [Gender]
**Authors:** Karan Singhal, Markus M. Grabka, Eva Sierminska
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18706
**Abstract:** This paper examines gender disparities in intergenerational wealth transfers in Germany over more than four decades, focusing separately on inheritances and inter vivos gifts. Drawing on longitudinal data from the Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP) and applying Oaxaca–Blinder and Recentered Influence Function (RIF) decompositions across the wealth distribution, the authors document that women more frequently report receiving inheritances, yet men receive larger gift amounts and larger overall transfer sums. Pronounced regional variation emerges: younger West German women face significant disadvantages in gifts received, while no systematic gender gaps appear in East Germany. Decomposition results show that gifts — rather than inheritances — are the primary driver of gender wealth disparities, and that standard characteristics cannot fully explain the gaps. Despite gender-neutral inheritance legislation, the authors find that testamentary freedom and persistent social norms continue to generate unequal outcomes, contributing meaningfully to broader gender wealth inequality in Germany.

## 5. Policy or Pandemic? Explaining the Rise in Young Adults' Private Health Insurance Take-Up in Australia [Health]
**Authors:** Nathan Kettlewell, Tiannan Gao
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18708
**Abstract:** This paper evaluates the effectiveness of a 2019 Australian age-based discount policy designed to increase private health insurance take-up among young adults by offering a perpetual premium discount of 2–10 percent for individuals who purchased coverage before age 30. Using a synthetic difference-in-differences approach that compares treated cohorts against a synthetic control group before and after policy implementation, the authors find that the discount policy produced minimal effects prior to the COVID-19 pandemic — a period characterized by declining overall enrollment. During the pandemic, treatment effects grew larger, coinciding with a broad surge in demand for private health insurance. Decomposition of the pandemic-era increase reveals that the relative rise in take-up among young adults is predominantly driven by factors other than the discount incentive. The findings suggest that modest financial incentives alone are insufficient to sustain voluntary insurance markets among the young, and that demand-side responses to health insurance policy are highly sensitive to ambient health risk perceptions.
