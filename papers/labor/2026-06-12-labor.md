# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-12

## 1. Dissecting the Gender Gap in Intergenerational Transfers: The Case of Germany [Gender]
**Authors:** Karan Singhal, Markus M. Grabka, Eva Sierminska
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18706
**Abstract:** This paper examines gender disparities in intergenerational wealth transfers in Germany using four decades of panel data. The analysis reveals persistent and asymmetric inequalities: women are in some cases more likely to report receiving an inheritance, yet men are more likely to receive gifts and obtain larger overall transfer amounts. Geographic heterogeneity is pronounced — younger West German women experience larger disadvantages in gift transfers, while East Germany shows no systematic gaps. The study demonstrates that gifts, more than inheritances, meaningfully contribute to gender wealth disparities. Using statistical decomposition methods, the authors find that gifts account for a measurable share of the mean gender wealth gap as of 2019. They conclude that despite gender-neutral inheritance laws, testamentary freedom and persistent social norms continue to generate unequal outcomes, and that failing to address these disparities allows wealth transfers to perpetuate economic inequality across generations and genders.

## 2. Caring Dads? Universal Childcare, Paternity Leave, and Fathers' Labor Supply [Fertility]
**Authors:** Mathias Huebener, Malin K. Mahlbacher, Sophia Schmitz
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18696
**Abstract:** This paper examines how expansions of publicly subsidized childcare for children under age three reshape the intra-household allocation of labor, with a particular focus on fathers' responses. Leveraging the staggered rollout of childcare facilities across German counties as a quasi-experimental source of variation, the authors find that greater childcare availability accelerates childcare entry and maternal return to work. Fathers, however, respond by increasing paternal leave uptake and reducing full-time employment, without proportionally increasing their weekday childcare activities. These paternal adjustments partially offset the aggregate labor supply gains arising from maternal re-entry, implying that childcare expansion policies generate smaller economy-wide labor supply effects than maternal responses alone would suggest. The findings contribute to the literature on the gendered effects of childcare policy and underscore the importance of modeling household bargaining when assessing family policy impacts.

## 3. Indoor Environment, Student Satisfaction and Performance in Higher Education [Education]
**Authors:** Stefan Flagner, Piet Eichholtz, Nils Kok, Rick Kramer, Steffen Künn, Wouter van Marken-Lichtenbelt, Guy Plasqui, Xudong Sun
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18697
**Abstract:** This paper uses a large-scale randomized experiment at Maastricht University to test whether improvements to the indoor physical environment enhance student outcomes. A total of 1,258 first-year students were randomly assigned to tutorial groups meeting in either a newly renovated building with improved lighting, air quality, and thermal comfort, or a standard comparison building. Students in the treatment building reported significantly higher satisfaction with indoor environmental quality and the overall learning environment. Despite these satisfaction gains, the experiment reveals no measurable improvements in academic performance, study effort, or instructor evaluations across multiple outcome measures. The null findings on performance, in contrast to the positive satisfaction effects, suggest that while physical learning environments shape subjective well-being, they may not be a binding constraint on educational attainment in well-functioning higher education systems. The paper has direct implications for the cost-effectiveness of capital investment in university facilities as an education quality policy.

## 4. Policy or Pandemic? Explaining the Rise in Young Adults' Private Health Insurance Take-Up in Australia [Health]
**Authors:** Nathan Kettlewell, Tiannan Gao
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18708
**Abstract:** This paper evaluates the effect of Australia's Lifetime Health Cover loading policy, which since 2019 grants a perpetual premium discount of 2%–10% to individuals who purchase private health insurance before age 30. Using synthetic difference-in-differences with administrative enrollment data, the authors identify modest positive enrollment effects in the pre-pandemic period, a time of generally declining private insurance membership. Enrollment among the targeted young adult cohort rose substantially during COVID-19, creating apparent evidence of a large policy effect. Careful identification, however, reveals that the relative surge in young adult demand is predominantly driven by pandemic-specific factors — heightened health salience, reduced elective surgery wait times, and income support programs — rather than the age-based discount itself. The paper illustrates how demand shocks correlated with policy rollout can confound impact estimates and contributes to the health economics literature on price incentives, take-up behavior, and the employment-linked determinants of health insurance coverage.

## 5. Characterizing the File Drawer: Evidence from a Meta-Analysis of Parent-Interventions Around the World [Education]
**Authors:** Peter Bergman, Nat Chowanajin
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18701
**Abstract:** This paper addresses publication bias in the literature on low-cost parental engagement interventions by combining 82 randomized controlled trials conducted across 20+ countries with unpublished studies retrieved from pre-registration registries. The authors estimate a hierarchical Bayesian model that jointly characterizes the distribution of true effects and the selective publication process, yielding bias-corrected average effect sizes of 0.05 SD for test scores, 0.07 SD for grades, 0.05 SD for attendance, and 0.03 SD for enrollment. Crucially, the analysis finds that statistically insignificant results in the test score domain are written up at high rates relative to other outcomes, suggesting that the file drawer problem is less severe than commonly assumed in this literature. Corrected effect sizes remain positive and economically meaningful, implying that low-cost parent-communication programs delivered via text message, phone, or app represent strong value for public investment. The authors recommend future research focus on identifying the sources of heterogeneity across settings rather than replicating the basic design in new contexts.
