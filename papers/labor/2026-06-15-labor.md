# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-15

## 1. Gender Convergence in Couples' Time Use Following the COVID-19 Pandemic [Gender]
**Authors:** Ariel J. Binder
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18657
**Abstract:** This paper uses American Time Use Survey data to show that prime-age men's and women's average weekly work hours followed parallel trends from 2011-19, but then abruptly converged in the years following the COVID-19 pandemic. This convergence was driven by the changing behavior of couples, for whom the gender gap in weekly hours of paid work closed by 4.3 on a base of 14.7 (29.3%). While historical gender convergence has been driven by wives, husbands accounted for three-quarters (all) of the recent convergence in paid work (unpaid housework). The paper finds that two labor market factors associated with the pandemic — sectoral reallocation and remote work-exposure — explain little of observed time-use changes in samples of husbands and fathers, although they explain 44% of the shrinking college-noncollege gap in paid work observed among fathers. These results suggest an ongoing shift in labor supply factors associated with fatherhood that may be stronger among the college-educated.

## 2. Persistent Effects of Early Academic Rank on Cognitive and Noncognitive Outcomes [Education]
**Authors:** Eunsik Chang, María Padilla-Romo, Cecilia Peluffo
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18683
**Abstract:** This paper examines how a student's academic ranking in elementary school influences later educational and personal development outcomes, using Mexican administrative data. The researchers compare third-grade students with comparable achievement levels but different ordinal positions within their cohorts and classrooms, finding that higher early academic rank correlates with improved performance on high school entrance examinations. Both school-cohort rank and classroom-specific rank independently contribute to achievement gains. Additionally, students with higher early ranks demonstrate improved self-perception, stronger academic aspirations, greater classroom responsibility, better learning approaches, and enhanced teamwork skills by ninth grade. The findings also indicate that early rank advantages extend to favorable high school placement outcomes, suggesting that ordinal feedback effects in elementary school generate persistent human capital gaps independent of underlying ability.

## 3. Grade Inflation and the Interpretation of Labor Market Signals [Education]
**Authors:** Zhizhong Pu, Martin Abel, Jeffrey P. Carpenter
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18654
**Abstract:** This experimental study examines how grading policies influence employer interpretation of academic credentials as labor market signals. Hiring managers reviewed letter grades from math tests and set wages based on their perceptions of candidate ability. When grading schemes became coarser (fewer grade categories), managers relied less on grades and more on pre-existing demographic assumptions, reducing hiring efficiency. Managers paradoxically extracted stronger signals from inflated grades, treating candidates who received As as a positively selected group; inflated As received disproportionately higher weight than compressed Bs, providing a wage premium despite widespread knowledge of grade inflation. The shift toward assumption-based decisions under coarse grading disproportionately affected female candidates, widening the gender wage gap when managers held gendered assumptions. The findings link education credential design to labor market signal quality and discriminatory wage-setting.

## 4. Demographic Divergence: The Legacy of the Opioid Epidemic [Health]
**Authors:** Carolina Arteaga, Victoria Barone, Stephen Claassen
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35340
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how the opioid epidemic influenced local population dynamics and labor market composition across the United States. Using variation in exposure derived from Purdue Pharma's targeted OxyContin marketing to high-cancer-mortality areas as an instrumental variable, the authors find that more exposed commuting zones experienced reduced population growth. By 2020, a one-standard-deviation increase in exposure lowered population growth among ages 18–64 by 2.4 percentage points. Drug-induced mortality contributed minimally to these changes. Instead, population losses stemmed primarily from migratory responses, with exposure increasing out-migration — particularly among college-educated individuals. The findings suggest the opioid epidemic functioned as a disamenity shock reducing local quality of life, and that the working-age population responded primarily through relocation rather than mortality. Rising fertility rates by 2020 partially offset these population losses, contributing to long-term divergence in demographic composition and labor force quality across U.S. commuting zones.

## 5. Risk Perceptions and Protective Behaviors at the Onset and Outset of the COVID-19 Pandemic [Health]
**Authors:** Joan Costa-Font, Melcior Rosselló Roig, Caroline Rudisill, Luca Salmasi
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18704
**Abstract:** This paper examines how individuals formed risk beliefs about three health threats — COVID-19, influenza, and food poisoning — during the pandemic's early stages, using U.S. survey data. The researchers find that perceived danger levels for COVID-19 were comparable to those for influenza and were not significantly shaped by proximity to infected individuals; instead, demographic characteristics including gender, education, and employment status determined perceived risk. Using instrumental variable techniques to establish causal identification, the authors find that risk beliefs had limited causal influence on most protective behaviors. One meaningful causal pathway did emerge: higher risk perceptions marginally increased the likelihood of seeking remote medical consultations by approximately one percentage point. The paper contributes to the health economics literature on how risk information translates — or fails to translate — into protective action, with implications for public health communication and health service utilization during labor-market-disrupting crises.
