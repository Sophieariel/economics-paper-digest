# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-16

## 1. The Female Sensitivity Hypothesis: Evidence From Experimental Economics [Gender]
**Authors:** Felipe Araujo, Neeraja Gupta, Lise Vesterlund
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35324
**Abstract:** We evaluate the empirical support for the female sensitivity hypothesis—the claim that women respond more strongly than men to changes in treatment. Analyzing over two hundred experimental economics papers and conducting additional pairwise tests using existing datasets, we find that gender is not predictive of responsiveness to treatment. We also document that the literature citing the female sensitivity hypothesis tends to selectively draw on papers that support it, contributing to the persistence of this view despite the lack of systematic evidence.

## 2. A Cohort Perspective on Latin America's Fertility Transition [Fertility]
**Authors:** Regina Calles, Tom Vogl
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35326
**Abstract:** We study Latin America's fertility decline using census microdata from 17 countries, tracking female birth cohorts from the 1920s through the 1970s across subnational regions. Children ever born fell one-for-one with mortality decline across cohorts. After adjusting for other covariates, women's education and sectoral shifts emerge as the leading drivers of fertility reduction. Despite this decline, we find that fertility transition was not systematically linked with improvements in children's outcomes, challenging canonical theories of the demographic transition that predict a quantity-quality tradeoff.

## 3. Playing it Safe? The Effect of Abortion Bans on Sexual Behavior [Fertility]
**Authors:** James M. Flynn, David R. Munro, Caitlin K. Myers
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35333
**Abstract:** We examine how total abortion bans enacted following Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization affected sexual behavior. Using a difference-in-differences design, we track gonorrhea rates and condom purchases as proxies for sexual activity and contraceptive use. Total abortion bans reduced gonorrhea rates by 21% among the population aged 15–44 and increased condom purchases by 5.4%. County-level analysis indicates that these behavioral shifts stem from awareness of state abortion policy rather than from geographic barriers to abortion access. Our findings suggest that abortion restrictions increase the perceived cost of unprotected sex, inducing more cautious sexual behavior.

## 4. Demographic Divergence: The Legacy of the Opioid Epidemic [Health]
**Authors:** Carolina Arteaga, Victoria Barone, Stephen Claassen
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35340
**Abstract:** We study how the opioid epidemic shaped local population dynamics in the United States. Using Purdue Pharma's targeted OxyContin marketing to high-cancer-mortality areas as an instrument for opioid exposure, we find that heavily affected commuting zones experienced reduced population growth among working-age adults by 2020. Drug-related deaths account for only a small share of these declines; instead, the epidemic functioned primarily as a quality-of-life shock that induced selective out-migration, particularly of college-educated individuals. We also document increased fertility rates in affected areas that partially offset population losses, with the combined effects producing lasting divergence in local demographic composition.

## 5. Sent Away: Displacement, Neighborhoods, and Children's Outcomes under Slum Clearance Policies [Education]
**Authors:** Fernanda Rojas-Ampuero, Felipe Carrera
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35318
**Abstract:** We study the long-run human capital and labor market effects of urban displacement by comparing two policy arms of Chile's 1979–1984 slum clearance program: relocation to peripheral public housing projects versus on-site redevelopment. Two-thirds of families were relocated while one-third received improvements in place. Four decades later, displaced children completed 0.62 fewer years of schooling and earn 10.2% less, with higher rates of labor market informality. Mechanisms analysis points to relocation to lower-opportunity neighborhoods and the disruption of social networks as the primary channels. The findings provide quasi-experimental evidence that forced displacement imposes large and persistent costs on children's human capital formation.
