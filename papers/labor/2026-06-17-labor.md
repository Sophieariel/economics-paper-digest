# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-17

## 1. Demographic Divergence: The Legacy of the Opioid Epidemic [Health]
**Authors:** Carolina Arteaga, Victoria Barone, Stephen Claassen
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35340
**Abstract:** The researchers investigate how the opioid epidemic influenced population dynamics across U.S. regions. Using variation in Purdue Pharma's targeted marketing of OxyContin to high-cancer-mortality areas as an instrumental variable, they find that more exposed commuting zones saw reduced population growth among working-age adults by 2020—a 2.4 percentage point reduction per standard-deviation increase in exposure. While drug-induced deaths played a limited role, out-migration—particularly among college-educated residents—drove population losses. Rising fertility rates partially offset these declines, indicating the epidemic functioned as a quality-of-life disamenity that reshaped local demographic composition and accelerated divergence in population trajectories nationwide. The paper appears in the NBER Labor Studies, Economics of Health, and Regional and Urban Economics programs.

## 2. Blindly Discriminating: The GI Bill and Racial Inequality [Education]
**Authors:** Lukas Althoff, Christiane Szerman
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35339
**Abstract:** This study examines how the WWII GI Bill, despite being formally race-neutral, produced vastly different outcomes for Black and white veterans due to decentralized implementation through segregated institutions. Using regression discontinuity methods and linked census data spanning 1940–1950 to 1990–2025, the authors find that the GI Bill widened racial inequality: it doubled white veterans' college completion rates while steering Black veterans into often-fraudulent vocational programs with no earnings returns. These disparities extended intergenerationally, widening the racial college completion gap among veterans' sons by approximately 5 percentage points. The research demonstrates that unequal program returns—rather than preexisting socioeconomic differences—explain persistent racial inequality, illustrating that race-blind policies do not necessarily produce equitable outcomes.

## 3. A Cohort Perspective on Latin America's Fertility Transition [Fertility]
**Authors:** Regina Calles, Tom Vogl
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35326
**Abstract:** The authors analyze Latin America's fertility transition using census microdata from 17 countries, tracking female birth cohorts from the 1920s through the 1970s across subnational regions. They find that cohort fertility decline corresponded directly with mortality reduction. While urbanization, education, employment, and sectoral shifts all predicted fertility declines, women's education and sectoral composition were the dominant forces after covariate adjustment. Notably, the fertility decline showed no systematic connection to improvements in children's educational outcomes or employment prospects—a finding that challenges theories linking fertility to child human capital investment while supporting accounts centered on women's own educational advancement and labor market opportunities.

## 4. Caring Dads? Universal Childcare, Paternity Leave, and Fathers' Labor Supply [Fertility]
**Authors:** Mathias Huebener, Malin K. Mahlbacher, Sophia Schmitz
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18696
**Abstract:** The researchers investigate how expanded public childcare availability affects household labor allocation, with particular attention to fathers. Exploiting quasi-experimental variation in the roll-out of childcare places for children under three across German counties, they find that increased childcare access accelerates childcare entry and maternal return to work. Fathers respond by taking additional parental leave and reducing full-time employment, though weekday caregiving activities do not increase substantially. The study concludes that universal childcare expansions produce smaller aggregate labor supply gains than maternal responses alone would suggest, because paternal labor supply partially offsets maternal gains—a behavioral margin that prior literature has largely overlooked.

## 5. Dissecting the Gender Gap in Intergenerational Transfers: The Case of Germany [Gender]
**Authors:** Karan Singhal, Markus M. Grabka, Eva Sierminska
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18706
**Abstract:** The researchers examine wealth transfer disparities between genders in Germany spanning over 40 years, analyzing both inheritances and inter vivos gifts using Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP) data. They find that while women sometimes report higher rates of inheritance receipt, men receive larger gifts and larger total transfer amounts. Regional variation is pronounced: younger West German women face particular disadvantages in gift receipt, while East Germany shows no systematic gaps. Using Oaxaca-Blinder decompositions and Recentered Influence Function (RIF) regressions, they demonstrate that transfers contribute meaningfully to gender wealth inequality across the distribution. Despite gender-neutral inheritance laws, testamentary freedom and prevailing social norms perpetuate unequal outcomes—implicating informal institutions as a persistent driver of gender economic gaps.
