# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-18

## 1. De-biasing or Backlash? Confronting Prejudice Among Police Officers in India [Gender]
**Authors:** Sofia Amaral, Kim Chaney, Victoria Kaiser, Nishith Prakash, Abhilasha Sahay
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18699
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a lab-in-the-field experiment with 323 Indian police officers to examine how confronting evidence of biased handling of gender-based violence complaints affects subsequent officer behavior. While no average treatment effect emerged, outcomes diverged sharply by officer gender. Female officers increased victim prioritization by 23 percentage points (27% above control), while male officers exhibited backlash — deprioritizing victims and applying negative stereotypes — following the intervention. The authors attribute this heterogeneity to baseline differences in bias: 72% of female officers display only mild bias, while 51% of male officers are strongly biased. The findings demonstrate that de-biasing interventions can have opposite effects across subgroups, with backlash offsetting potential gains and underscoring the importance of accounting for individual bias levels when designing anti-discrimination training programs.

## 2. Caring Dads? Universal Childcare, Paternity Leave, and Fathers' Labor Supply [Fertility]
**Authors:** Mathias Huebener, Malin K. Mahlbacher, Sophia Schmitz
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18696
**Abstract:** This paper examines how expansions of publicly subsidized childcare affect labor allocation within households, with emphasis on paternal rather than maternal responses. Exploiting geographic and temporal variation in childcare slot rollout across German counties in a difference-in-differences framework, the authors find that increased childcare availability accelerated maternal return to work, as expected. However, fathers responded by taking more parental leave and reducing full-time employment, yet without substantially increasing their weekday childcare participation. The results reveal that childcare policies reshape household labor dynamics in ways that produce smaller aggregate labor supply gains than maternal responses alone would suggest. The interplay between childcare expansion and paternity leave uptake implies that family policy instruments interact in important ways and should be evaluated jointly rather than in isolation.

## 3. The (Lack of) Efficacy of Social Belonging and Growth Mindset Interventions Among College Students [Education]
**Authors:** Abid N. Alam, Philip Oreopoulos, Uros Petronijevic
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35230
**Abstract:** This paper reports results from four large-scale randomized controlled trials conducted across two major Canadian universities with nearly 12,000 participants, assessing whether growth mindset and social belonging interventions improve undergraduate academic performance and persistence. The trials find no immediate or dynamic effects on student grades and no effect on persistence through university. Machine learning analysis of treatment effect heterogeneity across a rich set of student characteristics reveals no evidence of meaningful variation in treatment effects across subgroups — that is, the interventions did not help even the students who might seem most likely to benefit. The results challenge optimism generated by earlier small-sample studies and underscore the need for large-scale, high-powered evaluations to reliably assess low-cost behavioral nudges before widespread adoption in higher education settings.

## 4. Explaining the Historical Rise and Recent Decline in Social Security Disability Insurance Enrollment [Health]
**Authors:** Manasi Deshpande, Maxwell Kellogg, Magne Mogstad, Kuan-Ju Tseng
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35300
**Abstract:** This paper investigates why Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) enrollment grew substantially during the 1990s and 2000s but has declined markedly since 2013. Using detailed administrative data and a statistical decomposition approach combined with causal analysis of labor market conditions, the authors find that the vast majority of the post-2013 enrollment decline is attributable to drops in application and award rates within demographic groups, with minimal contribution from shifts in the demographic composition of the population or changes in formal eligibility rules. The decline is concentrated among less-skilled men who increasingly choose work over applying for SSDI benefits. The evidence points to improved labor market opportunities for this group as the primary driver, while the authors find evidence contradicting the alternative hypothesis that reduced awards at the appeals level are responsible. The findings have important implications for understanding the relationship between disability insurance and labor supply.

## 5. Demographic Divergence: The Legacy of the Opioid Epidemic [Health]
**Authors:** Carolina Arteaga, Victoria Barone, Stephen Claassen
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35340
**Abstract:** This paper examines how the opioid epidemic shaped long-run local population dynamics across the United States. Exploiting Purdue Pharma's targeted marketing of OxyContin to high-cancer-mortality areas as an instrumental variable for opioid exposure, the authors find that areas with greater exposure experienced lower population growth among working-age adults by 2020. The mechanism is not primarily direct drug mortality but rather elevated out-migration rates, especially among college-educated individuals. Concurrently, fertility rates rose in heavily affected areas, partially offsetting population losses. The epidemic thus contributed to widening demographic and human capital divergence across U.S. regions, with exposed commuting zones losing their most mobile and educated residents. The results highlight health crises as a driver of long-term labor supply and demographic change beyond their immediate mortality effects.
