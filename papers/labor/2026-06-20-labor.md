# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-20

## 1. Too Competitive to Care? The Overall Explanatory Power of Personality for Occupational Gender Segregation [Gender]
**Authors:** Thomas Buser
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18721
**Abstract:** This paper asks how much of occupational gender segregation can be explained by gender differences in personality traits and economic preferences. Using data from a representative Dutch survey panel, I link a comprehensive set of personality indicators to career outcomes including women's underrepresentation in management and math-intensive roles, and men's underrepresentation in teaching, caring, and public sector positions. Correcting for measurement error, I show that personality and preference measures can statistically explain a large part — typically half or more — of gender differences in occupational sorting. Beyond the usual suspects such as risk preferences and competitiveness, traits associated with negative social behavior (willingness to exploit others, externalizing behaviors, and psychopathic tendencies) account for a substantial portion of these occupational gender gaps. The results suggest that non-cognitive traits and preferences are a first-order determinant of occupational gender segregation.

## 2. Blindly Discriminating: The GI Bill and Racial Inequality [Education]
**Authors:** Lukas Althoff, Christiane Szerman
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18720
**Abstract:** The GI Bill, enacted in 1944 with race-neutral language, was among the most ambitious education and social mobility policies in U.S. history. We show that its decentralized implementation through segregated institutions produced sharply divergent outcomes by race. Using regression discontinuity methods and census data linkages spanning 1940–2025, we find that the program doubled white veterans' college completion rates while directing Black veterans toward vocational training with minimal earnings benefits. These disparities extended intergenerationally: the racial college attainment gap among the veterans' sons widened by approximately 5 percentage points (a 47% increase). Our findings demonstrate that race-blind subsidies channeled through discriminatory institutions can widen rather than close racial gaps in education and economic opportunity, with consequences that persisted across generations.

## 3. Supporting Mothers Back to Work: Experimental Evidence on Employment, Fertility, and Child Outcomes [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniela Del Boca, Luca Favero, Chiara D. Pronzato
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18630
**Abstract:** This paper examines an intervention targeting mothers who reduced or exited employment due to childcare responsibilities, providing evidence on the effects of work–family reconciliation support. Using a randomized controlled trial, we find that improving work–family reconciliation can support mothers' labor market reintegration, promote investments in existing children, and, under conditions of greater stability, strengthen fertility desires. The results highlight the multidimensional benefits of family-friendly policies: treated mothers show higher rates of return to employment, improved child outcomes along educational and developmental dimensions, and increased stated intentions to have additional children. These findings underscore that childcare and labor market reintegration policies can simultaneously address women's employment gaps and demographic decline.

## 4. The Labor Market and Health Impacts of Reducing Cesarean Section Deliveries [Health]
**Authors:** Sarah Miller, Petra Persson, Maya Rossin-Slater, Laura R. Wherry
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18629
**Abstract:** We study an intervention that lowered cesarean section rates among low-risk first-time mothers using linked California birth records and administrative earnings data. Mothers in the intervention group were 8% less likely to have a c-section, with no adverse health effects for mother or child. We find suggestive evidence that these mothers were more likely to return to their previous employer and achieved higher earnings rankings in the quarter following delivery. However, these labor market advantages diminished over time. Notably, mothers who subsequently had additional children showed reduced rates of both c-sections and preterm deliveries, suggesting our estimates of the benefits of reducing unnecessary c-sections may be understated. Our findings point to previously overlooked labor market consequences of delivery mode, with implications for maternal health policy.

## 5. Explaining the Historical Rise and Recent Decline in Social Security Disability Insurance Enrollment [Health]
**Authors:** Manasi Deshpande, Maxwell Kellogg, Magne Mogstad, Kuan-Ju Tseng
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35300
**Abstract:** Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) enrollment expanded substantially from the 1980s through the mid-2000s before declining sharply after 2013. We use administrative data to decompose enrollment trends across these two periods. We find that the vast majority of the decline in SSDI enrollment since 2013 is attributable to declines in application rates rather than changes in award rates conditional on application. This decline is concentrated among lower-skilled men who increasingly opted for work rather than applying for disability benefits. Causal analysis using variation in local labor market conditions suggests that improved employment opportunities for less-skilled workers explain much of this pattern. We also examine and reject alternative hypotheses centered on changes in SSA adjudication stringency. Our findings illuminate the dual role of health and economic conditions in shaping the boundary between work and disability benefit receipt.
