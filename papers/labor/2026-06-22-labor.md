# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-22

## 1. The Labor Market and Health Impacts of Reducing Cesarean Section Deliveries [Health]
**Authors:** Sarah Miller, Petra Persson, Maya Rossin-Slater, Laura R. Wherry
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18629
**Abstract:** This paper examines an intervention designed to lower cesarean delivery rates among low-risk first-time mothers, using California birth records linked to earnings data. Exposed mothers were 8% less likely to have a c-section, with no adverse health effects. These mothers were also more likely to return to their pre-birth employer and achieved higher within-firm earnings rankings in the quarter post-birth, though initial labor market advantages diminished over time. Among mothers who went on to have a second child, the intervention additionally reduced the likelihood of both c-section and preterm delivery, suggesting that the estimated effects from avoiding a first c-section represent lower bounds on total gains. The findings highlight an underexplored channel through which obstetric practices shape maternal labor market trajectories.

## 2. Supporting Mothers Back to Work: Experimental Evidence on Employment, Fertility, and Child Outcomes [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniela Del Boca, Luca Favero, Chiara D. Pronzato
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18630
**Abstract:** This paper studies how policy interventions addressing work–family reconciliation affect mothers' labor market reintegration and demographic outcomes. Using experimental evidence from a program targeting mothers who had reduced employment due to childcare responsibilities, the authors find that improving work–family balance supports mothers' return to the labor market, promotes investments in existing children, and—under conditions of greater economic stability—can strengthen fertility desires. The study is motivated by declining fertility rates and population aging across developed nations and provides causal evidence that well-designed reconciliation policies yield joint gains for employment and family formation.

## 3. Does Field of Study Shape the Gender Wage Gap? The Role of Migration Background [Gender]
**Authors:** Louise Devos, François Rycx, Thomas Senterre, Mélanie Volral
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18640
**Abstract:** This paper examines how gender differences in wage returns to fields of study vary by migration background and how educational specialization contributes to the gender wage gap. Using employer–employee matched data covering over 62,000 master's degree recipients, the study finds that wage returns vary substantially across disciplines, with law, economics, management, and science offering the highest compensation. Women consistently earn less than men in all fields, with larger disparities concentrated in high-paying disciplines. First-generation immigrants from developing countries experience the poorest returns regardless of field, while second-generation immigrants approach but do not fully align with native-born outcomes. Field of study accounts for a sizeable share of gender wage inequality among natives and second-generation immigrants, though broader wage disadvantages dominate for first-generation immigrants. The findings underscore how horizontal stratification in higher education interacts with both gender and migration background to shape labor market inequality.

## 4. Blindly Discriminating: The GI Bill and Racial Inequality [Education]
**Authors:** Lukas Althoff, Christiane Szerman
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18720
**Abstract:** This paper examines how the World War II GI Bill—despite its race-neutral design—produced sharply divergent outcomes by race due to decentralized implementation through segregated institutions. Using regression discontinuity analysis and census data linkages spanning 1940–2025, the authors quantify effects across two generations. The program doubled white veterans' college completion rates while steering Black veterans into vocational programs with no earnings returns, widening racial inequality. These disparities extended intergenerationally, producing a 5-percentage-point (47%) widening of the racial college gap among veterans' sons. The study concludes that race-blind subsidies channeled through discriminatory institutions can amplify rather than close racial gaps, with unequal program returns—rather than prewar socioeconomic differences—driving the persistent inequality.

## 5. The Effect of Non-Parental Childcare on Parental Employment, Care Time and Quality: Time Diary Evidence from Korea, the UK, and Denmark [Fertility]
**Authors:** Nabanita Datta Gupta, Kristian Hedeager Bentsen, Youngsook Kim, Leslie S. Stratton
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18738
**Abstract:** This paper compares the effects of non-parental care (NPC) for young children on mothers' and fathers' employment and childcare time using time diary data from Korea, the United Kingdom, and Denmark. Mothers using NPC are more likely to be employed, particularly in Korea. In all three countries, mothers using NPC spend less time on childcare on weekdays; in Korea, reductions also extend to weekends and to quality care time. Effects for fathers are rarely significant. The cross-country variation in responses is explained in part by differences in family-friendly workplace policies, care type, and prevailing gender norms. The findings shed light on how the design of childcare systems and workplace institutions jointly determine the labor supply effects of publicly subsidized non-parental care.
