# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-25

## 1. Intimate Partner Violence Victimization and Perpetration: Risk Factors and Consequences [Gender]
**Authors:** Arizo Karimi, Susan Niknami, Petra Ornstein, Anna Sandberg, Hanna Mühlrad
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18742
**Abstract:** Using large-scale Swedish administrative data, this paper examines intimate partner violence (IPV) dynamics and their consequences. Perpetrators and victims disproportionately come from economically disadvantaged populations, and family background explains approximately one-third of variation in male perpetration and female victimization, based on sibling comparisons. Using a matched difference-in-differences methodology, the study documents large, persistent negative effects of IPV on victims' labor market outcomes and health. Social support systems compensate for average earnings reductions among victims, while perpetrators face sharp earnings declines and deteriorating health. The findings indicate that intimate partner violence reinforces existing socioeconomic disparities within society.

## 2. The Effect of Non-Parental Childcare on Parental Employment, Care Time and Quality: Time Diary Evidence from Korea, the UK, and Denmark [Fertility]
**Authors:** Nabanita Datta Gupta, Kristian Hedeager Bentsen, Youngsook Kim, Leslie S. Stratton
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18738
**Abstract:** This paper examines how non-parental childcare (NPC) influences employment patterns and caregiving behaviors across three countries with distinct institutional settings. Using time diary data, the analysis reveals that mothers using NPC are more likely to be employed, particularly in Korea. Mothers utilizing such care reduce their childcare hours on weekdays in all three countries, though the reduction extends to weekends and quality care activities only in Korea. Effects for fathers are rarely significant. The study identifies family-friendly workplace policies, childcare arrangements, and cultural gender expectations as key factors explaining cross-country variation, with implications for childcare policy design and gender equality in the labor market.

## 3. Intergenerational Effects of Turkiye's Education Reform on Children's Academic and Health Outcomes [Education]
**Authors:** Betul Akar, Cagla Okten
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18735
**Abstract:** This paper examines how Turkey's 1997 compulsory schooling expansion—raising mandatory education from five to eight years—affected the outcomes of the next generation. Using data from the 2022 Turkish Child Survey and a regression discontinuity design exploiting exposure to the reform among mothers, the authors find that maternal educational gains produced measurable improvements in children's Turkish language performance, though mathematics achievement showed no significant change. The reform's effects extended to children's psychological well-being, with treated children experiencing greater happiness and reduced anxiety. Gender-based heterogeneity is evident: boys benefit more than girls. Increased parental investment is identified as a primary mechanism, while reduced child labor contributed minimally.

## 4. Housing Policy and Fertility Behavior in Egypt: Evidence from the 1996 Rental Liberalization [Fertility]
**Authors:** Cecilia D'Agostini, Lucia Ferrone, Gianna Claudia Giannelli
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18734
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how Egypt's 1996 rental market liberalization affected fertility outcomes. Using data from four waves of the Egypt Labor Market Panel Survey combined with district-level housing information from the 2006 Census, the authors exploit geographic and temporal variation in reform exposure. The study finds that exposure to the reform increased the likelihood of childbearing and positively affected other fertility measures, including age at first birth and parity at ages 25, 30, and 35. The evidence indicates that earlier marriage is the primary mechanism through which the policy operated, suggesting the rental liberalization reduced housing cost barriers for young couples seeking to form households and have children.

## 5. Can Maternal Education Improve Children's Dietary Diversity and Nutritional Outcomes? Evidence from the 2003 Education Reform in Kenya [Education]
**Authors:** Sana Khan, Gianna Claudia Giannelli, Lucia Ferrone
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18737
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether maternal schooling causally affects child nutrition in Kenya. Using a fuzzy regression discontinuity design that exploits the 2003 free primary education reform as an instrument for maternal schooling, the authors find that an additional year of maternal schooling significantly improves children's nutritional status and dietary diversity. Benefits emerged for both genders, though boys showed slightly stronger gains. Multiple mechanisms are identified: treated mothers delay first birth, have lower fertility, achieve higher paternal educational sorting, utilize prenatal care more, have greater media exposure, and attain higher employment rates. The findings suggest that expanding educational opportunities for girls is an effective policy tool for improving child nutrition in developing countries.
