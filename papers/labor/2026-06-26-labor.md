# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-26

## 1. Intimate Partner Violence Victimization and Perpetration: Risk Factors and Consequences [Gender]
**Authors:** Arizo Karimi, Susan Niknami, Petra Ornstein, Anna Sandberg, Hanna Mühlrad
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18742
**Abstract:** Using Swedish administrative data, this paper examines intimate partner violence (IPV) dynamics across both victims and perpetrators. The authors find that IPV perpetrators and victims are disproportionately drawn from disadvantaged backgrounds, with family background explaining roughly one-third of male IPV perpetration and female victimization variation. The study documents substantial, lasting adverse effects on victims' employment and health outcomes, though social welfare transfers partially compensate earnings reductions. Perpetrators also face significant earnings decreases and health deterioration. The findings indicate that IPV amplifies existing socioeconomic inequalities, linking gender-based violence to long-run labor market penalties for women.

## 2. Too Competitive to Care? The Overall Explanatory Power of Personality for Occupational Gender Segregation [Gender]
**Authors:** Thomas Buser
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18721
**Abstract:** This paper examines how personality traits and preferences collectively explain occupational gender disparities. Using data from a representative Dutch survey panel, the study links personality measures to three career outcomes showing substantial gender gaps: women's underrepresentation in management and math-intensive fields, and men's underrepresentation in teaching, caring, and the public sector. The findings indicate that preferences and personality can statistically explain a large part—typically half or more—of gender differences in occupational sorting. Notably, traits with negative connotations—such as willingness to engage in unethical behavior, externalizing tendencies, and psychopathic traits—account for a surprisingly significant portion of these occupational disparities, offering new evidence on the mechanisms behind occupational gender segregation.

## 3. The Effect of Non-Parental Childcare on Parental Employment, Care Time and Quality: Time Diary Evidence from Korea, the UK, and Denmark [Fertility]
**Authors:** Nabanita Datta Gupta, Kristian Hedeager Bentsen, Youngsook Kim, Leslie S. Stratton
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18738
**Abstract:** This paper examines how non-parental childcare (NPC) affects parental employment and time allocation across three countries with different institutional settings. Using time diary data from Korea, the United Kingdom, and Denmark, the authors find that mothers using NPC are more likely to be employed, particularly in Korea. In all three countries, mothers using NPC spend less time on childcare on weekdays; in Korea, this reduction also extends to weekends and to quality care time. Paternal effects are minimal across all settings. The study documents that workplace policies, care arrangements, and cultural gender norms drive observed cross-country variation, with implications for childcare policy design and maternal labor supply.

## 4. Can Maternal Education Improve Children's Dietary Diversity and Nutritional Outcomes? Evidence from the 2003 Education Reform in Kenya [Education]
**Authors:** Sana Khan, Gianna Claudia Giannelli, Lucia Ferrone
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18737
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether expanded maternal schooling causally improves child nutrition outcomes in Kenya, using a fuzzy regression discontinuity design that exploits the 2003 education reform as an instrument for maternal educational attainment. Findings show that an additional year of maternal schooling significantly improves children's nutritional status and dietary diversity, with somewhat stronger positive effects for boys. The analysis identifies multiple mechanisms through which education benefits children, including delayed childbearing, reduced family size, enhanced paternal education, improved healthcare access, greater media exposure, and increased maternal employment. The authors conclude that expanding educational opportunities for girls can be an effective policy tool for improving child nutrition in developing nations.

## 5. Policy or Pandemic? Explaining the Rise in Young Adults' Private Health Insurance Take-Up in Australia [Health]
**Authors:** Nathan Kettlewell, Tiannan Gao
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18708
**Abstract:** This paper examines how an age-based discount policy implemented in 2019 influenced private health insurance enrollment among young Australians. The policy offered perpetual premium discounts (2%–10%) for those purchasing insurance before age 30. Using a synthetic difference-in-differences design, the authors find the policy produced modest positive enrollment effects prior to COVID-19, but significantly larger treatment effects emerged during the pandemic as overall demand for private insurance increased. The study concludes that the relative increase in demand among the young is predominantly driven by pandemic-era factors rather than the discount policy itself, yielding lessons for the design of age-targeted health insurance incentives and their interaction with aggregate demand shocks.
