# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-27

## 1. Do Wage Floors Increase Employment Risk for Workers with Disabilities? Evidence from Minimum Wage Increases and Subminimum Wage Repeal [Health]
**Authors:** Roisin O'Neill, Shailee Manandhar, Douglas L. Kruse
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35368
**Abstract:** Individuals with disabilities represent a substantial portion of workers earning low wages, prompting concerns about whether elevated wage requirements might restrict employment possibilities. This research analyzes state-level minimum wage policy changes and subminimum wage elimination using survey data spanning 2010–2023. The researchers discovered minimal evidence that raising wage floors decreases employment participation among workers with disabilities, even those experiencing more significant disabilities. Though some results lack statistical precision, the confidence ranges generally exclude sizable negative employment consequences. The study similarly reveals no indication that eliminating subminimum wages diminishes job prospects for those workers, with some findings suggesting favorable employment results for demographics likely employed under subminimum arrangements. "These gains may reflect complementary policies that often accompany repeal, such as Employment First initiatives."

## 2. What Happens to Contractors After States Ban Affirmative Action? [Gender]
**Authors:** Benjamin Rosa
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35362
**Abstract:** Using Census business records, the researcher examines how state contracting affirmative action bans impact minority- and women-owned enterprises. The study finds that such bans caused these firms to gradually reduce their workforce, with the most significant decreases among Black-owned businesses and larger MWBEs. While existing firms did not face higher closure rates, new MWBEs emerged less frequently after bans took effect, altering the demographic makeup of new contractors. A calibrated economic model indicates the bans functioned similarly to substantial productivity and asset value reductions for MWBEs.

## 3. Demographic Divergence: The Legacy of the Opioid Epidemic [Health]
**Authors:** Carolina Arteaga, Victoria Barone, Stephen Claassen
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35340
**Abstract:** The research examines how the opioid crisis influenced regional population changes across America. Using exposure variation from Purdue Pharma's OxyContin marketing strategy targeting high-cancer-mortality regions, the scholars discovered that more heavily exposed commuting zones experienced diminished population expansion. By 2020, a standard-deviation increase in exposure "reduced population growth among individuals aged 18 to 64 by 2.4 percentage points." Rather than deaths accounting for population declines, "exposure increased out-migration rates, especially among college-educated individuals," suggesting the epidemic functioned as a quality-of-life deterioration shock. The analysis also identifies a counterbalancing fertility surge that partially offset these demographic losses, demonstrating how the opioid epidemic fundamentally reshaped demographic composition and intensified population divergence among U.S. commuting zones over the long term.

## 4. Shaping Major Choice: The Role of High School Counselors [Education]
**Authors:** Kennedy Johnston, Jonathan Meer, Danila Serra
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35365
**Abstract:** The researchers examined whether high school counselors can influence students' field-of-study preferences. They conducted a randomized intervention where counselors from Texas high schools attended a workshop aimed at increasing awareness of economics as a major. Using application data from a large public university and Texas Education Research Center records, they found the intervention "led to substantial increases in interest in economics at the college application stage, particularly among high-achieving women," though it did not significantly affect actual college major enrollment. The study concludes that while counselors shape initial preferences effectively, "translating preferences into enrollment requires additional exposure and reinforcement."

## 5. Sent Away: Displacement, Neighborhoods, and Children's Outcomes under Slum Clearance Policies [Education]
**Authors:** Fernanda Rojas-Ampuero, Felipe Carrera
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35318
**Abstract:** The researchers analyze how relocation versus on-site redevelopment affected families in Chilean slums. Their findings indicate that "displaced children receive 0.62 fewer years of schooling, earn 10.2% less, experience higher labor informality" 40 years later. The study attributes these negative outcomes to lower-opportunity neighborhoods and disrupted social networks, providing causal evidence on how neighborhood quality and social capital shape long-run human capital accumulation and labor market outcomes.
