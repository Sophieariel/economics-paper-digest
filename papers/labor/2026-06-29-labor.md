# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-29

## 1. Shaping Major Choice: The Role of High School Counselors [Gender] [Education]
**Authors:** Kennedy Johnston, Jonathan Meer, Danila Serra
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35365
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a randomized trial examining how high school counselors influence students' field-of-study decisions. They invited counselors from Texas schools to attend a workshop focused on the economics major, which faces gender and racial underrepresentation. Using university application data and enrollment records, they found that the intervention led to substantial increases in interest in economics at the college application stage, particularly among high-achieving women, but did not significantly change actual college major enrollment. The study concludes that while counselors shape initial field preferences, sustained exposure is needed to convert interest into actual enrollment.

## 2. Empathy, Social Networks, and Academic Achievement: Experimental Evidence on Cross-Productivity in Skill Formation [Education]
**Authors:** Flavio Cunha, Qinyou Hu, Yiming Xia, Naibao Zhao
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35370
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a randomized trial with 2,246 middle school students in China testing a parent-directed empathy program delivered via mobile app. The four-month intervention initially increased empathy and reduced bullying, with no immediate academic gains. However, longer-term results showed treatment students were 3.2 percentage points more likely to enter elite high schools and 2.5 percentage points less likely to miss the entrance exam. The authors attribute these gains to improved self-beliefs and a restructuring of classroom friendship networks that reduced academic homophily, demonstrating how non-cognitive skill investments can alter peer relationships to generate academic benefits.

## 3. Is the iPhone Birth Control? Causal Evidence from AT&T's 2007–2011 Carrier Monopoly [Fertility]
**Authors:** Caitlin K. Myers, Ezekiel Hooper
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35310
**Abstract:** The research investigates whether smartphone adoption affected U.S. fertility rates. Using the iPhone's exclusive AT&T availability from 2007–2011 as a natural experiment, the authors found that access to the device reduced births by 4.5–8.0% among women aged 15–19 and 3.2–6.6% among those aged 20–24. They estimate the iPhone's diffusion accounts for 33–52% of the decline in the general fertility rate among women aged 15–44. Survey data suggests the mechanism involves reduced in-person interaction and increased pornography consumption.

## 4. Empowering Inclusive Work [Health]
**Authors:** Yanyou Chen, Mitchell Hoffman, Huilan Xu, Zhe Yuan
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35372
**Abstract:** The researchers investigate whether AI technology can improve outcomes for deaf and hard of hearing workers on a Chinese food-delivery platform. Initially, these workers demonstrated slower performance and lower customer ratings than non-disabled colleagues. The platform introduced an AI-based intelligent outbound calling system designed to improve customer communication for DHH workers. Using difference-in-differences analysis, the study found that the tool enhanced worker speed, reduced negative ratings, and increased retention while eliminating approximately one-third of the wage gap between disabled and non-disabled workers.

## 5. Do Wage Floors Increase Employment Risk for Workers with Disabilities? Evidence from Minimum Wage Increases and Subminimum Wage Repeal [Health]
**Authors:** Roisin O'Neill, Shailee Manandhar, Douglas L. Kruse
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35368
**Abstract:** The researchers examined whether higher wage floors negatively affect employment for people with disabilities. Using American Community Survey data spanning 2010–2023, they analyzed state minimum wage increases and subminimum wage terminations. Their findings indicate little evidence that minimum wage increases reduce employment or labor force participation among people with disabilities, including those with severe disabilities. The study similarly found no employment reduction from eliminating subminimum wages, with some indicators showing potential gains. The authors conclude the results provide little support for the view that higher wage floors create disproportionate employment barriers for people with disabilities.
