# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-06-30

## 1. Do Female Leaders Reduce Corruption? New Evidence from Brazil [Gender]
**Authors:** Julieta Peveri, Clemence Tricaud
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35308
**Abstract:** The authors study whether electing female mayors reduces corruption in Brazilian municipalities using close mixed-gender elections over two decades and multiple corruption measures: budget-based predicted corruption scores, audit irregularities, and legal sanctions. Employing a regression discontinuity design on close elections, they find no evidence that electing a woman mayor affects corruption outcomes. The null result holds across time periods, mayor characteristics, and electoral cycles. A negative effect detected in the small subsample of municipalities randomly audited in early terms is attributed to a sharp incumbency imbalance rather than gender, suggesting that previously documented effects in the literature reflect incumbency advantages rather than a causal gender effect on governance.

## 2. Intimate Partner Violence Victimization and Perpetration: Risk Factors and Consequences [Health]
**Authors:** Arizo Karimi, Susan Niknami, Petra Ornstein, Anna Sandberg, Hanna Mühlrad
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18742
**Abstract:** Using large-scale Swedish administrative data, the authors study the risk factors and causal consequences of intimate partner violence (IPV). IPV perpetrators and victims are disproportionately drawn from disadvantaged backgrounds, and sibling correlations indicate that family background accounts for roughly one third of the variation in male IPV perpetration and female IPV victimization. Exploiting a matched difference-in-differences design, the paper documents large persistent negative effects of IPV on victims' labor market outcomes and health, with the social welfare system more than offsetting earnings losses on average. Perpetrators experience sharp earnings declines and deteriorating health. The findings suggest that IPV amplifies existing socioeconomic inequalities.

## 3. Housing Policy and Fertility Behavior in Egypt: Evidence from the 1996 Rental Liberalization [Fertility]
**Authors:** Cecilia D'Agostini, Lucia Ferrone, Gianna Claudia Giannelli
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18734
**Abstract:** Egypt experienced a fertility decline in the 1980s and 1990s followed by a reversal that peaked at 3.5 children per woman in 2014. This study investigates the impact of Egypt's 1996 rental market liberalization on fertility by exploiting geographic and temporal variation in exposure to the reform across districts. Using four waves of the Egypt Labor Market Panel Survey combined with district-level housing data from the 2006 Census, the authors examine whether women of marriageable age in 1996 residing in districts with greater availability of liberalized rental units experienced different fertility outcomes. Results show that exposure to the reform increased the likelihood of childbearing and positively affected other fertility measures, including age at first birth and parity at ages 25, 30, and 35. Evidence suggests that earlier marriage is the main mechanism, consistent with the reform lowering housing constraints for young couples.

## 4. Intergenerational Effects of Turkiye's Education Reform on Children's Academic and Health Outcomes [Education]
**Authors:** Betul Akar, Cagla Okten
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18735
**Abstract:** This paper examines the intergenerational effects of Turkiye's 1997 compulsory schooling reform, which increased mandatory schooling from five to eight years. Drawing on a novel dataset from the nationally representative 2022 Turkish Child Survey, the authors use a regression discontinuity design to identify the causal effect of mothers' exposure to the reform. They find that the reform significantly increased maternal educational attainment and improved children's performance in Turkish, while having no statistically significant effect on mathematics achievement. Maternal exposure to the reform also had positive effects on children's mental health, increasing happiness and reducing anxiety, but did not affect general health status. These effects are heterogeneous by child gender, with boys benefiting more than girls. Increased parental investment appears to be a key channel, while reduced child work plays a limited role.

## 5. Can Maternal Education Improve Children's Dietary Diversity and Nutritional Outcomes? Evidence from the 2003 Education Reform in Kenya [Education]
**Authors:** Sana Khan, Gianna Claudia Giannelli, Lucia Ferrone
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18737
**Abstract:** This study examines the causal effect of maternal education on child nutrition and dietary diversity in Kenya using a fuzzy regression discontinuity design, with exposure to the 2003 school reform as an instrument for educational attainment. An additional year of maternal schooling significantly improves children's nutritional status and dietary diversity, with positive effects for both boys and girls, though slightly stronger for boys. Results are robust to a range of sensitivity tests. Further analysis identifies several channels through which maternal education improves child health: delayed first birth, fewer children under age five, higher paternal education, greater use of prenatal care, increased access to information through media, improved literacy, and higher maternal employment rates. The findings indicate that expanding educational opportunities for girls is an effective policy tool for improving child nutrition in developing countries.
