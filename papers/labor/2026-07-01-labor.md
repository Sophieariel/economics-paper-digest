# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-01

## 1. Do Wage Floors Increase Employment Risk for Workers with Disabilities? Evidence from Minimum Wage Increases and Subminimum Wage Repeal [Health]
**Authors:** Roisin O'Neill, Shailee Manandhar, Douglas L. Kruse
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35368
**Abstract:** This study investigates whether higher wage floors adversely affect employment prospects for individuals with disabilities, particularly those experiencing more severe disabilities. Using American Community Survey data spanning 2010–2023, researchers analyzed state-level minimum wage increases and subminimum wage eliminations. Key findings indicate minimal evidence that minimum wage increases reduce employment or labor force participation among this population. The authors note that confidence intervals "generally rule out economically meaningful negative employment effects." Additionally, terminating subminimum wages did not decrease employment opportunities; some results suggest positive employment effects for workers most likely affected by subminimum arrangements, potentially due to accompanying policies like Employment First initiatives. The research challenges concerns that higher wage floors create disproportionate employment barriers for individuals with disabilities.

## 2. Intimate Partner Violence Victimization and Perpetration: Risk Factors and Consequences [Gender]
**Authors:** Arizo Karimi, Susan Niknami, Petra Ornstein, Anna Sandberg, Hanna Mühlrad
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18742
**Abstract:** This Swedish study examines risk factors and consequences of intimate partner violence using large-scale administrative data. The researchers found that IPV perpetrators and victims are disproportionately drawn from disadvantaged backgrounds, with family background explaining roughly one-third of variation in male perpetration and female victimization. Using a matched difference-in-differences design, they document that IPV produces large persistent negative effects on victims' employment and wellbeing, though social welfare benefits offset income losses. Perpetrators face sharp earnings declines and deteriorating health. Overall, the findings suggest that IPV serves to intensify existing socioeconomic disparities.

## 3. Intergenerational Effects of Turkiye's Education Reform on Children's Academic and Health Outcomes [Education]
**Authors:** Betul Akar, Cagla Okten
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18735
**Abstract:** This study analyzes how Turkey's 1997 compulsory education policy—extending mandatory schooling from five to eight years—affected children's outcomes through their mothers' increased education. Using data from the 2022 Turkish Child Survey and a regression discontinuity methodology, researchers found that maternal exposure to the reform boosted children's Turkish language performance but showed no significant effect on math scores. The reform improved children's mental health outcomes, including greater happiness and reduced anxiety, though general health remained unchanged. Effects varied by gender, benefiting boys more than girls. The analysis suggests increased parental investment may explain these gains.

## 4. The Effect of Non-Parental Childcare on Parental Employment, Care Time and Quality: Time Diary Evidence from Korea, the UK, and Denmark [Fertility]
**Authors:** Nabanita Datta Gupta, Kristian Hedeager Bentsen, Youngsook Kim, Leslie S. Stratton
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18738
**Abstract:** The researchers compare how non-parental childcare affects mothers' and fathers' work participation and time spent on childcare across three nations. Key findings reveal that mothers using non-parental childcare are more likely to be employed, particularly in Korea. Additionally, mothers using such care reduce their childcare time on weekdays across all countries studied, with Korean mothers showing decreased engagement on weekends and quality care activities. Paternal effects proved largely insignificant. The analysis identifies family-friendly workplace policies, childcare arrangement types, and cultural gender expectations as contributing factors to observed cross-national variations.

## 5. Housing Policy and Fertility Behavior in Egypt: Evidence from the 1996 Rental Liberalization [Fertility]
**Authors:** Cecilia D'Agostini, Lucia Ferrone, Gianna Claudia Giannelli
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18734
**Abstract:** This study examines how Egypt's 1996 rental market liberalization affected fertility outcomes. Researchers analyzed data from the Egypt Labor Market Panel Survey across four waves combined with 2006 Census housing information to assess geographic and temporal variation in the reform's impact. The investigation focused on whether women of marriageable age in 1996 living in districts with greater availability of liberalized rental units experienced different fertility outcomes. The findings indicate the reform increased childbearing likelihood and positively influenced other metrics such as age at first birth and parity at various ages. The research suggests that earlier marriage is the main mechanism, consistent with the reform lowering housing constraints for young couples.
