# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-02

## 1. Do Wage Floors Increase Employment Risk for Workers with Disabilities? Evidence from Minimum Wage Increases and Subminimum Wage Repeal [Health]
**Authors:** Roisin O'Neill, Shailee Manandhar, Douglas L. Kruse
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35368
**Abstract:** The researchers examined whether raising wage floors negatively affects employment prospects for individuals with disabilities using American Community Survey data spanning 2010–2023. They analyzed state minimum wage increases and subminimum wage terminations across the United States. Their findings indicate that minimum wage increases do not reduce employment or labor force participation among people with disabilities, contrary to what standard labor demand theory predicts. The study similarly found no adverse employment effects from eliminating subminimum wages—the long-standing legal provision allowing employers to pay disabled workers below the federal minimum—with some evidence suggesting positive outcomes for workers previously earning subminimum wages, potentially due to accompanying Employment First programs that promote competitive integrated employment. The results challenge the conventional concern that wage floor policies disproportionately harm workers with reduced productivity.

## 2. Intimate Partner Violence Victimization and Perpetration: Risk Factors and Consequences [Gender]
**Authors:** Arizo Karimi, Susan Niknami, Petra Ornstein, Anna Sandberg, Hanna Mühlrad
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18742
**Abstract:** This Swedish study examines intimate partner violence (IPV) through rich administrative data, finding that perpetrators and victims come disproportionately from disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds. Family circumstances—including parental violence exposure—explain roughly one-third of variation in male perpetration and female victimization. The research uses a comparative event study design exploiting variation in IPV reporting and criminal conviction timing. It documents large persistent negative effects on victims' labor market outcomes and wellbeing, including reduced employment and earnings, though social support programs partially offset earnings reductions on average. Perpetrators face significant income drops and health deterioration following legal consequences. The authors conclude that intimate partner violence reinforces existing socioeconomic disparities and identifies family background as a key risk factor, with implications for early intervention programs targeting at-risk households.

## 3. Intergenerational Effects of Turkiye's Education Reform on Children's Academic and Health Outcomes [Education]
**Authors:** Betul Akar, Cagla Okten
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18735
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how Turkey's 1997 compulsory education expansion—raising mandatory schooling from five to eight years—affected the next generation's outcomes through maternal human capital accumulation. Using data from the 2022 Turkish Child Survey and a regression discontinuity design exploiting the reform's age cutoff, the authors find that mothers exposed to the policy achieved higher educational attainment, which subsequently improved their children's Turkish language test performance. Mathematics scores showed no significant improvement. Beyond academic outcomes, the reform enhanced children's mental wellbeing by increasing self-reported happiness and reducing anxiety, though effects on general physical health remained statistically insignificant. Results varied by child gender, with boys experiencing greater academic benefits. Evidence points toward increased parental investment and enriched home learning environments as the primary transmission mechanism, with child labor reduction playing a lesser role. The findings highlight the long-run intergenerational returns to female education policy.

## 4. Housing Policy and Fertility Behavior in Egypt: Evidence from the 1996 Rental Liberalization [Fertility]
**Authors:** Cecilia D'Agostini, Lucia Ferrone, Gianna Claudia Giannelli
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18734
**Abstract:** This paper examines how Egypt's 1996 rental market liberalization—which relaxed rent controls and expanded market-rate housing supply—affected fertility outcomes. Utilizing four waves of the Egypt Labor Market Panel Survey alongside district-level housing data from the 2006 Census, the authors exploit geographic and temporal variation in reform exposure in a difference-in-differences framework. Key findings indicate that exposure to the reform increased the likelihood of childbearing and positively affected multiple fertility measures including total children ever born and completed fertility. Women in districts with greater availability of newly liberalized rental units experienced higher fertility rates. Earlier marriage emerges as the primary mechanism through which the policy operated, suggesting the reform reduced housing barriers that had previously delayed household formation for young couples. The results provide causal evidence that housing affordability constraints meaningfully shape demographic outcomes and fertility decisions.

## 5. Can Maternal Education Improve Children's Dietary Diversity and Nutritional Outcomes? Evidence from the 2003 Education Reform in Kenya [Education]
**Authors:** Sana Khan, Gianna Claudia Giannelli, Lucia Ferrone
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18737
**Abstract:** This research investigates whether mothers' schooling causally affects child nutrition and dietary diversity in Kenya. The authors employ a fuzzy regression discontinuity design, leveraging the 2003 Free Primary Education reform as an instrument for maternal educational attainment among cohorts born around the reform threshold. Their key finding shows that an additional year of maternal schooling significantly improves children's nutritional status and dietary diversity scores. Benefits emerge for children of both sexes, though effects are slightly more pronounced for boys. The analysis identifies multiple pathways through which maternal education improves child nutrition, including delayed childbearing, smaller family sizes, increased paternal education, expanded prenatal care utilization, improved media access and health information, enhanced maternal literacy, and greater maternal employment and income. The authors conclude that expanding educational opportunities for girls represents an effective policy tool for improving child nutrition and feeding practices in developing countries, with implications extending well beyond the direct beneficiary generation.
