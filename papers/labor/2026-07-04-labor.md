# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-04

## 1. GLP-1–Induced Weight Loss and the Female Obesity Penalty [Health] [Gender]
**Authors:** Rebecca Diamond
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35387
**Abstract:** The research examines how GLP-1 medications affecting weight loss influence social and economic outcomes for women. Using survey data, the study compares women who began GLP-1 treatment with matched women interested in starting but who had not yet done so. Key findings indicate that single women experienced a 29 percentage point increase in marriage or cohabitation rates, while previously unemployed women saw employment rise by 27 percentage points after six or more quarters of treatment. The analysis suggests "part of the female obesity penalty operates at new-match formation rather than only through health or incumbent productivity," offering novel evidence that body weight shapes women's labor market and marriage market outcomes through channels distinct from health and on-the-job performance.

## 2. A Missed Opportunity? Labor Demand and Workforce Diversity [Gender]
**Authors:** Anna Bindler, Barbara Boelmann, Lena Janys, Luisa Santiago-Wolf
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18756
**Abstract:** The paper examines how labor demand shocks influence workforce diversity in the absence of targeted diversity policies. Using German reunification as a natural experiment, the researchers study academic hiring patterns following the large-scale expansion of East German universities. They find "increased dispersion in the institutional quality of hires" and that "female representation did not increase despite qualified women in the pipeline." Notably, female applicants showed substantially higher average quality than their male counterparts, suggesting that demand-driven hiring expansions do not automatically translate into gender integration—even when the supply of qualified women is ample. The results highlight a missed opportunity for diversity gains and caution against assuming that tight labor markets will mechanically close gender gaps in high-skill occupations.

## 3. Losses from a Disability: Temporal Variety and Earnings in the Long Run [Health]
**Authors:** Daniel S. Hamermesh
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18761
**Abstract:** Using time-use survey data, this research examines how individuals with mobility and physical disabilities engage in fewer distinct daily activities and experience substantially lower earnings than comparable non-disabled peers. Beyond documenting the earnings disadvantage, the study quantifies the welfare cost of reduced activity variety, finding that "the monetary equivalent of lost variety exceeding that of lost earnings." This suggests that standard income-based measures significantly understate the total economic burden of disability. The findings contribute to the literature on disability and work by demonstrating that disability's labor market costs extend well beyond foregone wages to encompass a broader reduction in the richness of daily life.

## 4. Leaving School Early: A Mediation Analysis Linking Adolescent Mental Health Disorders to Early Adult Outcomes [Health] [Education]
**Authors:** Anna Adamecz, John Jerrim, Ágnes Szabó-Morvai
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18763
**Abstract:** The study investigates how school dropout mediates the relationship between adolescent mental health disorders and adult labor market and life outcomes. Using Hungarian administrative data tracking half the school population through age 22, the researchers find that adolescents diagnosed with mental health disorders between ages 14–16 were "5.8 percentage points (or 34%) more likely to drop out of secondary school" compared to their peers. These disorders were correlated with substantially worse outcomes by age 22, including lower employment rates, higher NEET (neither in education, employment, nor training) rates, reduced wages, and elevated rates of early motherhood, sexually transmitted diseases, and substance abuse. Approximately one-third of the negative associations between mental health disorders and adult outcomes operated through the school dropout pathway, though the magnitude of this mediation varied considerably across outcomes.

## 5. Compensation vs. Reinforcement: Experimental Identification of Parental Aversion to Inequality in Offspring [Education]
**Authors:** Felipe Barrera-Osorio, Leonardo Bonilla-Mejía, Matias Busso, Sebastian Galiani, Hyunjae Kang, Juan S. Muñoz-Morales, Juan Pantano
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35375
**Abstract:** The study examines how parents allocate educational investments across children when facing within-household inequality in resources. Using a conditional cash transfer program that randomly varied transfer exposure among siblings, the researchers find that transfers increased school attendance for treated students but generated negative spillovers on their siblings: "untreated siblings of treated students were 3.7 percentage points less likely to graduate from college, a decline of about 30 percent relative to the control mean." The authors develop a dynamic household model and use it to identify parental preferences regarding inequality in children's educational outcomes, finding "limited aversion to inequality" and demonstrating that "the negative spillover is primarily driven by substitution in educational investments toward the treated child." The findings have direct implications for how conditional cash transfer programs should be designed to avoid unintended consequences for human capital accumulation.
