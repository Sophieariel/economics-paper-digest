# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-05

## 1. A Missed Opportunity? Labor Demand and Workforce Diversity [Gender]
**Authors:** Anna Bindler, Barbara Boelmann, Lena Janys, Luisa Santiago-Wolf
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18756
**Abstract:** This paper examines how labor demand shocks influence workforce demographic composition in the absence of targeted diversity initiatives. Using German reunification as a natural experiment, the researchers analyze academic hiring patterns in social sciences and STEM fields across Eastern German universities before and after 1990. They find that despite a substantially enlarged qualified applicant pool—including many women who had trained under the more egalitarian East German system—female representation in university faculties did not increase, and hiring patterns converged toward the pre-existing Western German norms. Simulations indicate that women who secured positions possessed substantially stronger credentials than their male counterparts, consistent with a model in which hiring committees retained latent gender biases even under conditions of rising labor demand. The study identifies a missed opportunity for demand-driven diversity improvement and highlights the persistence of occupational segregation in knowledge-intensive sectors.

## 2. Compensation vs. Reinforcement: Experimental Identification of Parental Aversion to Inequality in Offspring [Education]
**Authors:** Felipe Barrera-Osorio, Leonardo Bonilla-Mejía, Matias Busso, Sebastian Galiani, Hyunjae Kang, Juan S. Muñoz-Morales, Juan Pantano
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35375
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether parents compensate educationally disadvantaged children or reinforce investments in better-performing siblings. The authors exploit a conditional cash transfer (CCT) experiment in Colombia that randomly assigned transfers at the individual student level within the same household, generating exogenous variation in relative resources across siblings. While treated students showed attendance gains, untreated siblings experienced negative educational spillovers: their probability of graduating from college fell by approximately 3.7 percentage points—about 30 percent below the control-group baseline. A dynamic model of household schooling decisions fitted to the experimental variation reveals limited parental aversion to inequality across children. The structural decomposition indicates that parental substitution of investment toward cash-transfer recipients is the dominant mechanism, rather than any direct resource crowding-out. The calibrated model accurately predicts held-out treatment effects from a separate experimental arm not used in estimation, providing external validation.

## 3. Leaving School Early: A Mediation Analysis Linking Adolescent Mental Health Disorders to Early Adult Outcomes [Health]
**Authors:** Anna Adamecz, John Jerrim, Ágnes Szabó-Morvai
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18763
**Abstract:** This paper investigates the extent to which early school leaving mediates the relationship between adolescent mental health disorders and early adult labor market and social outcomes. Using administrative panel data that link compulsory-schooling records, health registries, and employment histories for approximately half of Hungary's secondary school population—tracked from ages 14–16 through age 22—the authors apply causal mediation analysis. They find that adolescents diagnosed with mental health disorders between ages 14 and 16 are 5.8 percentage points (34%) more likely to leave school before completing secondary education. By age 22, mental health disorders are associated with substantially lower employment rates, higher NEET incidence, reduced wages, elevated rates of early motherhood, sexually transmitted infections, and substance abuse. Approximately one-third of these negative associations are transmitted through the school dropout pathway, though the mediation share varies considerably across outcomes. The findings highlight mental health as a critical early-life determinant of human capital accumulation and labor market attachment.

## 4. Losses from a Disability: Temporal Variety and Earnings in the Long Run [Health]
**Authors:** Daniel Hamermesh
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18761
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether persistent physical or mobility disability reduces not only earnings but also the variety of daily activities—an underexplored dimension of economic well-being. Using linked data from the American Time Use Survey and the Current Population Survey spanning 2009–2024, the author identifies individuals who report a disability in consecutive survey years to focus on persistent rather than transient conditions. Propensity-score matching to similar non-disabled individuals reveals that persistently disabled workers perform nearly 4 percent fewer distinct daily activities and earn approximately 50 percent less. A key finding is that the monetary equivalent of lost temporal variety—valued using standard willingness-to-pay methods—exceeds the earnings loss itself, suggesting that conventional wage-based measures substantially understate the full welfare cost of disability. Single-year disability reports, by contrast, yield notably smaller estimated impacts, highlighting the importance of longitudinal data for accurately measuring long-run labor market disadvantage.

## 5. AI and Labor Market Outcomes: Evidence from China [Gender]
**Authors:** Tony Fang, Carl Lin, Qing Liu
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18740
**Abstract:** This paper estimates the effect of artificial intelligence adoption on individual wages, with particular attention to heterogeneous impacts by gender. The authors construct city-year measures of AI-related labor demand from 1.6 million online job postings spanning 2016–2024 and merge these with household-level microdata from the China Family Panel Studies. Using fixed-effects regression models, they find that a one-unit increase in local AI demand raises wages by approximately 0.2–0.3 percent on average. However, women experience gains two to three times larger (0.5–0.7 percent), while men show no statistically significant wage impact. Gains are concentrated in western provinces and in major AI-cluster cities, and within job categories where AI complements rather than substitutes for interpersonal and coordination tasks—roles in which women are disproportionately employed. The results suggest that, in the Chinese context, AI adoption has served as an equalizing force in the gender wage gap by raising the relative productivity of female workers in communication-intensive occupations.
