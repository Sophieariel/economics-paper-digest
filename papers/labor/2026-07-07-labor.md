# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-07

## 1. Explaining the Historical Rise and Recent Decline in Social Security Disability Insurance Enrollment [Health]
**Authors:** Manasi Deshpande, Maxwell Kellogg, Magne Mogstad, and Kuan-Ju Tseng
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35300
**Abstract:** Following expansion in the 1990s–2000s, SSDI enrollment has declined since 2013. Using administrative data linked across Social Security records and employment files, the authors decompose the decline into changes in application rates within demographic groups versus changes in group composition. They find that the vast majority of the decline in SSDI enrollment since 2013 is attributable to declines in application rates within demographic groups. The analysis indicates that improved employment prospects for less-skilled men drove much of this decrease, with these workers becoming increasingly likely to pursue work rather than disability benefits, highlighting how labor market conditions shape the size of the disability insurance program.

## 2. Is the iPhone Birth Control? Causal Evidence from AT&T's 2007–2011 Carrier Monopoly [Fertility]
**Authors:** Caitlin K. Myers and Ezekiel Hooper
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35310
**Abstract:** The authors examine whether smartphone diffusion contributed to the 22% decline in U.S. fertility rates since 2007. Using the iPhone's exclusive availability on AT&T from 2007 to 2011 as a natural experiment, they exploit geographic variation in AT&T network coverage to identify causal effects. Entropy-balanced Poisson and synthetic difference-in-differences event studies show that access to the iPhone reduced births by 4.5–8.0% among women aged 15–19 and 3.2–6.6% among those aged 20–24. The study suggests iPhone diffusion accounts for 33–52% of the decline in the general fertility rate among women aged 15–44. Survey data indicate the technology reduced face-to-face interactions and sexual frequency while increasing time spent on online activities, pointing to social and behavioral channels through which smartphone adoption affects fertility.

## 3. When a Common Shock Hits Differently: Gender, Adolescence, and the COVID-19 Well-Being Gap [Gender]
**Authors:** Audrey Bousselin and Denisa M. Sologon
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18765/when-a-common-shock-hits-differently-gender-adolescence-and-the-covid-19-well-being-gap
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether a widespread societal disruption affects children's subjective well-being differently by gender. Using longitudinal data from the Luxembourg Child Well-Being Survey comparing pre-COVID (2019) and post-COVID (2021) responses, the authors apply fixed-effects models to track within-child changes across measures of life satisfaction, positive affect, and negative affect. Girls and boys report similar levels of life satisfaction and positive affect before the disruption, but follow different trajectories afterward. Girls showed steeper declines in life satisfaction and positive affect alongside increased negative affect. These disparities were particularly pronounced in school experiences, family relationships, self-perceptions, and loneliness, with effects intensifying with age. The findings demonstrate that gender gaps in well-being emerged uniformly across socioeconomic groups, pointing to gender-specific mechanisms of vulnerability to large common shocks during adolescence.

## 4. Demographic Divergence: The Legacy of the Opioid Epidemic [Health]
**Authors:** Carolina Arteaga, Victoria Barone, and Stephen Claassen
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35340
**Abstract:** This paper examines how the opioid epidemic reshaped U.S. population dynamics. The authors exploit Purdue Pharma's targeted OxyContin marketing to high-cancer-mortality regions as a source of quasi-experimental variation in opioid exposure. They find that more exposed commuting zones experienced significantly lower population growth, with college-educated individuals particularly likely to migrate out. Direct drug-related deaths explain only a fraction of these population losses; the primary driver is selective out-migration of high-skilled workers responding to deteriorating local quality of life. The study additionally identifies a counterbalancing rise in fertility rates by 2020 in more-exposed areas, suggesting compositional shifts in the remaining population. Together, the results reveal a mechanism through which a public health crisis produces lasting demographic divergence across local labor markets.

## 5. Empathy, Social Networks, and Academic Achievement: Experimental Evidence on Cross-Productivity in Skill Formation [Education]
**Authors:** Flavio Cunha, Qinyou Hu, Yiming Xia, and Naibao Zhao
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35370
**Abstract:** This paper evaluates a parent-directed empathy development program for middle school students in China using a randomized controlled trial with 2,246 participants. A four-month mobile app intervention increased students' empathy and reduced bullying in the short term. Long-run follow-up shows treatment students were 3.2 percentage points more likely to enter elite high schools and less likely to miss entrance exams. The authors identify a social mechanism: non-cognitive investments in empathy reshape peer network relationships, which in turn improve academic outcomes — a form of cross-productivity in skill formation. The results have implications for education policy, suggesting that socio-emotional interventions targeting non-cognitive skills can generate measurable gains in high-stakes educational attainment, with downstream consequences for labor market trajectories.
