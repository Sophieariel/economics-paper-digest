# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-08

## 1. Baby Busts and Growth Booms: Demographic Change and the Macroeconomy [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daron Acemoglu, David Autor, Keelan Beirne, Andrew Scott
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35401
**Abstract:** This paper examines how declining global birth rates over the past seven decades have affected economic growth and labor market outcomes. Contrary to common assumptions about demographic decline, the authors find that lower birth rates are associated with higher growth in GDP per working-age adult across countries and higher wage growth across U.S. commuting zones, with no negative impact on aggregate GDP or total earnings. The authors argue the mechanism operates through technological adaptation: regions with lower birth rates develop more labor-saving innovations and expand high-tech activity, generating higher total factor productivity growth. Using World War II mortality data as a natural experiment to isolate the effects of younger-cohort population decline (as distinct from overall population size), the study documents that declines in the share of younger workers—not population loss per se—drive these effects. The findings challenge standard demographic pessimism and suggest that tight labor markets induced by fertility decline spur innovation rather than retard growth.

## 2. Compensation vs. Reinforcement: Experimental Identification of Parental Aversion to Inequality in Offspring [Education]
**Authors:** Felipe Barrera-Osorio, Leonardo Bonilla-Mejía, Matias Busso, Sebastian Galiani, Hyunjae Kang, Juan S. Muñoz-Morales, Juan Pantano
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35375
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how parents allocate educational investments across their children when one child receives an external educational transfer. Using a conditional cash transfer experiment that randomized student-level transfers within multi-sibling households, the authors identify variation in transfer exposure across siblings. Transfers boosted school attendance for treated students, but generated significant negative spillovers on untreated siblings: untreated siblings of treated students were 3.7 percentage points less likely to graduate from college—a decline of approximately 30 percent relative to the control mean. The authors develop a dynamic household decision model that reveals parents exhibit limited aversion to inequality in children's educational outcomes. The negative spillover is primarily driven by substitution in educational investments toward the transfer-receiving child, suggesting parents redirect resources rather than compensating disadvantaged siblings. The findings have important implications for the design of targeted educational transfer programs and the intergenerational transmission of human capital.

## 3. Blindly Discriminating: The GI Bill and Racial Inequality [Education]
**Authors:** Lukas Althoff, Christiane Szerman
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18720
**Abstract:** This paper examines how the World War II GI Bill—a formally race-blind education subsidy—produced vastly unequal educational and economic outcomes for Black and white veterans. Because the program's decentralized administration left implementation to local officials and segregated institutions, Black and white veterans faced sharply different access to its benefits. Using a regression discontinuity design leveraging veterans' birth dates combined with census data spanning 1940–2025, the authors find that the program doubled white veterans' college completion rates while directing Black veterans toward vocational training with negligible earnings benefits. These disparities compounded across generations: sons of white veterans experienced significantly better neighborhood outcomes than sons of Black veterans. The analysis demonstrates that ostensibly neutral education policies implemented through racially discriminatory institutions can amplify rather than reduce racial economic gaps, with effects persisting across multiple generations.

## 4. AI and Labor Market Outcomes: Evidence from China [Gender]
**Authors:** Tony Fang, Carl Lin, Qing Liu
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18740
**Abstract:** This paper examines how artificial intelligence adoption affects labor market outcomes, with particular attention to gender heterogeneity. The authors construct city-year measures of AI labor demand from 1.6 million online job postings spanning 2016–2024, merged with household-level data from the China Family Panel Studies (2016–2022). Increased local AI labor demand is associated with higher individual wages: a one-unit increase in AI demand raises wages by approximately 0.2–0.3 percent on average. However, the wage effects are substantially larger for women—0.5–0.7 percent per unit increase—while effects for men are negligible. Effects concentrate in Western provinces and major AI-cluster cities with strong digital infrastructure. Occupational analysis reveals that women's gains are concentrated in service-oriented roles where AI complements interpersonal and coordination tasks rather than substituting for them. The findings suggest that AI diffusion produces unequal spillovers that may reduce gender wage gaps in economies where women are disproportionately employed in AI-complementary occupations.

## 5. Leaving School Early: A Mediation Analysis Linking Adolescent Mental Health Disorders to Early Adult Outcomes [Health]
**Authors:** Anna Adamecz, John Jerrim, Ágnes Szabó-Morvai
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18763
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how educational dropout functions as a mechanism linking teenage mental health conditions to adult labor market and health outcomes. Using Hungarian administrative records tracking approximately half the school population from secondary school through age 22, the authors find that adolescents diagnosed with mental health disorders between ages 14–16 are 5.8 percentage points—or 34 percent—more likely to drop out of secondary school than peers with similar observable characteristics. Mental health disorders are further associated with substantially worse early adult outcomes: diminished employment rates, higher rates of not being in education, employment, or training (NEET), lower earnings, elevated rates of early motherhood, health complications, and substance misuse by age 22. Importantly, approximately one-third of these negative associations are mediated through school dropout, with the mediated share varying considerably across outcomes. The results highlight adolescent mental health as a critical leverage point for education and labor market policy, and suggest that school dropout is a key pathway through which mental health conditions transmit to adult economic disadvantage.
