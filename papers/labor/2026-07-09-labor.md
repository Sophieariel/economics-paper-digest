# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-09

## 1. Baby Busts and Growth Booms: Demographic Change and the Macroeconomy [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daron Acemoglu, David Autor, Keelan Beirne, Andrew Scott
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35401
**Abstract:** The paper examines how declining birth rates have affected economic outcomes across countries. Contrary to the expectation that shrinking cohorts harm aggregate growth, the authors find that lower birth rates are associated with higher growth in GDP per working-age adult and higher wage growth, without negative effects on total GDP or aggregate earnings. They attribute this pattern to labor-saving technological innovation induced by workforce scarcity rather than to gains in educational attainment or female labor force participation. The study exploits cross-country variation in WWII military and civilian deaths as an instrument for younger cohort sizes, distinct from overall population scale, and corroborates findings with patent data, total factor productivity growth, and commuting-zone-level labor market outcomes across the United States. The results suggest that demographic change spurs labor-augmenting technological adaptation, reframing population decline as a driver of productivity rather than stagnation.

## 2. Compensation vs. Reinforcement: Experimental Identification of Parental Aversion to Inequality in Offspring [Education]
**Authors:** Felipe Barrera-Osorio, Leonardo Bonilla-Mejía, Matias Busso, Sebastian Galiani, Hyunjae Kang, Juan S. Muñoz-Morales, Juan Pantano
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35375
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how parents allocate educational resources across siblings when one child receives an external transfer. Using a conditional cash transfer experiment, the authors find that untreated siblings experience significant negative spillover effects: when one child receives program transfers, the probability of college graduation among non-treated siblings declines by approximately 30 percent. The authors employ a dynamic household investment model to recover structural preference parameters and find that parents exhibit limited aversion to inequality in children's educational outcomes. The dominant mechanism is investment substitution—resources and parental attention shift toward the treated child—rather than compensation toward the disadvantaged sibling. These findings have implications for the design of targeted educational programs, suggesting that individual-level transfers can depress human capital investments in other household members.

## 3. When a Common Shock Hits Differently: Gender, Adolescence, and the COVID-19 Well-Being Gap [Gender]
**Authors:** Audrey Bousselin, Denisa M. Sologon
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18765
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether a universal disruption—the COVID-19 pandemic—affected adolescents' subjective well-being differently by gender. Using longitudinal panel data from the Luxembourg Child Well-Being Survey, which tracks the same children in 2019 and 2021, the authors estimate within-child fixed-effects models to identify pandemic-induced changes in life satisfaction, positive affect, and negative affect. They find that girls experienced larger declines in life satisfaction and positive affect and a larger increase in negative affect compared to boys, despite comparable pre-pandemic levels. The well-being gap widened as children aged, with girls' deterioration most pronounced in domains of school experience, family relationships, self-perception, and social isolation. Robustness checks address selective attrition and alternative scale specifications. The findings document a systematic gender divergence in the socio-emotional consequences of aggregate disruptions during adolescence, with implications for subsequent educational trajectories and labor market readiness.

## 4. Leaving School Early: A Mediation Analysis Linking Adolescent Mental Health Disorders to Early Adult Outcomes [Health]
**Authors:** Anna Adamecz, John Jerrim, Ágnes Szabó-Morvai
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18763
**Abstract:** This paper quantifies how school dropout mediates the relationship between adolescent mental health disorders and early adult labor market and social outcomes. The authors link Hungarian administrative data on education, health, and employment records for approximately half the national school population, tracking cohorts from secondary school through age 22. Adolescents diagnosed with mental health disorders at ages 14–16 are 5.8 percentage points (34 percent) more likely to drop out of secondary school than unaffected peers. Using causal mediation analysis, the paper decomposes total effects into direct pathways and the indirect channel running through dropout. Mental health disorders are associated with lower employment rates, elevated NEET status, reduced wages, and higher rates of early motherhood, substance abuse, and sexually transmitted infections. School dropout accounts for approximately one-third of these adverse associations. The results underscore the importance of early mental health intervention as an education and labor market policy tool.

## 5. Re-Examining the Property Price Premium Around 'Better' Schools [Education]
**Authors:** Ellen Greaves, Alberto Venturin
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18750
**Abstract:** This paper revisits the widely documented finding that proximity to higher-performing schools is capitalized into housing prices. Applying a boundary discontinuity design to English secondary school data, the authors find that property price premiums are concentrated in areas where the quality gap between nearby schools is large, and are weak or absent where schools are more similar. Households pay comparable premiums to avoid low-performing schools as they do to access top-performing ones when the quality differential is symmetric. The paper further shows that a sufficiently affluent school peer group is a necessary condition for attainment advantages to generate price premia, indicating that families value peer composition as a distinct school attribute beyond academic value-added. These findings have implications for how school quality is valued in housing markets and for the distributional effects of school choice policies that create differential access to desirable peer environments.
