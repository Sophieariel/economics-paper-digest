# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-10

## 1. GLP-1–Induced Weight Loss and the Female Obesity Penalty [Gender]
**Authors:** Rebecca Diamond
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35387
**Abstract:** This study examines how GLP-1 medications affect social and economic outcomes for women. Using the Understanding America Study panel survey, the researcher compared women who initiated GLP-1 medications for weight loss with matched controls who desired treatment but had not yet started. Single women who began GLP-1 treatment experienced a 29 percentage point increase in marriage or cohabitation rates, while jobless women saw employment rise by 27 percentage points after six or more quarters on the medication. Notably, existing relationships remained stable and already-employed women showed no job advancement, suggesting that weight loss primarily affects new-match formation rather than operating solely through health channels. These findings document substantial economic penalties associated with female obesity and reveal that GLP-1–induced weight loss generates gains concentrated in the formation of new labor market and relationship matches.

## 2. The Labor Market Value of Community College Bachelor's Degrees: Initial Evidence from a Resume Audit Study in Early Childhood Education [Education]
**Authors:** Riley K. Acton, Camila Morales, Julia A. Turner, Lois Miller, Kalena Cortes
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35404
**Abstract:** Community colleges are more financially, academically, and geographically accessible than four-year institutions. Yet despite most community college students intending to earn a bachelor's degree, few successfully transfer and complete one. Community College Baccalaureate (CCB) programs have emerged as a pathway allowing community colleges to award bachelor's degrees directly. In the first resume audit study of CCB degrees, the researchers submitted fabricated job applications with varying educational credentials—CCB degrees, traditional bachelor's degrees, and associate degrees—to actual early childhood education job postings. Employers viewed CCB degrees similarly to both traditional bachelor's and associate degrees, with statistically indistinguishable interview-request rates across degree types. Text analysis of employer responses revealed minimal differential treatment, while net-price simulations demonstrated that surface-level cost comparisons overstate CCB affordability advantages. The study provides novel causal evidence on how credential signals shape employer hiring decisions in an accessible and rapidly expanding degree pathway.

## 3. Compensation vs. Reinforcement: Experimental Identification of Parental Aversion to Inequality in Offspring [Education]
**Authors:** Felipe Barrera-Osorio, Leonardo Bonilla-Mejía, Matias Busso, Sebastian Galiani, Hyunjae Kang, Juan S. Muñoz-Morales, Juan Pantano
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35375
**Abstract:** This study examines how parents allocate educational investments across children when facing resource constraints and inequality among siblings. The researchers conducted a conditional cash transfer experiment that randomly assigned transfers to individual students within households, generating exogenous variation in transfer exposure among siblings. Transfers boosted short-run school attendance among recipient students but generated significant negative spillovers on untreated siblings: non-recipient siblings of treated students were 3.7 percentage points less likely to graduate from college—a decline of approximately 30 percent. Using a dynamic model of household schooling decisions estimated against the experimental variation, the authors identify parental preferences regarding inequality in children's educational outcomes. Results suggest parents exhibit limited aversion to inequality among offspring, with the negative spillovers stemming primarily from substitution of educational investments toward the treated child. The findings have implications for the design of targeted education programs and the understanding of intergenerational human capital transmission.

## 4. Losses from a Disability: Temporal Variety and Earnings in the Long Run [Health]
**Authors:** Daniel S. Hamermesh
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35417
**Abstract:** This study examines how mobility and physical disabilities affect both daily activity variety and long-run labor market earnings. Using time diary data from the American Time Use Survey spanning 2009–2024 and covering adults aged 25–61, the researcher used propensity-score matching to compare persistently disabled individuals against demographically similar non-disabled peers. People reporting a mobility or physical disability in each of two consecutive annual surveys undertake almost 4 percent fewer distinct activities per day and earn roughly 50 percent less than comparable non-disabled workers. A key finding is that the monetary value of the lost daily temporal variety—valued using a hedonic method—exceeds the earnings losses themselves, suggesting that conventional wage-based measures substantially understate the full welfare cost of disability. The research also reveals that analyses relying on single-wave disability reports substantially underestimate long-term effects, highlighting the importance of longitudinal identification.

## 5. Baby Busts and Growth Booms: Demographic Change and the Macroeconomy [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daron Acemoglu, David Autor, Keelan Beirne, Andrew Scott
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35401
**Abstract:** The secular decline in birth rates across the globe over the past seven decades has slowed population growth, raised average ages, and reshaped labor markets and the macroeconomy. Contrary to the widespread expectation that demographic decline hampers economic growth, the authors find that lower birth rates are associated with higher growth in GDP per working-age adult across countries and higher wage growth across US commuting zones, with no negative impact on aggregate GDP or total earnings. To establish causality, the paper exploits cross-country variation in WWII military and civilian deaths as a natural experiment, using mortality shocks to isolate the causal impact of declining younger cohort sizes on subsequent economic outcomes. The authors attribute the positive growth effects primarily to labor-saving technological innovation responding to relative labor scarcity, finding supporting evidence in patent data and total factor productivity growth. Educational improvements and female labor force participation do not account for the patterns, pointing to directed technological change as the primary mechanism through which demographic contraction raises living standards.
