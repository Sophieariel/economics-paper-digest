# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-11

## 1. Gender Differences in Self-Promotion and Career Advice [Gender]
**Authors:** Rigissa Megalokonomou, Juliana Silva Goncalves, Roel van Veldhuizen
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18777
**Abstract:** We study the role of self-promotion and career advice in sustaining gender differences in labor market outcomes. We conduct a pre-registered experiment in which "advisers" advise "workers" to attempt either a more or a less ambitious task. We find that women promote themselves less than men and, as a result, are 12 percentage points less likely to be advised to choose the more ambitious task. This gender gap in advice persists across both quantitative and qualitative self-assessments and is robust to variation in advisers' information sets — including when advisers observe workers' actual performance — but is eliminated and even reversed when advisers are informed of the gender gap in self-promotion.

## 2. Do State-Funded Pre-K Programs Improve Long-Run Outcomes? [Education]
**Authors:** Maya Mikdash, Jiee Zhong
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18790
**Abstract:** We study how access to state-funded early childhood education affects long-run educational achievement and labor market outcomes. Using the staggered rollout of Texas's public Pre-K program as a source of quasi-experimental variation and confidential administrative records linked to earnings, we find that Pre-K access substantially raised enrollment, especially in areas with limited alternative preschool options. In districts serving both three- and four-year-olds, Pre-K access increased earnings at age 27 and high-school graduation by approximately 4 percent, with no effect on college enrollment. In districts serving only four-year-olds, we find no significant impacts on employment or educational outcomes, suggesting that program funding adequacy — rather than availability alone — drives effectiveness.

## 3. The Rise of Female Autonomy and the Decline of Fertility: The Role of Mismatch [Fertility]
**Authors:** Claudia Goldin
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35425
**Abstract:** The fertility decline is everywhere in the world today and goes back decades for rich countries. Birthrates have been below replacement in the U.S. and Europe since the mid-1970s. I argue that the primary driver of the fertility decline in high-income nations is not the rising cost of children per se but the increase in female autonomy combined with a growing mismatch between women's and men's preferences over family roles. Men generally benefit more from maintaining traditions; women often benefit more from eschewing them. When men are unwilling to fully abandon traditional expectations regarding the division of domestic labor, career-oriented women face a stark trade-off and increasingly choose to forgo having children entirely or delay childbearing until the biological clock constrains them. I document these mechanisms using individual-level and cross-country data on fertility outcomes, gender-role attitudes, and labor market participation, and show that the degree of mismatch predicts the depth of fertility decline across high-income countries.

## 4. The Mitigating Effects of Unemployment Insurance on Short-Run Mortality [Health]
**Authors:** Andrew J. Hill, Krishna Regmi, Isaac D. Swensen
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18776
**Abstract:** We examine how unemployment insurance (UI) generosity affects mortality following mass layoffs. Using county-level data spanning 1995–2010, we exploit variation in state UI replacement rates and benefit durations to estimate the causal effect of UI on cause-specific mortality. We find that more generous UI reduces suicide rates at the county level in the aftermath of mass layoff events. These protective effects coincide with sharp spikes in overall mortality following job loss, suggesting UI offsets a meaningful share of the mortality cost of displacement. We detect no impact on overdose deaths, indicating that the mechanism operates primarily through financial stabilization and reduced economic distress rather than through substance-abuse channels.

## 5. The Labor Market Value of Community College Bachelor's Degrees: Initial Evidence from a Resume Audit Study in Early Childhood Education [Education]
**Authors:** Riley Acton, Camila Morales, Julia A. Turner, Lois Miller, Kalena E. Cortes
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18786
**Abstract:** We provide the first causal evidence on how employers value Community College Baccalaureate (CCB) degrees relative to traditional bachelor's and associate degrees. Using a resume audit design, we submitted fictitious applications to actual job openings in early childhood education while systematically varying degree type, institution name, and applicant demographics. Employers respond to CCB degrees similarly to traditional bachelor's and associate degrees, with statistically indistinguishable interview-request rates across credential types. Qualitative analysis of callback messages reveals no systematic preference for one credential over another. We also show that the apparent cost advantage of CCB programs may be overstated when actual net prices — rather than sticker prices — are used for comparisons. These findings provide initial evidence that the labor market does not penalize holders of this emerging credential, though broader replication across additional fields is needed.
