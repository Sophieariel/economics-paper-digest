# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-12

## 1. Demographic Divergence: The Legacy of the Opioid Epidemic [Health]
**Authors:** Carolina Arteaga, Victoria Barone, Stephen Claassen
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35340
**Abstract:** The researchers examine how the opioid crisis reshaped U.S. regional demographics and labor supply. Using Purdue Pharma's targeted OxyContin marketing as a plausible instrument for local opioid exposure, they find that heavily affected areas experienced reduced population growth, particularly among working-age adults. Drug-related mortality explains part of the decline, but the dominant channel is out-migration: opioid exposure significantly increased out-migration rates, with the largest responses among college-educated workers. As a partial demographic offset, fertility rates rose in heavily exposed areas by 2020, though not enough to restore pre-crisis trajectories. The results imply that the opioid epidemic had lasting effects on local labor force composition, human capital stocks, and long-run economic potential.

## 2. Economic Incentives or Social Norms? Labor Supply Differentials Between East and West German Mothers [Gender]
**Authors:** Bastien Chabé-Ferret, Zainab Iftikhar, JungJae Park
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18600
**Abstract:** Despite converging wages and institutional environments, East German mothers work roughly 350 more hours per year than their West German counterparts. The authors use a collective model of family formation and labor supply estimated on GSOEP data from 2000–2017 to decompose this gap into economic incentives and social norms. They find that the working-mother stigma prevalent in West Germany accounts for 73 percent of the gap. Strikingly, standard wage-equalization policies would actually widen the East–West disparity because higher wages raise the opportunity cost of staying home and interact with the norm to amplify it. The results imply that the effectiveness of work–family reconciliation policies depends critically on the social norm environment, and that norm-blind policy reforms risk exacerbating regional inequality in maternal labor supply.

## 3. Job Loss and Mental Health: The Role of Anticipation and Re-employment in Recovery Patterns [Health]
**Authors:** Olivier B. Bargain, Nicolas Herault, Daniel Nettle
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18549
**Abstract:** The researchers use 22 years of panel data from the Australian HILDA survey—covering 14,195 individuals across 4,251 redundancy events—to track the mental health trajectory before, during, and after involuntary job loss. Using fixed-effects regressions with SF-36 mental health scores, they document a generalized pre-displacement decline in psychological wellbeing that begins well before the layoff event, regardless of whether workers anticipated the job loss. Post-displacement, those who find re-employment achieve complete mental health recovery, while those who remain jobless show persistent deterioration. Anticipated versus unanticipated job loss does not meaningfully alter either the pre-displacement decline or the pace of subsequent recovery. The findings highlight re-employment as the critical policy lever for mental health following displacement, and suggest that mental health costs begin accumulating during periods of job insecurity rather than only after the layoff itself.

## 4. Work from Home and Fertility [Fertility]
**Authors:** Steven J. Davis, Cevat Giray Aksoy, Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Katelyn Cranney, Mathias Dolls, Pablo Zarate
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34963
**Abstract:** Drawing on the Global Survey of Working Arrangements and the U.S. Survey of Working Arrangements and Attitudes, the authors document that realized fertility from 2023 to 2025 and stated plans for future children are both higher among adults who work from home at least one day per week. The relationship strengthens when both partners in a couple work remotely. The authors estimate that lifetime fertility is greater by 0.32 children per woman when both partners WFH one or more days per week, a magnitude large enough to meaningfully shift completed cohort fertility. Effects vary substantially across countries with different rates of remote-work adoption, suggesting that the aggregate demographic impact of WFH depends on the labor market context. The results connect the post-pandemic reorganization of work to demographic outcomes, opening a new channel through which remote-work norms may affect long-run population dynamics and labor supply.

## 5. Is Teacher Effectiveness Fully Portable? Evidence from the Random Assignment of Transfer Incentives [Education]
**Authors:** Matthew A. Kraft, John P. Papay, Jessalynn K. James, Manuel Monti-Nussbaum
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34845
**Abstract:** A central assumption in teacher labor market policy is that effectiveness is a largely portable individual trait: a high-value-added teacher will produce similar gains wherever she teaches. The authors test this by using the Talent Transfer Initiative—in which randomly selected low-performing schools were offered $20,000 stipends to recruit high-performing teachers from higher-performing schools—as a natural experiment. Prior value-added is only moderately predictive of performance in the receiving schools: teachers who accepted transfer incentives experienced value-added declines of 0.12 student standard deviations relative to their pre-transfer records. The authors attribute this to weaker teacher–school organizational match, adverse institutional spillovers from disadvantaged school environments, and reduced familiarity with individual students. The findings caution against policies that rely heavily on redistributing effective teachers without accounting for context-dependence, and have implications for the design of educator recruitment and compensation schemes.
