# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-13

## 1. GLP-1–Induced Weight Loss and the Female Obesity Penalty [Gender] [Health]
**Authors:** Rebecca Diamond
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35387
**Abstract:** The study examines how GLP-1 medications affecting weight loss influence social and economic outcomes for women. Using a matched quasi-experimental comparison of women who initiated GLP-1 medications for weight loss against matched women desiring to start treatment but having not yet done so, drawn from the Understanding America Study, the author finds that single women's marriage and cohabitation rates rise by 29 percentage points and employment among baseline non-employed women rises 27 percentage points after six or more quarters. Existing partnerships remain stable and already-employed women show no additional job mobility. The research suggests the female obesity penalty operates partly through initial relationship and employment formation rather than solely through health effects or incumbent productivity channels.

## 2. Gender Differences in Self-Promotion and Career Advice [Gender]
**Authors:** Rigissa Megalokonomou, Juliana Silva Goncalves, Roel van Veldhuizen
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18777
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a controlled experiment examining how self-promotion and career guidance interact to produce gender disparities in professional advancement. Their findings reveal that women promote themselves less than men and, as a result, are 12 percentage points less likely to be advised to choose the more ambitious task. Notably, this disparity persists regardless of whether advisers had access to objective performance data. However, when advisers received explicit information about the gender gap in self-promotion behavior, the gap in advice was eliminated or reversed. The results suggest that informing evaluators about systematic differences in self-assessment behavior is a promising lever for reducing gender-based disparities in career progression.

## 3. Do State-Funded Pre-K Programs Improve Long-Run Outcomes? [Education]
**Authors:** Maya Mikdash, Jiee Zhong
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18790
**Abstract:** The researchers investigated whether access to state-funded early childhood education produces lasting benefits by analyzing Texas's public Pre-K rollout. Using administrative data and exploiting the program's phased implementation across districts, they find that access to state-funded Pre-K substantially increases enrollment, particularly in districts without alternative preschool options. Where districts served both three- and four-year-olds simultaneously, Pre-K access boosted earnings at age 27 and high-school completion by approximately 4 percent, though college enrollment remained unaffected. Conversely, programs serving primarily four-year-olds showed no measurable impact on educational or labor market outcomes. The findings suggest program funding levels and comprehensiveness, rather than mere program access, may determine long-run effectiveness.

## 4. Private Schooling and Fertility Decline in India [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniel Fernández-Kranz, Trong-Anh Trinh
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18771
**Abstract:** The study examines how expansion of fee-based private schooling reshaped fertility decisions in India, a country that achieved below-replacement fertility despite low income and persistent traditional norms. Merging data from the National Family Health Survey (2015–2021) with administrative district-level data from the Unified District Information System for Education covering 37,251 women across 525 districts, the authors analyze parity progression from the second to the third child. They find that households with only daughters are less responsive to local private-school prevalence than otherwise similar households with sons, driven by son preference interacting with the rising cost of child education in a competitive private schooling environment. The results illuminate a novel mechanism linking educational market expansion to demographic transition in developing economies.

## 5. Child Disability and Effects on Sibling Mental Health [Health]
**Authors:** Janet Currie, N. Meltem Daysal, Mette Gørtz, Jonas Cuzulan Hirani
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18783
**Abstract:** The study investigates how physical disability in a younger sibling impacts the mental health of older siblings using Danish register data from families with three or more children. Employing difference-in-differences models that compare first- and second-born children based on the third child's disability status, the researchers find that use of mental health services increases 13 percent among second-born children, with a 23 percent increase in psychiatric visits and an 18 percent increase in use of psychiatric medications. Effects concentrate in households where mothers lack a high school education. The findings persist even after accounting for child test scores, with evidence pointing to caregiving demands, parental stress, and constrained family resources as key mechanisms linking sibling disability to downstream mental health costs borne by other children in the household.
