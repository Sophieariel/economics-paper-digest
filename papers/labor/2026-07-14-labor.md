# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-14

## 1. Baby Busts and Growth Booms: Demographic Change and the Macroeconomy [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daron Acemoglu, David Autor, Keelan Beirne, Andrew Scott
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35401
**Abstract:** The research examines how declining global birth rates over seven decades have affected economic growth. The authors find that lower birth rates are associated with higher growth in GDP per working-age adult and wage increases, without harming overall GDP or earnings. Rather than traditional explanations like education gains or female workforce participation, they attribute these outcomes to the endogenous, labor-saving response of technology to the scarcity of younger workers. Evidence includes increased labor-saving patents and high-tech activity in lower-birth-rate regions, plus elevated total factor productivity growth. Using historical variation in WWII casualties as an instrument, the authors demonstrate that declining younger populations—not total population size—drive these macroeconomic effects.

## 2. Gender Differences in Self-Promotion and Career Advice [Gender]
**Authors:** Rigissa Megalokonomou, Juliana Silva Goncalves, Roel van Veldhuizen
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18777/gender-differences-in-self-promotion-and-career-advice
**Abstract:** This study examines how self-promotion disparities and mentoring recommendations contribute to ongoing workplace gender inequality. Through a pre-registered experimental design, researchers assigned participants to roles as either advisers or workers, with advisers recommending whether workers should pursue more or less challenging tasks. The findings reveal that women understate their capabilities compared to men, resulting in a 12 percentage point reduction in receiving guidance toward ambitious roles. The gender disparity in recommendations remained consistent regardless of whether advisers evaluated workers through quantitative metrics, qualitative assessments, or actual performance data. However, the gap disappeared and even reversed when advisers received explicit information about the gender-based self-promotion differences, suggesting information provision as a policy lever.

## 3. Do State-Funded Pre-K Programs Improve Long-Run Outcomes? [Education]
**Authors:** Maya Mikdash, Jiee Zhong
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18790/do-state-funded-pre-k-programs-improve-long-run-outcomes
**Abstract:** The researchers examined how access to state-funded early childhood education affects educational achievement and employment outcomes over time. Using data from Texas's Pre-K program rollout across different districts and years, they found that access to state-funded Pre-K substantially increases enrollment, particularly in districts without alternative preschool options. Where districts served both three- and four-year-olds simultaneously, Pre-K access boosted high school completion and age-27 earnings by approximately 4 percent, though college enrollment remained unaffected. In districts serving primarily four-year-olds only, the program produced no significant impact on these outcomes. The authors suggest that the effectiveness of state-funded Pre-K depends on program funding and dosage rather than access alone.

## 4. The Labor Market Impacts of GLP-1s [Health]
**Authors:** N. Meltem Daysal, Camille Fredrickson, Ida Lykke Kristiansen, Mircea Trandafir, Jonathan Zhang
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18787/the-labor-market-impacts-of-glp-1s
**Abstract:** The researchers examine causal effects of GLP-1 medications (such as Semaglutide/Ozempic) on employment-related outcomes using Danish administrative records and a matched stacked difference-in-differences methodology. They contrast individuals who began Semaglutide treatment during its initial two years of availability with comparable individuals who started treatment four years later. The study found that GLP-1 treatment reduces long-term sickness leave by 17.3% and estimates total fiscal benefits of GLP-1 initiation of approximately 1.3–1.5% of annual labor income per employed individual. However, the analysis revealed no statistically significant or economically substantial changes in earnings, workforce participation, or employment status over the four-year observation period, suggesting that the primary labor market benefit operates through reduced absenteeism rather than extensive-margin employment effects.

## 5. Child Disability and Effects on Sibling Mental Health [Health]
**Authors:** Janet Currie, N. Meltem Daysal, Mette Gørtz, Jonas Cuzulan Hirani
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18783/child-disability-and-effects-on-sibling-mental-health
**Abstract:** Using Danish administrative records from families with three or more children, the researchers employed difference-in-differences models comparing older siblings in households where a younger sibling did or did not have a physical disability. The study found that use of mental health services increases 13% among second-born children, with a 23% increase in psychiatric visits and an 18% increase in use of psychiatric medications. The negative effects concentrated in households where maternal education was below high school completion and remained significant after accounting for child academic performance. The authors suggest that caregiving demands, stress, and strained household resources are potential mechanisms underlying these associations, with implications for how disability policy and family support programs should be designed.
