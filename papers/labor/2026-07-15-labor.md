# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-15

## 1. Gender Differences in Self-Promotion and Career Advice [Gender]
**Authors:** Rigissa Megalokonomou, Juliana Silva Goncalves, Roel van Veldhuizen
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18777
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a controlled experiment examining how self-promotion affects career guidance. Their findings indicate that "women promote themselves less than men and, as a result, are 12 percentage points less likely to be advised to choose the more ambitious task." The gender gap in recommendations persisted across different types of assessments and remained stable even when advisers had access to actual performance data. However, the disparity was notably eliminated—and even reversed—when advisers became aware of the gender gap in self-promotion behavior.

## 2. Do State-Funded Pre-K Programs Improve Long-Run Outcomes? [Education]
**Authors:** Maya Mikdash, Jiee Zhong
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18790
**Abstract:** The researchers examined how access to state-funded early childhood education affects educational achievement and employment outcomes by analyzing Texas's Pre-K program rollout. Using administrative data, they found that access "substantially increases enrollment, particularly in districts without alternative preschool options." In districts serving both three- and four-year-olds simultaneously, Pre-K access boosted earnings at age 27 and high-school graduation rates by approximately 4%, though college enrollment showed no change. However, in districts serving primarily four-year-olds only, the program showed no measurable effects. The authors suggest that "program funding rather than access alone" may determine effectiveness.

## 3. The Rise of Female Autonomy and the Decline of Fertility: The Role of Mismatch [Fertility]
**Authors:** Claudia Goldin
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35425
**Abstract:** The paper examines the global fertility decline, noting that birthrates have been below replacement in the U.S. and Europe since the mid-1970s. Goldin argues that "greater female autonomy and a mismatch between the desires of men and women" drive this trend. She explains that men typically benefit from maintaining traditional family structures, while women often gain from rejecting them. When men are unlikely to abandon these traditions, some career-focused women opt against having children or postpone parenthood. The analysis draws on long-run cross-country and within-country variation to document how shifts in female autonomy correlate with declining completed fertility, and uses the framework to explain why high-education, career-oriented women exhibit the lowest fertility rates.

## 4. The Labor Market Impacts of GLP-1s [Health]
**Authors:** N. Meltem Daysal, Camille Fredrickson, Ida Lykke Kristiansen, Mircea Trandafir, Jonathan Zhang
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18787
**Abstract:** The researchers examined how GLP-1 medication treatment (Semaglutide) affects employment outcomes using Danish administrative records and a matched difference-in-differences methodology. By comparing individuals who began treatment early versus four years later, they found that "GLP-1 treatment reduces long-term sickness leave by 17.3%." The study estimates fiscal benefits of approximately 1.3–1.5% of yearly labor income per employed person. However, the analysis found no statistically significant impacts on earnings, labor participation, or employment status over the four-year period studied, suggesting that reductions in absenteeism represent the primary labor market benefit of GLP-1 adoption.

## 5. (Not) Talking Through Mental Health Problems: How Talk Therapy Impacts Healthcare Utilization, Health and Employment [Health]
**Authors:** Roger Prudon
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18773
**Abstract:** The author evaluates a Dutch initiative providing talk therapy through nurse practitioners at general practice clinics nationwide. His research reveals that the program expanded therapeutic access by 6 percentage points among those already seeking mental health services. However, it simultaneously "widened existing disparities in care utilization." Notably, while uptake increased, "the increased take-up of talk therapy did not significantly improve the health and employment outcomes of patients," suggesting potential limitations in the program's effectiveness despite broader accessibility. The findings raise questions about whether expanding access to mental health services translates into measurable improvements in labor market outcomes.
