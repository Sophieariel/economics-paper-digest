# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-17

## 1. Gender Differences in Self-Promotion and Career Advice [Gender]
**Authors:** Rigissa Megalokonomou, Juliana Silva Goncalves, Roel van Veldhuizen
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18777
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a randomized experiment examining how self-promotion and career guidance influence gender disparities in employment. Their findings show that women promote themselves less than men and, as a result, are 12 percentage points less likely to be advised to choose the more ambitious task. Notably, this disparity remained consistent across different assessment methods and adviser information levels, including when advisers reviewed actual performance data. However, the gap disappeared entirely—and even reversed—once advisers received explicit information about the gender gap in self-promotion.

## 2. Do State-Funded Pre-K Programs Improve Long-Run Outcomes? [Education]
**Authors:** Maya Mikdash, Jiee Zhong
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18790
**Abstract:** The researchers examine long-term consequences of accessing state-funded early childhood education on schooling achievement and workforce participation. They leverage variation created by Texas's phased Pre-K rollout across districts and cohorts using restricted administrative data. Key findings indicate that access to state-funded Pre-K substantially increases enrollment, particularly in districts without alternative preschool options. In districts serving both three- and four-year-olds simultaneously, Pre-K access boosts earnings at age 27 and high school completion by roughly 4 percent, though college attendance remains unaffected. However, districts exclusively serving four-year-olds showed no such improvements. The authors suggest the effectiveness of state-funded Pre-K depends on program funding rather than access alone.

## 3. The Labor Market Value of Community College Bachelor's Degrees: Initial Evidence from a Resume Audit Study in Early Childhood Education [Education]
**Authors:** Riley Acton, Camila Morales, Julia A. Turner, Lois Miller, Kalena E. Cortes
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18786
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted the first resume audit examining how employers perceive Community College Baccalaureate (CCB) degrees. They submitted fictitious job applications with varying credentials to actual early childhood education vacancies. Key findings indicate that employers view CCB degrees similarly to traditional bachelor's and associate degrees, with statistically indistinguishable interview-request rates. The analysis revealed minimal differential employer treatment in callback communications, though affordability comparisons suggest sticker prices may misrepresent actual CCB cost advantages. This pilot study represents initial evidence supporting an expanded audit across multiple fields.

## 4. The Labor Market Impacts of GLP-1s [Health]
**Authors:** N. Meltem Daysal, Camille Fredrickson, Ida Lykke Kristiansen, Mircea Trandafir, Jonathan Zhang
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18787
**Abstract:** The researchers utilized Danish administrative records with a matched stacked difference-in-differences methodology. They compared individuals starting GLP-1 treatment during Semaglutide's initial availability versus those beginning four years later. Key findings included a 17.3% reduction in extended sick leave, with total fiscal benefits of GLP-1 initiation of approximately 1.3–1.5% of annual labor income per employed individual. The study detected no statistically significant effects on income, workforce participation, or employment status over the four-year period.

## 5. Child Disability and Effects on Sibling Mental Health [Health]
**Authors:** Janet Currie, N. Meltem Daysal, Mette Gørtz, Jonas Cuzulan Hirani
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18783
**Abstract:** The research investigates how a younger sibling's physical disability impacts the mental wellbeing of older siblings using Danish administrative data from families with three or more children. The study employed difference-in-difference methodology comparing first- and second-born children based on whether a third-born had a disability. Key findings indicate that mental health service usage rose by 13% among second-born children, accompanied by a 23% increase in psychiatric visits and an 18% increase in psychiatric medication usage. The effects were most pronounced in families where the mother had not completed high school and remained significant even after accounting for child academic performance. Parental behavior patterns suggest that increased caregiving responsibilities, psychological stress, and constrained household resources may explain these outcomes.
