# Labor Economics Papers — 2026-07-18

## 1. Gender Differences in Self-Promotion and Career Advice [Gender]
**Authors:** Rigissa Megalokonomou, Juliana Silva Goncalves, Roel van Veldhuizen
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18777/gender-differences-in-self-promotion-and-career-advice
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a controlled experiment examining how self-promotion behavior influences the career advice people receive. Their findings reveal that women promote themselves less than men and, as a result, are 12 percentage points less likely to be advised to choose the more ambitious task. The gender disparity in advice remained consistent across different assessment types and information scenarios. However, when advisers received explicit notification about the gender gap in self-promotion, this difference was eliminated and even reversed, suggesting that information interventions can help correct advice disparities driven by differential self-promotion behavior.

## 2. Do State-Funded Pre-K Programs Improve Long-Run Outcomes? [Education]
**Authors:** Maya Mikdash, Jiee Zhong
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18790/do-state-funded-pre-k-programs-improve-long-run-outcomes
**Abstract:** The researchers investigated how access to state-funded early childhood education affects long-term educational and employment outcomes using administrative data and Texas's phased Pre-K rollout as a natural experiment. They found that access to state-funded Pre-K substantially increases enrollment, particularly in districts without alternative preschool options. In districts serving both three- and four-year-olds simultaneously, Pre-K access raised earnings at age 27 and high school graduation by approximately 4 percent, though college enrollment remained unaffected. However, districts primarily serving only four-year-olds experienced no measurable labor market or educational benefits, with evidence suggesting program funding level—rather than mere access—determines effectiveness.

## 3. The Labor Market Impacts of GLP-1s [Health]
**Authors:** N. Meltem Daysal, Camille Fredrickson, Ida Lykke Kristiansen, Mircea Trandafir, Jonathan Zhang
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18787/the-labor-market-impacts-of-glp-1s
**Abstract:** The research team examined how GLP-1 medication (used to treat obesity and diabetes) affects employment and earnings using Danish administrative records. Comparing individuals who started treatment early with similar patients beginning therapy four years later, they found that GLP-1 treatment reduces long-term sickness leave by 17.3%, generating fiscal benefits of roughly 1.3–1.5% of yearly labor earnings per working person. However, the researchers found no significant effects on income, overall workforce involvement, or employment status over the four-year observation window, suggesting the medication's primary labor market benefit operates through reduced work absence rather than employment or wage gains.

## 4. Private Schooling and Fertility Decline in India [Fertility]
**Authors:** Daniel Fernández-Kranz, Trong-Anh Trinh
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18771/private-schooling-and-fertility-decline-in-india
**Abstract:** The research explores how India's expansion of private, fee-based education shaped fertility decisions despite conditions typically associated with higher birth rates. Using data on over 37,000 women across 525 districts, the authors analyzed how increased costs for child education influenced family size choices and found that the effect varies by child gender due to son preference in private school enrollment. Their key discovery is that households with only daughters are less responsive to local private-school prevalence than otherwise similar households with sons, a pattern consistent across multiple analytical approaches and demographic subgroups. This asymmetry illuminates how gender-biased investment norms interact with educational costs to shape fertility behavior in developing countries.

## 5. Degrees of Mobility: Shifting Low-Income Students to Higher Quality Colleges [Education]
**Authors:** Elizabeth Burland, Nora Delaney, Susan Dynarski, Katherine B. Leu, CJ Libassi, Katherine Michelmore, Stephanie Owen, Mary A. Quiroga, Elizabeth Salinas
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35434
**Abstract:** The research examines how a scholarship program offering four years of tuition coverage at University of Michigan campuses affected low-income, disadvantaged students. The intervention increased enrollment at UM by 21 percentage points, primarily redirecting students from less-selective institutions rather than expanding college attendance overall. Four-year degree completion improved by 4 percentage points, and the scholarship substantially enhanced degree prestige, raising the likelihood of earning a bachelor's from a highly selective institution by 12 percentage points. Using instrumental variables, the researchers estimate that induced attendees were 56 percentage points more likely to obtain degrees from elite colleges, potentially yielding lifetime earnings gains between 18–25 percent.
