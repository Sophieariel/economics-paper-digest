# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-01

## 1. Mapping AI into Production: A Field Experiment on Firm Performance [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Hyunjin Kim, Dahyeon Kim, and Rembrand Koning
**Date:** April 3, 2026 (INSEAD Working Paper No. 2026/20/STR; HBS Working Paper; SSRN posted April 2026)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6513481
**Abstract:** Although recent evidence shows that AI can deliver substantial productivity gains on individual tasks, evidence on whether these gains aggregate to firm-level performance remains limited. This paper studies what the authors call the "mapping problem": discovering where and how AI creates value within a firm's production process. Across 515 high-growth startups worldwide, the authors run a randomized field experiment in which treated firms receive structured information about how peer firms have reorganized production around AI across product development, strategy, sales, marketing, operations, and other functions. Treated firms identify 44% more AI use cases relative to controls, with the additional use cases concentrated in product development and strategy rather than in routine back-office tasks. These changes translate into economically meaningful firm-level performance gains: treated firms complete 12% more tasks, are 18% more likely to acquire paying customers, and generate roughly 1.9 times higher revenue than controls. Effects are largest among ventures in the top decile of baseline performance and are accompanied by a roughly $220,000 (39.5%) reduction in external capital demand and no detectable change in labor demand. The authors interpret the results as evidence that, even at current capability levels, the binding constraint on AI's firm-level returns is organizational—discovering where and how to deploy AI inside the firm—rather than the underlying technology itself, and that information frictions in the diffusion of AI use cases across firms generate sizable productivity losses.

## 2. AI Users Are Not All Alike: The Characteristics of French Firms Buying and Developing AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Flavio Calvino and Luca Fontanelli
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 5, Article 105473)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105473
**Abstract:** This paper provides representative firm-level evidence on the characteristics of French firms using artificial intelligence and on the relationship between AI use and productivity. Using the official 2018 French ICT survey, the authors distinguish AI users that source AI systems from external providers ("AI buyers") from those that develop their own AI in-house ("AI developers"). AI buyers tend to be larger than non-users, but this size premium is largely accounted for by ICT-related characteristics such as digital infrastructure and skills; AI developers, by contrast, are larger and younger even after controlling for ICT factors and rely on substantially more specialized ICT skills, including data science and software development competencies. Aggregate productivity comparisons show that AI users are on average more productive than non-users, but most of this gap is driven by selection: more productive firms are more likely to adopt AI in the first place. After accounting for selection, the productivity premium associated with AI use is no longer statistically significant for AI buyers, while it remains positive and significant for AI developers. The authors interpret these patterns as evidence that the productivity returns to AI are concentrated among firms that internalize AI capabilities and combine them with complementary digital and human capital, with implications for measurement, diffusion policy, and inequality across firms in the early phase of AI adoption.

## 3. Measuring the Impact of Data Centers in the United States Economy: Monetary Damage from Air Pollution and Greenhouse Gas Emissions [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Nicholas Z. Muller
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35100)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35100
**Abstract:** This paper provides the first comprehensive estimates of the environmental external costs of the U.S. data-center sector, an industry whose recent expansion is driven primarily by demand for AI training and inference. Using facility-level data on approximately 2,800 operational U.S. data centers in 2025, combined with grid-level emissions intensities and the AP4 integrated assessment model, the author quantifies monetized damages from local air pollutants (PM2.5, NOx, SO2) and greenhouse gas emissions associated with electricity consumption. Data centers consume roughly 250 TWh annually—about 5–6% of total U.S. electricity generation—generating approximately $25 billion in gross external damages (GED) per year, with a plausible range of $10–$33 billion depending on assumptions about the social cost of carbon and marginal grid emissions. Damages are highly geographically concentrated: Texas and Virginia together account for roughly 30% of national totals. Although aggregate GED amounts to about 5% of data-center industry GDP, this ratio varies substantially across states, reflecting differences in the underlying generation mix. Forward-looking projections suggest that anticipated AI-driven expansion could increase data-center electricity demand and associated environmental damages by up to 85% in the near term, although preliminary calculations indicate that the implied environmental costs remain modest relative to plausible AI-driven productivity gains. The paper provides new empirical inputs for ongoing debates about the regulation, siting, and grid-integration of AI infrastructure.

## 4. Specialisation and the Career Outcomes of Inventors [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Jordan Bisset
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 5, Article 105477)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105477
**Abstract:** This paper examines how the increasing specialization of inventive work affects the career outcomes of inventors and the impact of the inventions they produce. Building on the observation that modern innovation has become more team-based and more concentrated in narrow technological niches, the author constructs a panel of highly prolific inventors observed throughout their careers using harmonized patent and inventor-disambiguation data, and develops a framework in which inventors face a trade-off between accumulating knowledge depth (within a narrow technology class) and knowledge breadth (across classes). After controlling for selection into specialized careers using inventor fixed effects and instruments based on early-career exposure to specialized teams and employers, the empirical results show that knowledge depth is consistently negatively associated with the probability that an inventor contributes to an invention that receives citations in the top quantile of the technology-class distribution. The negative association persists across technological fields, career stages, and alternative measures of inventive impact, and is robust to controlling for inventor productivity, team size, and employer type. The author interprets the results as evidence that the secular shift toward inventor specialization, while privately rewarded, may come at the cost of fewer high-impact inventions—an empirically grounded concern for innovation policy given the disproportionate social value of breakthrough patents.

## 5. Political, Economic and Research Disintegration: The Impact of Geopolitical Uncertainty on Innovation and Cross-Border R&D Collaborations [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Enrico Vanino, Mustapha Douch, and Ismail Çakmak
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 5, Article 105471)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105471
**Abstract:** This paper provides causal empirical evidence on how geopolitical uncertainty affects innovation and cross-border R&D collaboration. The authors exploit the Brexit referendum and subsequent withdrawal process as a quasi-natural experiment that generated a sustained, largely exogenous increase in policy uncertainty for UK-based organizations engaged in EU-linked research networks. Using detailed patent and inventor data linked to organization-level information for the UK and EU, and applying difference-in-differences and synthetic-control designs, the authors document a clear restructuring of cross-border research collaborations after the Brexit referendum: the share of EU-based co-inventors on patents filed by UK organizations declines significantly, while the share of UK-based co-inventors rises by a comparable magnitude. This compositional shift is associated with reduced patenting activity by UK organizations at the European Patent Office and other foreign patent offices, while domestic UK IPO filings are largely unaffected, suggesting a geographic refocusing of innovation toward the home market. The authors further show that the quality of post-referendum UK inventions—measured by forward citations and originality—declines on average, consistent with the loss of complementary knowledge inputs from former EU collaborators. The findings provide new empirical evidence that geopolitical uncertainty disintegrates research networks, with persistent costs for the productivity and quality of national innovation systems, and inform ongoing debates over science diplomacy, international collaboration, and the design of R&D policy under heightened geopolitical fragmentation.
