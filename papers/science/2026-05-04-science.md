## 1. AI Patents in the United States and China: Measurement, Organization, and Knowledge Flows [Economics of Science]

**Authors:** Hanming Fang, Xian Gu, Hanyin Yan, Wu Zhu
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper 35022
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35022

**Abstract:** The authors develop a high-precision classifier to measure AI patents and apply it to U.S. patents from 1976–2023 and Chinese patents from 2010–2023. The analysis documents rapid AI patenting growth in both nations, with convergence in intensity and subfields, though China recently surpassed the U.S. in annual counts. Sharp organizational differences emerge: U.S. AI patenting concentrates among large private firms in established innovation hubs, while Chinese activity is more geographically distributed across universities and state-owned enterprises. For publicly listed companies, AI patents carry significant market-value premiums in both countries. Cross-border citation analysis demonstrates continued technological interdependence rather than decoupling, providing empirical evidence on knowledge flows in the global AI innovation system.

## 2. Forecasting the Economic Effects of AI [Economics of AI]

**Authors:** Ezra Karger, Otto Kuusela, Jason Abaluck, Kevin A. Bryan, Basil Halperin, Todd R. Jones, Connacher Murphy, Philip Trammell, Matt Reynolds, Dan Mayland, Ria Viswanathan, Ananaya Mittal, Rebecca Ceppas de Castro, Josh Rosenberg, Philip Tetlock, et al.
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper 35046
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35046

**Abstract:** The paper compares AI economic impact forecasts elicited from five distinct groups: academic economists, AI company employees, AI policy researchers, highly accurate forecasters (superforecasters), and the general public. The median forecast anticipates substantial advances in AI capabilities by 2030, with annual GDP growth reaching 2.5 percent. Under a rapid AI progress scenario, experts predict GDP growth around 4 percent and labor force participation declining from 62 percent to 55 percent by 2050, with approximately 10 million jobs displaced by AI. The findings reveal that expert disagreement stems primarily from differing views on the economic effects of highly capable AI systems rather than from differences in beliefs about AI development pace, highlighting distinct sources of uncertainty about AI's macroeconomic trajectory.

## 3. How (un)Stable Are LLM Occupational Exposure Scores? Evidence from Multi-Model Replication [Economics of AI]

**Authors:** Michelle Yin, Hoa Vu, Claudia Persico
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** NBER Working Paper 35110
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35110

**Abstract:** The researchers demonstrate that measures of AI's labor-market effects derived from large language models are highly unstable across models. Testing three frontier LLMs on identical task-annotation assignments revealed a 3.6-fold divergence in mean occupational exposure scores, with agreement as low as 57 percent across models. This measurement inconsistency has substantial downstream consequences for empirical research: coefficient magnitudes in labor-market regressions vary significantly depending on which model annotated the data. The paper raises critical concerns about treating evolving AI systems as reliable static measurement instruments for economic research and provides guidance for robustness testing in AI-exposure studies.

## 4. Attention (And Money) Is All You Need: Why Universities Are Struggling to Keep AI Talent [Economics of Science]

**Authors:** Ufuk Akcigit, Craig A. Chikis, Emin Dinlersoz, Nathan Goldschlag
**Date:** March 2026 *(outside 30-day window by ~4–5 weeks)*
**Source:** NBER Working Paper 34964
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34964

**Abstract:** The paper constructs a novel dataset linking academic publication records to U.S. Census employer-employee data to track approximately 42,000 AI researchers over two decades. The findings reveal a significant and accelerating talent migration toward industry, disproportionately affecting younger researchers and foreign-born scientists. A dramatic wage gap has emerged: the top 1 percent of publishing industry scientists now earn $1.5 million more annually than comparable academics, a fivefold increase since 2001. Researchers who move to industry publish less but patent more, consistent with a shift from open science toward proprietary innovation. The results point to structural forces reshaping the scientific labor market and the public–private allocation of AI knowledge production.

## 5. Knowledge Spillovers and Local Outcomes: An Existence Proof from the Establishment of the National Labs [Economics of Science]

**Authors:** Susan Helper, Resem Makan, Daniel W. Shoag
**Date:** March 2026 *(outside 30-day window by ~4–5 weeks)*
**Source:** NBER Working Paper 35011
**URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35011

**Abstract:** This paper examines how the U.S. National Laboratories established during the 1940s–1950s generated local economic spillovers. The authors exploit the fact that lab locations were chosen for security and political reasons rather than existing innovative capability, enabling causal identification by comparing lab counties to runner-up locations. Using newly digitized county-level data spanning 1936–1970, the study documents sustained increases in patenting by non-lab inventors in lab counties, with patents increasingly aligned to laboratory research areas, as well as long-lasting growth in retail sales and household income. Wage gains accrued to pre-existing residents, particularly college-educated workers, and younger cohorts exposed during school years attained higher education. Notably, these spillovers occurred despite extensive secrecy around early nuclear research, suggesting that the benefits of public R&D co-location persist even under restricted information regimes.
