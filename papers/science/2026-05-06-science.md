# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-06

## 1. Slaying the Undead: How Long Does It Take to Kill Zombie Papers? [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Marc Joëts and Valérie Mignon
**Date:** March 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 2, Article 105401)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733325002306
**Abstract:** This paper investigates the persistence of zombie papers—flawed publications that have been retracted or are destined for retraction but continue to influence academic discourse through ongoing citations. Analyzing 25,480 retracted papers published over the 1923–2023 period, the authors combine survival analysis with a novel Zombie Population Decay Dynamics (ZPDD) model to identify the determinants of retraction speed and zombie persistence. Papers tainted by serious misconduct, such as data fabrication or falsification, show significantly longer delays to retraction than those retracted for honest errors, and geographic disparities across regions and disciplines further exacerbate these inefficiencies. Among a portfolio of editorial and institutional interventions assessed counterfactually, data transparency requirements emerge as the single most impactful lever for curtailing zombie persistence, followed by enhanced plagiarism detection and reproducibility measures such as pre-registration and replication studies. A well-balanced combination of targeted editorial interventions can substantially accelerate retraction processes and limit the downstream damage that zombie papers inflict on cumulative scientific knowledge.

## 2. Do Synthesis Programs Facilitate Interdisciplinary Research? Evidence from the NSFC's Major Research Plan [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Qianqian Jin, Hongshu Chen, Xuefeng Wang, and Jia Liu
**Date:** March 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 2)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733325002082
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether synthesis programs—a distinctive funding instrument that bundles separate research grants around a common integrative scientific challenge—promote interdisciplinarity more effectively than equivalent financial support alone. Using the National Natural Science Foundation of China's Major Research Plan as the empirical setting and developing a dual-approach quantitative framework that measures both cross-disciplinary knowledge absorption (via references) and knowledge integration (via textual content analysis of publications), the authors find that synthesis programs promote wider absorption and more balanced integration of dissimilar knowledge domains relative to comparable standalone grants. The interdisciplinarity advantage is shaped more by the institutional breadth of the collaboration network than by team size, and it persists across major scientific fields. The findings suggest that coordinated funding architectures that couple financial support with a shared scientific mission generate interdisciplinary outputs that decentralized grant-making tends to under-produce.

## 3. Firm Data on AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ivan Yotzov, Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Philip Bunn, Steven J. Davis, Kevin M. Foster, Aaron Jalca, Brent H. Meyer, Paul Mizen, Michael A. Navarrete, Pawel Smietanka, Gregory Thwaites, and Ben Zhe Wang
**Date:** February 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34836; revised March 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836
**Abstract:** This paper surveys approximately 6,000 senior business executives at firms in the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, and Australia to develop new evidence on artificial intelligence adoption and its near-term effects on productivity, employment, and output. Across countries, 69% of firms actively use AI, with adoption rates higher at younger, more productive, and higher-paying firms; firms with older boards of directors and older founding dates are less likely to have adopted AI. Despite widespread adoption, executives report minimal AI impact on their own firms over the past three years: nine in ten report no impact on either employment or productivity. Forward-looking expectations diverge sharply from this retrospective assessment: executives predict AI will boost firm-level productivity by an average of 1.4%, raise output 0.8%, and cut employment 0.7% over the next three years, while employees at the same firms anticipate a small employment increase of 0.5% over the same horizon. The paper documents this expectations gap across multiple specifications, examines heterogeneity by country, sector, and firm characteristics, and provides a cross-national baseline dataset for tracking AI's realized economic impacts as adoption continues to deepen.

## 4. Still Waters, Rapid Currents: Early Labor Market Transformation under Generative AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard
**Date:** May 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 33777; revised March 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777
**Abstract:** This paper examines the early labor-market effects of AI chatbot adoption using Danish matched employer–employee administrative data linked to firm-level AI adoption surveys. The findings reveal a striking paradox: most employers in occupations with high chatbot exposure have now adopted such tools, yet earnings and hours worked in these occupations show precise null effects, ruling out earnings changes larger than 2% over the 2021–2023 period. Rather than surfacing in earnings or hours, the labor-market transformation appears in task content: firms adopting chatbots restructured job responsibilities to include content generation, AI oversight, and integration tasks, and workers in adopting firms reallocated toward higher-paying task bundles even without aggregate compensation gains. The paper documents that 85% of chatbot users reallocate the time savings to other job tasks rather than reducing hours. Comparing results for AI chatbots with survey-based measures of traditional pre-generative AI reveals substantially larger chatbot adoption rates, consistent with the distinctively rapid diffusion of generative AI tools. The findings support a model in which technological change reshapes the organization of work well before it surfaces in standard labor-market aggregates such as wages and employment.

## 5. AI and Worker Well-Being: Evidence from a Nationally Representative Study [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Alex Bryson, Antti Kauhanen, and Petri Rouvinen
**Date:** April 7, 2026 (ETLA Working Paper No. 137; IZA Discussion Paper No. 18540)
**Source:** https://www.etla.fi/en/publications/working-papers-en/ai-and-worker-well-being-evidence-from-a-nationally-representative-study/
**Abstract:** Using data from Statistics Finland's Quality of Work Life Survey spanning 2018–2023, this paper provides nationally representative evidence on the relationship between artificial intelligence use and worker well-being at the individual level. Contrary to existing studies that document either positive or inverted-U-shaped associations between AI use and job satisfaction in other countries, the authors find no systematic association between AI use intensity and job satisfaction among Finnish wage earners. However, workers who are directly and intensively engaged with AI in their daily tasks report elevated work engagement, particularly those for whom AI constitutes a core element of job performance. Technology-replacement fears have remained stable despite the rapid diffusion of generative AI and do not predict subsequent labor-market transitions. The authors attribute these null effects to Finland's high-trust institutional environment and robust social safety net, which appear to moderate the disruptive psychological and economic shocks typically associated with rapid technological change. The paper contributes nationally representative, population-level evidence to a literature dominated by firm- and platform-level studies, and highlights the importance of country context for understanding workers' AI-related well-being outcomes.
