# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-07

## 1. How Artificial Intelligence Shapes Science: Evidence from AlphaFold [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ryan R. Hill and Carolyn Stein
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35143)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35143
**Abstract:** This paper studies how a frontier AI model affects the direction and pace of scientific discovery by examining the release of the AlphaFold2 protein-structure prediction algorithm in July 2021. When researchers gained access to hundreds of thousands of AI-predicted structures virtually overnight, experimental structure-determination rates remained unchanged; instead, scientists used predicted structures to complement rather than substitute for experimental work. Using a difference-in-differences design that compares proteins that received AlphaFold2 predictions to structurally similar proteins that did not, the authors find that basic research on proteins that had no prior structure information increased by 15 to 40% relative to previously-studied proteins, indicating that the tool redirected scientific attention toward understudied areas of the proteome. Despite this reorientation of basic research effort, the authors detect no evidence that more applied, early-stage drug development has yet targeted these newly accessible proteins, suggesting that the translation from basic AI-enabled discovery to downstream application operates on a longer horizon. The findings contribute to the economics-of-science literature on how general-purpose scientific tools alter the allocation of research effort and the direction of knowledge accumulation.

## 2. Forecasting the Economic Effects of AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ezra Karger, Otto Kuusela, Jason Abaluck, Kevin A. Bryan, Basil Halperin, Todd R. Jones, Connacher Murphy, Philip Trammell, Matt Reynolds, Dan Mayland, Ria Viswanathan, Ananaya Mittal, Rebecca Ceppas de Castro, Josh Rosenberg, and Philip Tetlock
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35046)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35046
**Abstract:** This paper elicits and analyzes quantitative forecasts about AI's economic effects from five groups—academic economists, AI company employees, AI policy researchers, expert forecasters, and the general public—using a structured survey instrument designed to decompose disagreement into beliefs about AI capability trajectories versus beliefs about the economic consequences of capability advances. Respondents across groups anticipated substantial AI capability advances by 2030 and baseline annual GDP growth of around 2.5%. Under a rapid AI progress scenario, expert forecasters projected annualized GDP growth rising to approximately 4% and the labor force participation rate declining from its current level of 62% to 55% by 2050, with roughly half of that decline—approximately 10 million workers—attributed directly to AI. The study finds that disagreement across groups stems primarily from differing views on the economic consequences of highly capable AI rather than from divergent predictions about the pace of technological advance itself. Policy preferences varied substantially: expert groups favored targeted programs such as worker retraining and education subsidies, while the general public additionally supported broader redistribution measures including job guarantees and universal basic income. The paper provides a systematic baseline for tracking how expert and public beliefs about AI's macroeconomic effects evolve as the technology matures.

## 3. Attention (And Money) Is All You Need: Why Universities Are Struggling to Keep AI Talent [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ufuk Akcigit, Craig A. Chikis, Emin Dinlersoz, and Nathan Goldschlag
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34964)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34964
**Abstract:** This paper studies the allocation of AI research talent between academia and industry using a novel longitudinal dataset that links academic publication records to U.S. Census Bureau employment and earnings data for approximately 42,000 AI scientists observed over two decades. The authors document a dramatic and accelerating divergence in compensation between academic and industry AI researchers: the top 1% of publishing industry scientists now earn $1.5 million more annually than observationally comparable academics, a fivefold increase relative to 2001. Industry increasingly recruits younger and internationally trained researchers, while academic institutions exhibit greater improvement in gender diversity. Researchers who transition to industry substantially reduce their academic publication output but significantly increase patent filings, consistent with a reallocation from open, reproducible scientific knowledge production toward proprietary innovation. The paper estimates transition hazard models and finds that both monetary incentives and non-pecuniary factors—including publication attention, citation impact, and access to large-scale computational resources—predict sectoral mobility. The findings raise questions about the long-run social returns to publicly funded AI research training when a large and growing share of trained talent migrates to private-sector proprietary research, and contribute to the science-of-science literature on university-industry relations in high-technology labor markets.

## 4. Knowledge Spillovers and Local Outcomes: An Existence Proof from the Establishment of the National Labs [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Susan Helper, Resem Makan, and Daniel W. Shoag
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35011)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35011
**Abstract:** This paper estimates the local economic effects of public research institutions by exploiting the placement of U.S. National Laboratories during the 1940s and 1950s as a natural experiment. Because laboratory locations were determined primarily by national security considerations and political factors rather than by pre-existing local innovation capacity, the authors use archival records to identify runner-up candidate sites as a comparison group, constructing a plausible counterfactual for the counties that ultimately received laboratories. Difference-in-differences estimates using newly digitized county-level economic data spanning 1936 to 1970 document significant and persistent positive effects on retail sales and household income. Census microdata from 1940 to 1950 reveal wage gains for incumbent residents, with particularly pronounced effects for college-educated workers. Non-lab inventors in laboratory counties substantially increased their patenting activity, with their research shifting toward laboratory technology classes and with growing citation linkages to lab patents, providing direct evidence of localized knowledge spillovers. Younger cohorts exposed to laboratory establishment during their schooling years attained significantly higher educational attainment, suggesting human capital formation as a spillover channel. Notably, these substantial economic and knowledge spillovers occurred despite extensive secrecy restrictions on early nuclear research, indicating that proximity to public research institutions generates meaningful local advantages even when information diffusion through formal channels is severely constrained.

## 5. Artificial Intelligence, Productivity, and the Workforce: Evidence from Corporate Executives [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Salomé Baslandze, Zachary Edwards, John Graham, Ty McClure, Brent H. Meyer, Michael Sparks, Sonya R. Waddell, and Daniel Weitz
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34984)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34984
**Abstract:** This paper uses a new survey of approximately 750 corporate executives to measure AI's effects on firm-level productivity, output, and employment, with particular attention to the gap between executives' perceptions and the implied economic magnitudes. Across sectors, over half of surveyed firms have already invested substantially in AI, with adoption rates higher among larger and more productive organizations. Labor productivity gains from AI adoption are positive and sector-varying, with the largest reported effects concentrated in high-skill services and finance; the authors' implied measures suggest these gains reflect improvements in total factor productivity linked to innovation and demand channels rather than simple capital deepening. A striking finding is that executives' subjective productivity assessments exceed the implied quantitative measures, which the authors attribute to delayed revenue recognition and organizational adjustment lags. On employment, the paper finds little evidence of near-term aggregate job losses: while larger firms anticipate modest workforce reductions, smaller firms expect slight employment growth, and the net aggregate effect is close to zero in the short run. The paper also documents compositional changes in labor demand, with declining demand for routine clerical tasks and rising demand for skilled technical and managerial roles. These results are consistent with a task-based model in which AI substitutes for specific task bundles while complementing others, producing within-firm labor-demand reallocation rather than outright workforce contraction.
