# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-08

## 1. Shelter in Scholarship: Evidence from a Global Survey of Hosts for Displaced Ukrainian Scientists [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Michael E. Rose, Katarína Juríková, Marina Pelepets, Olga Slivko, and Julia Yereshko
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 3, Article 105425)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105425
**Abstract:** This paper examines the global scientific community's response to the displacement of Ukrainian researchers following Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022, using a systematic survey of support opportunities registered in the #ScienceForUkraine database. The authors contacted hosts offering 2,416 registered support opportunities and obtained a 22.3% response rate. Among respondents, 56.9% of support offers received at least one eligible application and 47.4% successfully hosted at least one Ukrainian scientist. Analysis of the determinants of offer attractiveness reveals that scholarships and fellowships attracted substantially more applicants than academic positions, joint funding applications, or offers of access to laboratory resources and equipment. Offers in humanities and social sciences attracted greater demand relative to their supply than offers in STEM fields. Host institutions cited solidarity with Ukrainian colleagues and the availability of existing funding as the primary motivations for registering support. The paper documents substantial heterogeneity across host countries, institutions, and disciplines in both the design of support offers and the rate of successful placement, and identifies features of program design—particularly flexible start dates, remote-work options, and multilingual communication—that predict higher uptake. The findings contribute to the economics-of-science literature on scientist labor markets under political disruption, and provide actionable evidence for institutions designing emergency academic mobility programs.

## 2. Firm Training, Automation, and Wages: International Worker-Level Evidence [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Oliver Falck, Yuchen Guo, Christina Langer, Valentin Lindlacher, and Simon Wiederhold
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 3, Article 105424)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105424
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether employer-sponsored training protects workers against automation risk, addressing a widely held policy belief that has lacked rigorous empirical support. Using harmonized data on over 90,000 workers across 37 industrialized countries, the authors construct individual-level automation risk scores based on the task content of each worker's job rather than occupational averages, thereby capturing the substantial within-occupation heterogeneity that occupation-level automation measures obscure. Entropy-balancing methods combined with within-occupation and within-industry variation are used to identify the causal effect of firm-provided training on automation exposure. The results show that firm training reduces workers' individual automation risk by 3.8 percentage points, equivalent to 8% of the sample mean automation risk level, and that this reduction accounts for approximately 15% of the observed wage premium associated with training participation. Training also raises workers' digital skill scores, suggesting skill upgrading as a proximate mechanism. The protective effect of training against automation risk is remarkably robust: it holds across 36 of the 37 countries in the sample and does not vary systematically with workers' gender, age group, or educational attainment. The findings provide cross-national causal evidence in support of training-based policies as a tool for managing labor-market transitions induced by automation and AI adoption.

## 3. Abstract Patents and Startup Funding: Evidence from Alice v. CLS Bank [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Josh Feng and Peyton Williams
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 4, Article 105429)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105429
**Abstract:** This paper uses the Supreme Court's 2014 Alice v. CLS Bank decision as a natural experiment to identify the effects of abstract patent invalidation on venture capital financing of early-stage technology firms. Alice substantially reduced the patentability of software and business-method inventions by applying a stricter "abstract idea" test, leading to a large decrease in the number of patents granted in Alice-affected technology classes. Despite this contraction in patenting, the authors find that total venture capital funding flowing into Alice-affected industries either held steady or increased in the years following the decision, indicating that startup financing in these sectors is not patent-dependent. Examining patent quality, the authors document that newly granted patents in affected areas are approximately 60% less likely to be subsequently purchased by patent assertion entities (PAEs), consistent with Alice having screened out the low-quality "troll-friendly" patents that the decision targeted. However, PAEs continue to acquire pre-Alice patents at undiminished rates, suggesting the ruling's scope is limited to prospective issuances. The paper contributes to the literature on intellectual property and innovation finance by demonstrating that startup ecosystems can remain robustly funded even under significant IP-protection rollbacks, with implications for the design of patent eligibility standards in high-technology sectors.

## 4. From Evidence to Impact: The Use of Scientific Research in Policy Documents [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Andreas P. Distel, Christoph Grimpe, and Marion Poetz
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 3, Article 105426)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105426
**Abstract:** This paper examines how scientific research actually enters and shapes policy documents, using clinical practice guidelines for diabetes management as an empirical setting. The authors deploy natural language processing to compare the textual content of academic publications with that of policy guidelines, identifying three distinct citation modes: formal citations with substantive textual use, hidden citations (research that shapes guideline language without formal attribution), and token citations (papers cited formally but not reflected in guideline text). Using this framework to analyze a large corpus of clinical guidelines and the scientific literature they draw upon, the paper documents that both hidden and token citations are pervasive, implying that formal citation counts severely misrepresent the true relationship between science and policy. Examining the downstream correlates of citation type, the authors find that hidden citations are positively associated with expert-rated actual impact on patient outcomes and clinical practice, while token citations associate more strongly with bibliometric measures of scientific impact. These patterns imply that policy citations—commonly used as indicators of societal impact in research evaluation frameworks—capture primarily scientific visibility rather than real-world effectiveness, and that a large share of genuinely impactful research remains invisible to citation-based accounting. The findings challenge assumptions embedded in research evaluation systems and inform the design of more accurate indicators of the science-policy interface.

## 5. Labor Market Impacts of AI: A New Measure and Early Evidence [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Maxim Massenkoff and Peter McCrory
**Date:** March 5, 2026
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts
**Abstract:** This paper introduces "observed exposure," a new measure of occupational AI displacement risk that combines theoretical assessments of large language model capabilities with real-world data on how AI tools are actually being used. Existing exposure measures—which rely on expert judgments of which tasks AI could in principle perform—may overstate near-term displacement risk by ignoring the gap between technological capability and realized implementation. The authors address this by reweighting task-level exposure scores from Eloundou et al. using occupation-specific usage rates derived from Claude interaction logs, thereby constructing a measure that reflects both capability and adoption. The resulting index is then validated against time-series data from the Current Population Survey and Bureau of Labor Statistics employment projections spanning the period through early 2026. The main finding is that there is no systematic increase in unemployment among workers in the most AI-exposed occupations, ruling out widespread displacement to date. However, the analysis detects tentative evidence that new hiring into AI-exposed professions has slowed among workers aged 22–25, suggesting that the labor-market effects of AI may be operating through reduced entry rather than outright job loss. The paper demonstrates the value of grounding exposure measures in observed usage patterns rather than capabilities alone, and provides an updatable framework for monitoring AI's labor-market effects as both capabilities and adoption continue to evolve.
