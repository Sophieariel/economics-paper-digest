# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-09

## 1. The Microstructure of AI Diffusion: Evidence from Firms, Business Functions, and Worker Tasks [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Kathryn Bonney, Cory L. Breaux, Emin Dinlersoz, Lucia S. Foster, John C. Haltiwanger, and Aditya A. Pande
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35141
**Abstract:** This paper examines how artificial intelligence is actually deployed within firms across the U.S. economy, using new data from the 2026 AI supplement to the Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey covering the period November 2025–January 2026. The study documents adoption patterns at three levels of organizational granularity: firm, business function, and individual worker task. Key findings include: 18% of firms used AI in at least one business function (rising to 32% on an employment-weighted basis), with adoption rates of 50–60% (60–70%, employment-weighted) among the very largest firms. Among adopters, usage remains concentrated, with 57% employing AI in three or fewer functions; the most common applications are Sales and Marketing (52% of adopting firms), Strategy (45%), and Information Technology (41%). The large majority of adopting firms (66%) deploy AI primarily for task enhancement rather than job elimination, though a positive correlation is observed between firm performance metrics and broader functional AI integration. Functional deployment depth and operational investment in AI are associated with lower employment levels, suggesting that deeper integration—beyond early adoption—may carry larger labor-market implications than the initial adoption decision itself. The paper provides the first systematic evidence on AI diffusion at the micro-level using official U.S. Census data, and documents substantial heterogeneity across firm size, sector, and deployment depth that aggregate adoption statistics obscure.

## 2. Early AI Adoption and Firm Productivity Growth in a Middle-Income Economy [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Juan Duran-Vanegas
**Date:** April 3, 2026
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6515438
**Abstract:** This paper studies the effect of early artificial intelligence adoption on firm-level labor productivity growth using Colombia as an empirical laboratory, providing some of the first causal evidence on AI productivity effects in a middle-income economy where institutional context and technology-diffusion patterns differ substantially from the high-income settings studied in prior work. The analysis combines firm-level data on AI use from the 2019 Colombian Enterprise ICT Survey with longitudinal administrative manufacturing data, and estimates productivity growth differentials between AI adopters and non-adopters using entropy balancing to control for pre-adoption differences in firm characteristics and productivity trajectories. The findings indicate that AI adoption is associated with a 16 percent cumulative increase in labor productivity over the 2016–2019 period, equivalent to roughly 5 percent annualized growth. These differentials are driven by higher sales and value added rather than reductions in costs or employment, are similar in magnitude for firms developing AI in-house and those outsourcing its development, and are larger for firms with higher preexisting technical capabilities. The analysis points to organizational restructuring—rather than cost-cutting or headcount reduction—as the primary margin of adjustment through which AI raises productivity. The results suggest that early AI adoption confers substantial and durable productivity advantages even in developing-country manufacturing contexts, and that complementary technical capabilities within the firm are an important precondition for realizing these gains.

## 3. Creation, validation, obsolescence: observed evidence of AI-driven labor market displacement, 2020–2025 [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Nassim Dehouche
**Date:** May 7, 2026
**Source:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-dynamics/articles/10.3389/fhumd.2026.1815037/full
**Abstract:** This systematic review synthesizes the empirical evidence on artificial-intelligence-driven labor-market changes accumulated over the post-GPT-3 and ChatGPT deployment period, 2020–2025. The authors screened 1,847 records and analyzed 94 studies qualitatively and 42 quantitatively. The synthesis documents: (i) a 14–41 percent reduction in job postings for entry- and mid-level software development and content-creation roles in high-income economies; (ii) a 15–22 percent wage premium for workers demonstrating demonstrable AI-augmentation capabilities; and (iii) sector-specific divergence, with infrastructure and cybersecurity roles expanding while software development positions contract. Online labor-market data show a 2–21 percent reduction in posting volumes for automatable creative tasks following ChatGPT's November 2022 release. The evidence documents wage polarization, credential erosion, and geographic unevenness concentrated among routine cognitive tasks and junior-level roles. Developing economies dependent on cognitive-task outsourcing face disproportionate disruption through both direct displacement and indirect demand effects. The authors characterize the overall pattern as a 'creation–validation–obsolescence' cycle in which AI tools initially augment workers before progressively displacing the validation and execution steps their work previously required. Significant data gaps persist regarding longer-run worker outcomes, re-employment trajectories, and non-Anglophone labor markets. Published in Frontiers in Human Dynamics (DOI: 10.3389/fhumd.2026.1815037).

## 4. Political, economic and research disintegration: The impact of geopolitical uncertainty on innovation and cross-border R&D collaborations [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Mustapha Douch, Enrico Vanino, and Ismail Ç. Akmak
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0048733326000624
**Abstract:** This paper uses the 2016 Brexit referendum as an exogenous, quasi-experimental shock to the environment of international scientific and economic cooperation to estimate the causal effect of geopolitical policy uncertainty on innovation and cross-border research collaboration. The analysis exploits PATSTAT patent data at the applicant-quarter level for the period 2013–2019 and identifies causal effects using a panel two-way fixed effects (TWFE) difference-in-differences design. The results reveal significant and persistent disruption to cross-border research collaborations between UK and EU-based organizations following the referendum: the share of EU-based inventors on patents filed by UK applicants falls markedly, and inventor-level data confirm a clear compositional shift in the knowledge networks of UK innovators—with international co-authorship links severed and replaced by more domestic-facing research networks. These disruptions translate into lower patenting quantities and, on quality-adjusted measures, lower-quality innovation output. The effects are concentrated in knowledge-intensive sectors where cross-border collaboration was most prevalent prior to the referendum, consistent with the interpretation that co-inventor networks and cross-border knowledge access function as important complements to domestic R&D capacity rather than substitutes. The paper contributes to the economics-of-science literature on knowledge spillovers and university-industry relations by demonstrating that geopolitical frictions that sever international research networks impose measurable and economically significant costs on innovation productivity.

## 5. When Does Automating AI Research Produce Explosive Growth? Feedback Loops in Innovation Networks [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Tom Davidson, Basil Halperin, Thomas Houlden, and Anton Korinek
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35155
**Abstract:** AI labs are increasingly using AI itself to accelerate AI research, creating a feedback loop that could lead to an intelligence explosion. This paper examines when such recursive automation produces superexponential or 'explosive' aggregate growth. The authors develop a general semi-endogenous growth model incorporating an innovation network in which research and automation advances in one sector raise the productivity of research in other sectors. They derive clean analytical conditions under which growth becomes superexponential, identifying two reinforcing mechanisms: a technological feedback loop across research sectors and an economic feedback loop in which higher output finances further research. Growth becomes explosive when the combined strength of these feedback loops overcomes diminishing returns to ideas. A simulation calibrated to observed trends in AI capability progress finds that fully automating software research, combined with modest (5%) automation in other research sectors, could generate a growth singularity within six years. The analysis further shows that resource bottlenecks are unlikely to prevent explosive growth if task-level automation advances sufficiently rapidly. The paper contributes to the economics-of-science literature on how AI-assisted research production—specifically the endogenization of the research production function itself—affects the long-run rate of innovation, and provides a tractable framework for evaluating policy responses to AI-driven acceleration of the research process.
