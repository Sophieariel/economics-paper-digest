# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-11

## 1. Does Peer Review Penalize Scientific Risk Taking? Evidence from NIH Grant Renewals [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay and Wesley H. Greenblatt
**Date:** February 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 33495); revised March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33495
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether high-risk scientific projects face systematic obstacles in securing funding renewal. Analyzing over 100,000 R01 grants from the National Institutes of Health spanning 1980–2015, the authors employ four distinct proxies for scientific risk-taking: extreme tail outcomes (unusually high or low subsequent citation impact), disruptiveness (the degree to which a grant's outputs destabilize prior literature, measured via a disruption index), pivoting (departure from an investigator's prior research trajectory), and distinctiveness (standing out from the crowd within one's subfield). After controlling for investigator credentials, grant characteristics, and institutional fixed effects, risky grants are renewed at markedly lower rates than less risky ones across all four measures. The risk penalty is not uniform: it is amplified for grants operating in nascent or frontier research areas and for early-career investigators, who face both higher baseline uncertainty and reduced reputational capital to buffer against negative evaluations. The results survive numerous robustness checks including alternative operationalizations of risk and models that allow for heterogeneous effects across NIH institutes and disease areas. The findings imply that the peer-review renewal process, as currently structured, may systematically select against exploratory, high-variance science in favor of incremental, predictable work—with potential long-run consequences for the diversity and originality of the knowledge frontier.

## 2. Political, Economic and Research Disintegration: The Impact of Geopolitical Uncertainty on Cross-Border R&D Collaborations and Innovation [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Enrico Vanino, Mustapha Douch, and Ismail Çakmak
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 4, Article 105451)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105451
**Abstract:** This paper estimates the causal effect of geopolitical and economic uncertainty on international R&D collaboration and patenting activity, using the United Kingdom's exit from the European Union as a quasi-natural experiment. The authors exploit the Brexit referendum of June 2016 as a plausibly exogenous shock to the policy environment and apply a difference-in-differences design that compares the collaborative research behavior of UK organizations with EU-based inventors to that of comparable organizations with non-EU partners before and after the referendum. The empirical results reveal a significant and persistent disruption in cross-border research collaborations between UK and EU inventors following the referendum, with EU-based co-inventors substantially reducing joint research activity with UK counterparts in the years leading up to and following formal withdrawal. The analysis further documents reductions in both the quantity and quality of patents produced by UK organizations, with shifts in the technological composition of patented inventions consistent with a withdrawal from international knowledge networks. The findings hold across alternative specifications, placebo tests using non-EU partner countries, and subgroup analyses by technology class and firm size. The paper contributes to the economics-of-science literature on the determinants of knowledge production and diffusion, providing causal evidence that geopolitical fragmentation carries measurable costs for the generation and international exchange of scientific and technological knowledge.

## 3. Artificial Intelligence and the Labor Market [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Menaka Hampole, Dimitris Papanikolaou, Lawrence D.W. Schmidt, and Bryan Seegmiller
**Date:** February 2025 (NBER Working Paper No. 33509); revised September 2025
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33509
**Abstract:** This paper uses natural language processing to construct novel measures of task-level exposure to artificial intelligence and machine learning, covering the period 2010–2023 and capturing variation across firms, occupations, and time. The authors document that tasks with higher AI exposure subsequently experience reduced labor demand, while occupation-level exposure averages depress within-firm labor demand and the concentration of AI exposure across tasks partially offsets these losses by enabling workers to reallocate effort toward less-exposed tasks. To distinguish causal effects from selection, the authors instrument for occupation-level AI adoption using historical university hiring networks, arguing that institutions with stronger ties to AI-specialized departments introduced AI tools to their workforces earlier and more extensively for reasons unrelated to pre-existing labor demand. Causal estimates confirm the model's predictions: mean exposure reduces labor demand while task-concentration partially offsets this effect through within-firm reallocation. Notably, despite substantial task-level substitution, overall employment effects are modest—reduced demand in AI-exposed occupations is largely offset by productivity-driven increases in labor demand at firms that adopt AI, consistent with a task-based model in which AI's within-firm displacement is counterbalanced by expansion at the firm level. The paper further shows that AI exposure is highest for middle-to-upper-wage occupations, a pattern that contrasts with the routine-biased technological change of earlier automation waves.

## 4. AI, Output, and Employment [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Andrew C. Johnston and Christos Makridis
**Date:** July 2025 (SSRN Working Paper, Abstract ID: 5375017)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5375017
**Abstract:** This paper provides causal estimates of generative AI's impact on output, employment, and wages using a difference-in-differences design that exploits the workplace rollout of AI tools beginning in 2021, combined with cross-sector variation in occupational AI exposure. The authors distinguish between two theoretically motivated types of exposure: displacing exposure, in which AI can perform tasks independently and thereby substitute for human labor, and augmenting exposure, in which AI requires human judgment or collaboration to be effective. Using U.S. administrative data aggregated to the industry level, they find divergent effects by exposure type: sectors with predominantly augmenting AI exposure experience employment gains of approximately 4% and positive wage growth, while sectors with predominantly displacing exposure see no significant employment effect and reductions in total wage bills. The results imply that AI's labor market impact is not uniformly positive or negative, but depends critically on the mode of human–AI interaction embedded in specific occupational tasks. The authors interpret these findings through a task-based framework in which human complementarity is the key margin governing whether AI-driven productivity gains translate into expanded employment or labor cost reductions, and discuss implications for workforce policy and the design of AI deployment strategies.

## 5. The Labor Market Effects of Generative Artificial Intelligence [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Jonathan Hartley, Filip Jolevski, Vitor Melo, and Brendan Moore
**Date:** January 2026 (SSRN Working Paper, Abstract ID: 5136877)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5136877
**Abstract:** This paper develops a new survey instrument to measure generative AI adoption and labor market outcomes across the U.S. workforce, linking occupation-level survey responses to administrative data on earnings, job openings, and employment. The survey tracks large language model adoption from late 2023 through December 2025 and documents rapid diffusion: the share of U.S. workers using LLMs grew from 30.1% in December 2024 to 38.3% in December 2025, with adoption concentrated among younger, college-educated, and higher-earning employees, and particularly prevalent in customer service, marketing, and information technology. Despite this rapid uptake, the paper finds small but statistically significant positive wage effects across AI-exposed occupations, alongside no measurable change in job openings or aggregate employment. These near-zero employment effects hold even among workers who report substantial productivity gains from AI tool use, suggesting that firms are absorbing AI-enabled productivity increases through expanded output rather than workforce reduction. The paper further examines heterogeneity by worker age, education, and occupation and finds that wage gains accrue disproportionately to workers already near the top of the AI-adoption distribution, raising questions about distributional consequences as adoption broadens. The results suggest that generative AI is currently functioning primarily as a labor productivity enhancer rather than a substitute, with aggregate labor demand adjustment—if it materializes—likely to emerge on a longer horizon.
