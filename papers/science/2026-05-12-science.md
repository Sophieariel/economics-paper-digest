# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-12

## 1. Reproducibility and Robustness of Economics and Political Science Research [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Abel Brodeur, Derek Mikola, Nikolai Cook, Lenka Fiala, Thomas Brailey, Ryan Briggs, and more than 100 additional co-authors across participating institutions
**Date:** April 2026 (Nature, Vol. 652, Issue 8108, pp. 151–156)
**Source:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10251-x
**Abstract:** This large-scale collaborative study assesses the computational reproducibility and empirical robustness of published findings in economics and political science by recruiting independent research teams to re-analyze 110 articles from leading journals that had adopted mandatory data and code sharing policies. On the reproducibility dimension, more than 85% of published claims were computationally reproducible, meaning that running the original code on the original data returned the reported estimates. On the robustness dimension, 72% of originally statistically significant estimates remained statistically significant and pointed in the same direction after independent robustness checks, and the median reproduced effect size was approximately 99% of the originally published value—indicating minimal average attenuation. Six independent research teams conducted the robustness assessments, and the study finds that experienced researchers tended to identify somewhat lower robustness levels than less experienced ones, though all groups found broadly consistent results. Robustness showed no systematic correlation with author characteristics, journal prestige, or data type. The paper provides the most comprehensive empirical accounting to date of the reliability of published quantitative social science and contributes to the science-of-science literature on peer review, publication standards, and the self-corrective function of replication in economics.

## 2. Growth is Getting Harder to Find, Not Ideas [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Teresa C. Fort, Nathan Goldschlag, Jack Liang, Peter K. Schott, and Nikolas Zolas
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35182)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35182
**Abstract:** This paper revisits the influential hypothesis—associated with Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen, and Webb (2020)—that the economy is running out of ideas, as evidenced by rising R&D costs per unit of productivity growth. Using a 45-year panel of U.S. firm-level patent filings linked to financial and productivity data, the authors document that average patents per unit of R&D input are actually increasing rather than declining once research inputs are properly measured, and they find no systematic evidence of a secular decline in patenting after conditioning on research spending. Firms continue to exhibit a sustained relationship between patenting and productivity growth across the four decades of the panel. The paper's central finding challenges the "falling ideas productivity" narrative: the bottleneck is not in generating innovations but in translating them into broad-based economic growth. Average firm growth rates, after controlling for patent growth, have declined over time—suggesting that the transmission mechanism connecting innovation to aggregate output expansion, rather than the innovation process itself, is the locus of the productivity slowdown. These results carry direct implications for R&D policy and for growth accounting models that infer innovation productivity from output data, and contribute to the economics-of-science literature on the returns to research and the relationship between science, patenting, and economic performance.

## 3. Early AI Adoption and Firm Productivity Growth in a Middle-Income Economy [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Juan Duran-Vanegas
**Date:** April 3, 2026 (SSRN Working Paper No. 6515438)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6515438
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether early adoption of artificial intelligence generates measurable firm-level productivity gains in a middle-income economy by combining data on AI use from the 2019 Colombian Enterprise ICT Survey with longitudinal manufacturing plant data spanning 2016–2019. To account for the non-random selection of AI adopters, the author applies entropy balancing to match adopters and non-adopters on pre-adoption characteristics and productivity trajectories, then estimates labor productivity growth differentials using a difference-in-differences design. AI adoption is associated with a 16% cumulative increase in labor productivity over the three-year sample period, equivalent to roughly 5 percentage points of annualized growth—a magnitude comparable to estimates from high-income country studies despite Colombia's lower baseline level of technology adoption. The productivity differential is driven predominantly by higher revenues and value added rather than by cost reductions or workforce downsizing, and the effects are similar across firms that develop AI capabilities in-house and those that procure them from external vendors. Effects are larger and more precisely estimated among firms with higher preexisting technical capabilities, consistent with a complementarity between AI and prior absorptive capacity. The paper also provides suggestive evidence that changes in organizational structure—in particular, shifts toward more team-based task allocation—constitute an important adjustment margin through which AI adoption reshapes production. The results offer early empirical evidence on AI's productive potential outside the advanced economies that dominate existing studies.

## 4. Automation, Trade and Multinational Activity: Micro Evidence from Spain [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Katherine Stapleton and Michael Webb
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 5; available online 2026)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0048733326000636
**Abstract:** This paper examines how robot adoption by domestic manufacturers affects their subsequent trade patterns and multinational activity, testing the "reshoring" hypothesis—the widely discussed prediction that automation in high-income countries will reduce demand for cheaper labor in low-income trading partners by substituting domestic robots for imported goods and offshore affiliates. Using a rich panel of Spanish manufacturing firms observed from 1990 to 2016, the authors employ a shift-share instrumental variable strategy that combines baseline occupational employment shares with time-varying shocks derived from the textual content of robotics patents—identifying which occupational tasks specific robotic inventions were designed to perform—to isolate the causal effect of robot adoption. Contrary to the reshoring prediction, robot adoption is found to increase rather than reduce firms' trade exposure to lower-income countries: automating firms become significantly more likely to begin importing from, and to open new affiliates in, lower-income partner countries following adoption. The mechanism is an output-expansion effect: automation raises firm productivity, which induces firms to scale up production overall, generating complementary demand for imported intermediate inputs and expanding the scale of foreign operations rather than contracting them. Robot adoption also increases firms' productivity and total employment, with no evidence that automation displaces domestic workers at the firm level. These findings challenge standard predictions about automation and reshoring, highlight the importance of separating substitution and scale effects of automation technology, and contribute to the economics of AI literature on how automation reshapes global production networks.

## 5. AI Adoption, Productivity and Employment: Evidence from European Firms [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Iñaki Aldasoro, Leonardo Gambacorta, Rozalia Pal, Debora Revoltella, Christoph Weiss, and Marcin Wolski
**Date:** 23 January 2026 (BIS Working Paper No. 1325; EIB Economics Working Paper 2026/02)
**Source:** https://www.bis.org/publ/work1325.htm
**Abstract:** This paper provides causal evidence on how artificial intelligence adoption affects productivity and employment in European firms using matched data from over 12,000 non-financial companies across the European Union and the United States. The identification strategy instruments each EU firm's AI adoption decision with the rate of AI adoption among U.S. firms in the same industry and of comparable size, exploiting the U.S.–EU technology diffusion frontier as a source of exogenous variation. The instrumental-variables estimates indicate that AI adoption raises labor productivity by approximately 4%, a magnitude consistent with productivity-enhancing mechanisms operating over a three-year horizon. Decomposing the sources of the productivity gain, the paper finds that gains are driven predominantly by capital deepening—firms that adopt AI tend to accumulate complementary investments in software, hardware, and data infrastructure—rather than by reductions in workforce size. Employment levels in AI-adopting firms are not significantly different from those in non-adopting firms over the observed window, suggesting that the short-run labor-market effects of AI in Europe are primarily compositional rather than aggregate. The productivity premium from AI adoption is concentrated among medium and large enterprises, while small firms show attenuated benefits, a pattern consistent with high upfront fixed costs in AI implementation. AI-adopting firms also display higher wages, elevated innovation outputs, and greater integration in global value chains relative to non-adopters. The paper underscores that realizing AI's productivity potential depends critically on coordinated complementary investments in data infrastructure, enterprise software, and workforce training.
