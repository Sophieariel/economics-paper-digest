## 1. Attention (And Money) Is All You Need: Why Universities Are Struggling to Keep AI Talent [Economics of Science]

**Authors:** Ufuk Akcigit, Craig A. Chikis, Emin Dinlersoz, Nathan Goldschlag  
**Date:** March 2026  
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34964  
**Abstract:** The researchers analyzed 42,000 AI specialists over twenty years using linked publication and employment records. Key findings reveal that private companies increasingly recruit younger and international researchers, while academia sees greater gender diversity gains. Industry compensation for top researchers has surged dramatically — the top 1% of publishing industry scientists now earn $1.5 million more annually than comparable academics, a fivefold increase since 2001. This wage growth correlates with concentration at established corporations. When researchers transition to industry roles, their publication output decreases while patent generation increases, suggesting a shift from open science toward proprietary innovation.

## 2. Funding the U.S. Scientific Training Ecosystem: New Data, Methods, and Evidence [Economics of Science]

**Authors:** Dror Shvadron, Hansen Zhang, Lee Fleming, Daniel P. Gross  
**Date:** June 2025  
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33944  
**Abstract:** The researchers examined newly-collected data on U.S. STEM PhD graduates since 1950 to understand funding sources and training patterns. They found that the U.S. federal government is by far the largest source of financial and in-kind support for STEM PhD training in America. The study identifies which universities and fields receive government, industry, or philanthropic support and tracks PhD production in critical technology areas including AI, quantum information technology, and biotechnology. Using variation in government support across agencies and time, the authors provide evidence that increased government-funded PhD trainee positions correlate with proportional increases in PhD production. The research includes public datasets showing PhD graduates categorized by technology area and funding source.

## 3. Forecasting the Economic Effects of AI [Economics of AI]

**Authors:** Ezra Karger, Otto Kuusela, Jason Abaluck, Kevin A. Bryan, Basil Halperin, Todd R. Jones, Connacher Murphy, Philip Trammell, Matt Reynolds, Dan Mayland, Ria Viswanathan, Ananya Mittal, Rebecca Ceppas de Castro, Josh Rosenberg, Philip Tetlock  
**Date:** April 2026  
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35046  
**Abstract:** This research elicits economic forecasts from five distinct groups: academic economists, AI company employees, AI policy researchers, highly accurate forecasters, and members of the general public. Respondents across all groups anticipate substantial advances in AI capabilities by 2030 and project annual GDP growth of 2.5%, exceeding baseline government and private forecasts. Under a rapid AI progress scenario — where systems surpass human performance on numerous cognitive and physical tasks — experts predict GDP growth around 4% and labor force participation declining from 62% to 55% by 2050. The analysis reveals that expert disagreement stems primarily from differing views on highly capable AI's economic effects rather than disagreement about AI development speed. Policy preferences diverge significantly: experts favor targeted worker retraining programs, while the public supports both targeted measures and broader interventions including job guarantees and universal basic income.

## 4. Artificial Intelligence, Productivity, and the Workforce: Evidence from Corporate Executives [Economics of AI]

**Authors:** Salomé Baslandze, Zachary Edwards, John Graham, Ty McClure, Brent H. Meyer, Michael Sparks, Sonya R. Waddell, Daniel Weitz  
**Date:** March 2026  
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34984  
**Abstract:** The researchers surveyed approximately 750 corporate executives to examine how artificial intelligence affects productivity and employment. They find substantial heterogeneity in AI adoption across firms, with more than half having already invested. Productivity improvements are anticipated to increase in 2026, particularly in high-skill services and finance sectors. The gains stem from total factor productivity rather than capital expansion. Interestingly, executives perceive larger productivity improvements than measured data shows, suggesting delayed revenue recognition — a productivity paradox. Regarding employment, the study reveals little evidence of near-term aggregate employment declines due to AI, though larger companies anticipate workforce reductions while smaller firms expect modest expansion. The research identifies shifting labor demand toward technical roles and away from routine administrative positions.

## 5. Firm Data on AI [Economics of AI]

**Authors:** Ivan Yotzov, Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Philip Bunn, Steven J. Davis, Kevin M. Foster, Aaron Jalca, Brent H. Meyer, Paul Mizen, Michael A. Navarrete, Pawel Smietanka, Gregory Thwaites, Ben Zhe Wang  
**Date:** February 2026 (revised March 2026)  
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836  
**Abstract:** The researchers surveyed nearly 6,000 business executives across the US, UK, Germany, and Australia to examine AI adoption patterns and impacts. Key findings include: 69% of firms actively use AI, with higher adoption among younger and more productive companies. While over two-thirds of executives personally use AI, their average weekly usage is only 1.5 hours. Regarding effects over the past three years, nine-in-ten executives report no impact on employment or productivity. However, executives anticipate significant future changes, projecting AI will increase productivity by 1.4%, raise output 0.8%, and reduce employment 0.7% over the next three years. Notably, employees forecast a 0.5% employment increase, revealing a significant expectations gap between management and workers.
