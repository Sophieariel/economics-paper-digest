# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-14

## 1. Data Centers and Local Economies in the Age of AI: A Shift-Share Approach [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Fernando E. Alvarez, David Argente, Joyce Chow, and Diana Van Patten
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35194
**Abstract:** This paper examines how data center expansion affects local U.S. economies, assembling facility-level data on global data centers and mapping them to county-level economic indicators including employment, income, housing prices, and electricity prices. To address the endogeneity of data center location choices, the authors construct shift-share instruments based on proximity to fiber-optic infrastructure and historical college-graduate populations—factors that predict data center siting but are plausibly exogenous to local economic trajectories. The IV estimates show that data center growth generates positive effects on total employment, data-processing employment, construction employment, business establishments, house prices, and electricity prices across multiple time horizons. The analysis also documents gains in tax returns, adjusted gross income, and wages, though payroll responses are less stable across specifications. The paper provides the first causal evidence on the local economic consequences of AI-related physical infrastructure investment, demonstrating that data centers function as a meaningful driver of place-based economic activity while simultaneously exerting upward pressure on housing costs and local electricity prices—tradeoffs with direct relevance for the siting policies and public utility regulation that communities and regulators face as AI infrastructure buildout accelerates.

## 2. The Microstructure of AI Diffusion: Evidence from Firms, Business Functions, and Worker Tasks [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Kathryn Bonney, Cory L. Breaux, Emin Dinlersoz, Lucia S. Foster, John C. Haltiwanger, and Aditya A. Pande
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35141
**Abstract:** This paper examines AI adoption at three organizational levels—firms, business functions, and individual worker tasks—using data from the 2026 AI supplement to the Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS), a near-real-time panel of roughly 45,000 U.S. businesses. As of November 2025–January 2026, 18% of firms (32% on an employment-weighted basis) report using AI, with adoption expected to rise to 22% within six months. Adoption is highly concentrated: large firms and knowledge-intensive sectors lead, with 50–60% of very large firms in Information, Professional Services, and Finance reporting current AI use. Even among adopters, scope is limited—57% deploy AI in three or fewer business functions, with Sales/Marketing (52%), Strategy (45%), and IT (41%) as the most common domains. At the worker level, AI use appears in 23% of firms (41% employment-weighted), concentrated in writing, document analysis, and information-search tasks. The overwhelming majority of AI use (66% of adopters) is for task augmentation rather than task replacement; outright employment reductions remain rare (2%). Cross-sectionally, broader AI integration correlates with higher firm performance, though functional deployment and capital investment in AI operations associate with employment decline at the margin. The results provide a granular, nationally representative baseline for tracking how AI diffuses through the U.S. economy at the task and function level.

## 3. Does Peer Review Penalize Scientific Risk Taking? Evidence from NIH Grant Renewals [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay and Wesley H. Greenblatt
**Date:** February 2025 (revised March 4, 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33495
**Abstract:** This paper analyzes the determinants of renewal for more than 100,000 R01 grants from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) between 1980 and 2015, using four distinct proxies to measure the degree of scientific risk taking embedded in funded projects: (i) extreme tail outcomes in subsequent citation distributions; (ii) disruptiveness of the research, as captured by the CD index; (iii) the degree to which an investigator pivots from prior work; and (iv) the extent to which a grant "stands out from the crowd" relative to contemporaneous grants in the same field. After controlling carefully for investigator fixed effects, grant characteristics, institution quality, and time trends, the authors find that across all four proxies, risky grants are renewed at markedly lower rates than less risky ones. The risk penalty is amplified for grants in more novel research areas and for early-career investigators, patterns consistent with peer review panels preferring predictable, safe research over high-variance, potentially transformative projects. The magnitude of the estimated penalty is economically large and robust to a range of specification checks. These findings contribute to the science-of-science literature on peer review and funding allocation by providing some of the largest-scale empirical evidence to date that the NIH grant renewal process structurally disadvantages exploratory research, raising questions about whether the design of renewal review can be reformed to better balance scientific risk against expected return.

## 4. Randomized Experiments in the Science of Science: The Case of Peer Review and the Evaluation of New Knowledge [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Kevin J. Boudreau
**Date:** January 5, 2026
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6025114
**Abstract:** This paper synthesizes the growing body of randomized field experiments embedded in peer-review systems at academic journals, scientific conferences, and grant-funding agencies, asking what these interventions reveal about how peer review functions, where institutional reform is plausible, and which directions for future experimental research are most likely to be informative. The experiments surveyed cover reviewer assignment mechanisms, double-blind versus single-blind anonymization, conflict-of-interest disclosure requirements, review length and structure, evaluator diversity and domain expertise, and iterative evaluation formats. Across this evidence base, the paper documents a sharp empirical asymmetry: most system-level interventions reliably shift participation rates, timing, compliance, and other behavioral margins of the review process, while core evaluative judgments—the substantive assessment of scientific merit—respond primarily to who evaluates the work and to the depth of their domain knowledge rather than to changes in procedural rules. The analysis clarifies which aspects of peer review are empirically responsive to institutional and system-level intervention, and where further experimental research is most likely to yield actionable guidance. The paper identifies the evaluation of new and unfamiliar knowledge—where reviewer expertise is hardest to match and evaluative errors most consequential—as the frontier most in need of additional experimental investigation.

## 5. Early AI Adoption and Firm Productivity Growth in a Middle-Income Economy [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Juan David Duran Vanegas
**Date:** April 3, 2026
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6515438
**Abstract:** This paper examines the relationship between AI adoption and firm-level productivity growth in a middle-income economy, combining data on AI use from the 2019 Colombian Enterprise ICT Survey with longitudinal manufacturing microdata from the Annual Manufacturing Survey (EAM) for 2016–2019. To address selection into AI adoption, the author applies entropy balancing to construct a reweighted comparison group of non-adopters that closely matches adopters on pre-adoption observable characteristics and productivity trajectories. The main finding is that AI adoption is associated with a 16% cumulative increase in labor productivity over 2016–2019, equivalent to roughly 5% annualized growth. This productivity differential is driven primarily by higher sales and value added rather than by reductions in employment or production costs, and the estimated gains are similar for firms that develop AI in-house versus those that outsource development. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that productivity returns to AI adoption are larger for firms with higher preexisting technical capabilities, suggesting important complementarities between AI and organizational absorptive capacity. The results contribute to the empirical literature on AI and firm productivity by offering evidence from an emerging-market manufacturing context, where AI adoption trajectories, input markets, and complementary human and organizational capital differ substantially from those in advanced economies, and where the productivity consequences of general-purpose technology adoption remain poorly documented.
