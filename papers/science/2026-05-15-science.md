# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-15

## 1. How Artificial Intelligence Shapes Science: Evidence from AlphaFold [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ryan R. Hill, Carolyn Stein
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35143
**Abstract:** The authors investigate how AlphaFold2's release in July 2021 influenced scientific research in structural biology. Despite providing hundreds of thousands of AI-predicted structures virtually overnight, experimental structure determination rates remained largely unchanged. Instead, researchers leveraged predicted structures to enhance their experimental work. Key findings include: basic research on previously unstructured proteins increased by 15–40% compared to proteins with existing structural data, redirecting focus toward underexplored proteins. However, no evidence so far emerges of increased drug development targeting these newly studied proteins, though such activity may develop in the future. The research examines how frontier AI tools reshape scientific discovery patterns and research priorities across related fields.

## 2. AI Patents in the United States and China: Measurement, Organization, and Knowledge Flows [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Hanming Fang, Xian Gu, Hanyin Yan, Wu Zhu
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35022
**Abstract:** The researchers developed an advanced classifier to identify artificial intelligence patents, refining PatentSBERTa on manually labeled USPTO data. Their model achieved 97.0% precision, 91.3% recall, and a 94.0% F1 score, and successfully applied to Chinese patents. Analysis of U.S. patents (1976–2023) and Chinese patents (2010–2023) reveals rapid AI patenting expansion in both nations with converging subfield compositions. However, organizational structures diverge significantly: U.S. AI innovation concentrates among major private firms and established regions, while Chinese innovation distributes more broadly across universities and state enterprises. For publicly traded companies, AI patents demonstrate measurable market value in both countries. Citation patterns indicate ongoing technological interdependence rather than decoupling, with Chinese AI inventors relying more heavily on U.S. frontier knowledge than vice versa.

## 3. The Microstructure of AI Diffusion: Evidence from Firms, Business Functions, and Worker Tasks [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Kathryn Bonney, Cory L. Breaux, Emin Dinlersoz, Lucia S. Foster, John C. Haltiwanger, Aditya A. Pande
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35141
**Abstract:** This research examines AI adoption across three organizational levels using data from the 2026 AI supplement to the U.S. Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey. Key findings include: 18% of firms used AI in at least one function (32%, employment-weighted), with adoption expected to reach 22% within six months. Adoption concentrates in large firms and knowledge-intensive sectors, reaching 50–60% among very large firms. Among adopting firms, 57% use AI in three or fewer functions, most often Sales and Marketing (52%), Strategy (45%), and IT (41%). Worker-level use appears in 23% of firms for writing, document analysis, and information search. Most firms (66%) use AI for task augmentation, while employment reductions are rare (2%). Performance improves with broader AI integration, though functional deployment and operational investment correlate with employment declines.

## 4. Data Centers and Local Economies in the Age of AI: A Shift-Share Approach [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Fernando E. Alvarez, David Argente, Joyce Chow, Diana Van Patten
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35194
**Abstract:** The paper examines how data center expansion affects local U.S. economies. Using county-level data combined with facility-specific information on global data centers, the researchers employ shift-share instrumental variables to address siting decisions. Their findings indicate that data center growth generates positive effects on total employment, data-processing employment, construction employment, establishments, house prices, and electricity prices. The study also documents gains in tax returns, adjusted gross income, and wages. However, the authors note that data center development involves tradeoffs, potentially increasing pressure on electricity infrastructure and land use while creating local economic activity and investment opportunities.

## 5. Forecasting the Economic Effects of AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ezra Karger, Otto Kuusela, Jason Abaluck, Kevin A. Bryan, Basil Halperin, Todd R. Jones, Connacher Murphy, Philip Trammell, Matt Reynolds, Dan Mayland, Ria Viswanathan, Ananaya Mittal, Rebecca Ceppas de Castro, Josh Rosenberg, Philip Tetlock, et al.
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35046
**Abstract:** The researchers surveyed five distinct groups — academic economists, AI company employees, AI policy researchers, accurate forecasters, and the general public — regarding AI's economic impact on the United States. Respondents anticipated significant AI capability improvements by 2030 and projected annual GDP growth of 2.5%, surpassing baseline government and private forecasts. Under a "rapid" AI progress scenario involving systems exceeding human performance across cognitive and physical domains, the study found experts anticipating GDP growth reaching approximately 4% annually and labor force participation declining from 62% to 55% by 2050, with roughly half this decline — approximately 10 million jobs — attributed to AI displacement. The analysis reveals that disagreement among experts stems primarily from divergent views on highly capable AI's economic effects rather than differing assessments of AI advancement speed. Policy preferences diverged substantially: experts championed targeted worker retraining initiatives, while the general public endorsed both targeted programs and broader mechanisms including job guarantees and universal basic income.
