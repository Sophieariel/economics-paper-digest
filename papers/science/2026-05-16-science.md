# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-16

## 1. Growth is Getting Harder to Find, Not Ideas [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Teresa C. Fort, Nathan Goldschlag, Jack Liang, Peter K. Schott, and Nikolas Zolas
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35182)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35182
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether the secular slowdown in U.S. aggregate productivity growth reflects diminishing returns to innovative effort or factors unrelated to the innovative process itself. Using a 45-year panel of U.S. firm-level patent records linked to R&D expenditure and productivity data, the authors find that average patent output per unit of R&D input has increased over time rather than declined, and that there is a positive, significant, and fairly steady relationship between firm-level patent growth rates and labor productivity growth rates across the full sample period. These findings rule out declining innovative capacity as the primary driver of the productivity slowdown. However, the authors document that average firm growth, after conditioning on patent growth, declines substantially over the panel, implying that the forces depressing aggregate productivity are operating through channels that decouple firm growth from innovative output—including intensified product-market competition, weaker demand transmission, or increasing difficulties in commercializing new technologies—rather than through declining research productivity per se. The paper provides new microdata evidence that disentangles the innovation margin from other growth-constraining margins, with implications for the design of R&D policy and for interpreting aggregate evidence on the "ideas production function."

## 2. AI in Science: When and Where It Makes a Difference [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Stefano Bianchini, Valentina Di Girolamo, Julien Ravet, and David Arranz
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Vol. 55, Issue 6, Article 105478; online April 2026)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105478
**Abstract:** This paper provides large-scale empirical evidence on the diffusion and effects of artificial intelligence across more than 170 scientific fields between 2005 and 2023, drawing on a dataset of over 80 million scientific publications and assessing AI's contribution to knowledge creation through bibliometric indicators of novelty and citation impact. The findings show that, on average, AI use is associated with more novel and more highly cited research, and that these effects are strengthened by recent advances in AI technology—particularly the introduction of transformer architectures and large language models. AI's contribution to novelty is not uniform across fields or across time, however: it intensifies with the penetration of AI within a given field and with the "roughness" of the underlying knowledge space, defined as the degree to which a field's ideas are fragmented and combinatorially complex. In fields where knowledge is organized around a small number of dominant frameworks, AI's novelty-enhancing effect is attenuated. The authors interpret these patterns as indicating that AI functions most powerfully as a combinatorial search tool in high-complexity epistemic environments, with implications for the allocation of AI-assisted research effort across scientific disciplines and for science policy aimed at stimulating breakthrough discovery.

## 3. Research Excellence Is Hard to Sustain: Evidence from Japan's WPI Initiative [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Arsev Umur Aydınoğlu and Özgür Kadir Özer
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Vol. 55, Issue 6, Article 105493; online April 2026)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105493
**Abstract:** This paper provides the first systematic multi-center evaluation of Japan's World Premier International Research Center Initiative (WPI), a concentrated science-funding program launched in 2007 that selects a small number of institutions to receive sustained, internationally competitive levels of support with the explicit goal of building globally visible centers of research excellence. Using panel data covering all WPI centers and matched comparison institutions, the authors document that WPI centers exhibited significantly higher publication output and citation impact than their host universities during the period of active WPI funding. Post-funding trajectories diverged markedly across centers, however: institutions that successfully built alternative external resource streams—through competitive grants, industry partnerships, or sustained host-university investment—maintained elevated research performance following the end of exceptional WPI support, whereas centers that remained dependent on institutional baseline budgets reverted toward host-university performance benchmarks. Interpreted through Resource Dependence Theory, the findings underscore that externally induced excellence gains are contingent on reconfiguring the institutional resource environment before exceptional support ends, not on funding intensity per se. The results have direct implications for the design and expected duration of concentrated science-funding initiatives in late-developing academic systems, and contribute empirical evidence to the long-standing debate over the social returns to targeted scientific excellence programs.

## 4. Early AI Adoption and Firm Productivity Growth in a Middle-Income Economy [Economics of AI]
**Author:** Juan Duran-Vanegas
**Date:** April 3, 2026 (SSRN Working Paper No. 6515438)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6515438
**Abstract:** This paper examines the effects of early AI adoption on firm-level productivity growth in Colombia, a middle-income economy where digital infrastructure and complementary technical capability vary substantially across firms, providing a context largely absent from the existing AI-productivity literature that centers on high-income countries. Using data from the 2019 Colombian Enterprise ICT Survey linked to longitudinal manufacturing firm records, the author applies entropy balancing to estimate productivity growth differentials between AI-adopting and non-adopting firms over 2016–2019. AI adoption is associated with a 16 percent cumulative increase in labor productivity over the period, equivalent to approximately 5 percent annualized growth. These gains are driven by increases in sales and value added rather than by reductions in employment or input costs, making labor-saving substitution an unlikely proximate mechanism. Effects are similar in magnitude for firms developing AI in-house and those sourcing AI externally, suggesting that access—not internal capability for AI development—is the primary constraint on adoption benefits in this setting. The productivity differential is substantially larger for firms with higher preexisting technical capabilities, indicating that complementary human capital and organizational capacity are key to realizing AI's productivity potential. The analysis further identifies changes in organizational structure as an adjustment margin, consistent with AI reshaping task allocation within firms rather than simply replacing existing factor inputs.

## 5. The Labor Market Effect of Generative Artificial Intelligence on Artists [Economics of AI]
**Author:** Christos A. Makridis
**Date:** February 2026 (Journal of Cultural Economics)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10824-026-09575-3
**Abstract:** This paper investigates the labor-market effects of generative AI on U.S. artists—a category of creative workers often cited as among the most exposed to large language model capabilities due to their intensive use of content generation, ideation, and synthesis tasks. The author constructs an occupation-level index of generative AI exposure by combining task-content assessments with LLM capability ratings and links this measure to establishment-level employment and earnings outcomes from the Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics and to individual-level microdata from the American Community Survey. Event-study specifications comparing more- versus less-exposed artistic occupations from 2017 to 2024 find little evidence of short-run earnings declines attributable to AI exposure through 2023, with estimated effects near zero and in some specifications modestly positive. Within-person estimates from Gallup Workplace Panel data show limited average well-being effects of AI adoption among artists, though heterogeneous patterns emerge across specializations. AI use among artists is concentrated in ideation and creative-support tasks rather than final work completion, consistent with augmentation of creative labor in the early diffusion phase rather than substitution. The findings are consistent with task reallocation as the initial margin of adjustment to generative AI, while leaving open the possibility of more significant medium-run labor-market effects as AI capabilities deepen, adoption broadens, and complementary investments in AI-assisted creative workflows accumulate across the industry.
