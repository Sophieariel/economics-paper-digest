# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-17

## 1. Data Centers and Local Economies in the Age of AI: A Shift-Share Approach [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Fernando E. Alvarez, David Argente, Joyce Chow, and Diana Van Patten
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35194)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35194
**Abstract:** Data centers form the foundational infrastructure supporting cloud computing, AI, and enterprise software systems. The expanding adoption of artificial intelligence is driving heightened demand for computational resources, spurring accelerated data center investment, and generating debate regarding their local economic and environmental consequences. These facilities present a nuanced policy challenge—they offer capital inflows, construction jobs, and specialized employment opportunities, yet simultaneously increase requirements for electricity, land, and grid infrastructure. This investigation examines county-level impacts across the United States using facility-level data tracking global data centers with geographic coordinates, capacity metrics, and revenue figures. Researchers merged this information with County Business Patterns, IRS income statistics, housing prices, and electricity rates. To account for strategic facility placement—data centers are not randomly sited, creating endogeneity concerns—the study employs instrumental variable estimation using two shift-share instruments: pre-existing proximity to InterTubes long-haul fiber nodes, and the 1980 county share of U.S. urban college population. Results demonstrate positive and statistically significant associations between data center expansion and total employment, specialized computing-sector jobs, construction employment, business establishments, and property values. Tax returns, adjusted gross income, and wages likewise show positive correlations across different timeframes. Annual payroll effects are positive but less consistent across specifications, reflecting variation in the timing of employment effects. The paper contributes causal county-level evidence on how AI infrastructure investment shapes local economies, offering a framework for evaluating the distributional and geographic consequences of the ongoing build-out of AI compute capacity.

## 2. How Retrainable are AI-Exposed Workers? [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Benjamin G. Hyman, Benjamin Lahey, Karen Ni, and Laura Pilossoph
**Date:** Revised May 2026 (originally issued August 2025); NBER Working Paper 34174
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34174
**Abstract:** This research examines how workers adapt to advancing artificial intelligence capabilities by analyzing 1.9 million occupational training spells funded by the U.S. Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA) from 2012 to 2024. Trainees are compared against matched control groups receiving only job search assistance, enabling identification of the causal earnings gains from formal retraining. The study's key finding is that returns to retraining are strongly heterogeneous by AI exposure level: workers from low-AI-exposure occupations earned positive quarterly returns throughout the sample period, while returns for workers from high-exposure occupations rose sharply—from approximately $900 quarterly before 2020 to $2,900 by 2022–2024. The authors attribute the bulk of these gains primarily to transitions into less AI-exposed occupations, with secondary contributions from the expansion of training programs that build AI-complementary skills. To characterize retraining potential systematically, the researchers introduce an AI Retrainability Index (AIR) that identifies a large share of occupations as "AI-retrainable," suggesting substantial scope for worker adaptation across the broader economy. The May 2026 revision incorporates updated WIOA administrative records through mid-2025 and extends the analysis of AI-complementary skill pathways. The findings offer evidence-based guidance for workforce policy at a time when AI capabilities are advancing rapidly and publicly funded retraining programs face growing demand from workers in exposed occupations.

## 3. Automated Routing, Patent Examiner Specialization and Quasi-random Assignment at the USPTO [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Nicholas A. Pairolero and Charles A.W. de Grazia
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 4, Article 105460); posted SSRN February 2026
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6286238
**Abstract:** A large body of empirical research on patents and innovation relies on the assumption that patent applications are quasi-randomly assigned to examiners within art units at the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). This assumption enables researchers to use examiner-level leniency—measured by grant rates—as an instrumental variable for identifying causal effects of patent grant decisions on downstream outcomes such as firm investment, employment, and R&D. This paper documents that the USPTO's 2020 transition to an automated routing system fundamentally altered the assignment process in ways that invalidate this identifying assumption. Using administrative data from the USPTO, the authors show that the new routing system increased technical overlap between examiner work histories and newly assigned cases by nearly 57 percent, representing a large and systematic increase in examiner specialization. Applications are now matched to examiners based on technological similarity, meaning the assignment of an application to a particular examiner is no longer random conditional on observable case characteristics. The authors provide formal tests of the quasi-randomness assumption before and after the 2020 transition, confirm its breakdown in post-2020 data, and assess the robustness of previously published research using USPTO examiner designs to the methodological change. The findings have far-reaching implications: researchers using USPTO patent data must restrict samples to pre-2020 observations or adopt alternative identification strategies, and a significant share of existing empirical work in the patent-examination literature warrants reexamination.

## 4. Does Early Regional Scientific Leadership Translate Into Lasting Innovation Advantage? [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Dragan Filimonovic, Jeffrey T. Macher, Christian Rutzer, and Rolf Weder
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 4); WWZ Working Paper, University of Basel
**Source:** https://edoc.unibas.ch/96718/
**Abstract:** This paper asks whether regions that establish early scientific leadership in an emerging technology sustain a lasting advantage in downstream innovation as measured by patenting activity. The question bears directly on the economics of agglomeration, path dependence in regional specialization, and the design of place-based science policy. The authors assemble a dataset covering 27 emerging technologies—including Artificial Intelligence, Cloud Computing, and biotechnology—across U.S. and OECD metropolitan regions over a 20-year horizon, identifying "pioneer" regions as those with disproportionate scientific output in a given technology during its early commercialization window. Using panel regression methods with region and technology fixed effects, the study finds that pioneer regions achieve a large and increasing patenting advantage over non-pioneer regions, with the gap widening rather than narrowing over the observation period. The advantage is especially pronounced in "super-cluster" regions—those that combine pioneering scientific research in an emerging field with robust existing patenting capacity in technologically adjacent domains—suggesting that complementarity between scientific and technological assets is a key amplifying mechanism. The findings have implications for science and innovation policy: early investments in foundational research appear to generate durable competitive advantages that compound over time rather than being eroded by imitation or technology diffusion, with regional R&D policy consequently facing strong path dependence and a high premium on early positioning.

## 5. AI and the Quantity and Quality of Creative Products: Have LLMs Boosted Creation of Valuable Books? [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Imke Reimers and Joel Waldfogel
**Date:** Revised May 2026 (originally issued January 2026); NBER Working Paper 34777
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34777
**Abstract:** From 2022 to 2025, the availability of large language models tripled new e-book releases on Amazon's Kindle platform, rising from approximately 100,000 monthly releases in the pre-LLM period to over 300,000 by late 2025. This paper examines whether this surge in AI-assisted book creation generated net consumer value, or whether quality degradation offset the quantity gains. The authors analyze over 333,000 e-book releases and use AI-detection tools to classify books by the likely presence of AI-generated content. The findings reveal a clear trade-off: the introduction of large language models was associated with a rapid increase in the number of new e-book releases and a simultaneous decline in average quality, as measured by reader engagement metrics. Books with detected AI content receive significantly lower quality ratings, and their growing market share—exceeding half of all 2025 releases—accounts for the overall downward trend in average book quality. Despite the quality decline, the authors apply consumer preference modeling and find that if output tripled at the new (lower) quality level, consumer surplus rises by approximately 10 percent relative to pre-LLM conditions; conversely, at the reduced average quality with pre-LLM release volumes, surplus would fall by 13 percent. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that books outside the top 100 per category actually improved in quality relative to pre-LLM benchmarks, while established authors from before the LLM era continued to produce higher-quality work without apparent displacement. Author selection differences—LLM adopters differ systematically from traditional authors—account for most of the quality gap between AI-assisted and human-written books, and this gap is narrowing over time as adoption broadens.
