# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-18

## 1. Did the Defend Trade Secrets Act Spur the Reliance on Trade Secrets? [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Katrin Hussinger
**Date:** June 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 5, Article 105474; Short Communication)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S004873332600065X
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether the 2016 U.S. Defend Trade Secrets Act (DTSA) altered firms' intellectual property protection strategies by increasing their reliance on trade secrets relative to patents. The DTSA created a federal civil cause of action for trade secret misappropriation, substantially strengthening the enforceability and perceived security of trade secret protection. The author exploits cross-sectoral variation in the pre-DTSA importance of trade secrets—measured by sector-level differences in firms' propensity to rely on secrecy as a protection mechanism prior to the Act—as an identification strategy for causal inference. The results show that the DTSA increased trade secret declarations at the intensive margin, that is, the volume of declarations among firms already using trade secrets, primarily in service industries. At the extensive margin—whether previously non-using firms began filing trade secret declarations—increases are documented in both manufacturing and service industries, with the strongest growth occurring in R&D-intensive manufacturing sectors. The findings contribute to the economics of science and innovation literature by providing causal evidence on how federal IP law reforms shape firms' choices between the two principal forms of appropriability: formal patents and informal secrecy.

## 2. Political, Economic and Research Disintegration: The Impact of Geopolitical Uncertainty on Cross-Border R&D Collaborations and Innovation [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Enrico Vanino, Mustapha Douch, and Ismail Çakmak
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 4; "Rethinking science and technology strategies in a multipolar world" special collection)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/research-policy/vol/55/issue/4
**Abstract:** This paper examines how geopolitical uncertainty arising from the Brexit process affected cross-border R&D collaborations and innovation outcomes in the United Kingdom. Using the UK's exit from the European Union as a quasi-natural experiment, the authors find robust evidence that patent quality deteriorated during the period of heightened uncertainty. While UK organizations expanded the technological scope of their patent portfolios—increasing the number of International Patent Classification sub-classes per patent and shifting the composition of patenting activity away from traditional operations and transport technologies toward applied physics—this broadening of scope did not translate into improvements in patents' generality or originality, two standard measures of high-impact innovation. Inventor-level data document a clear shift in the composition of innovation teams: the share of EU-based inventors on UK patents fell significantly following the Brexit vote, indicating that UK organizations adopted a more inward-looking innovation strategy and reduced the intensity of cross-border R&D collaboration with European partners. The study provides empirical evidence that political and economic disintegration can substantially disrupt the international scientific collaborations and inventor networks that underpin high-quality innovation, and contributes to the growing literature on geopolitics as a determinant of science and technology trajectories.

## 3. AI Users Are Not All Alike: The Characteristics of French Firms Buying and Developing AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Flavio Calvino and Luca Fontanelli
**Date:** March 19, 2026 (online first); June 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 5)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733326000648
**Abstract:** This paper studies AI adoption at the French firm level by distinguishing two types of AI users: firms that purchase AI capabilities from external providers (AI buyers) and firms that develop AI systems in-house (AI developers). Using data from the official French ICT survey matched to administrative business records, the authors document that the two adoption modes are associated with markedly different firm profiles. Firm size is a positive predictor of AI buyer status, but this relationship is largely accounted for by firms' pre-existing information and communication technology infrastructure, human capital, and organizational complementarities. AI developer firms, by contrast, are significantly larger and younger independently of ICT and human capital factors, and they require substantially more specialized technical personnel than buyers do. On the productivity side, AI-using firms outperform non-users on standard productivity measures, though a large share of this premium reflects ex ante selection of already-productive firms into AI adoption rather than causal productivity gains from AI. Importantly, the positive link between AI use and productivity remains statistically significant beyond selection effects specifically for firms developing their own AI systems, suggesting that internal AI development generates broader organizational and process complementarities that external purchasing does not. The findings imply that aggregate statistics on "AI adoption" conceal important heterogeneity across modes of use, with distinct policy implications for AI regulation, competition policy, and firm-level industrial strategy.

## 4. Digital Skill Adoption and Employment Dynamics: Evidence from Matched Vacancy-Employer–Employee Data [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Mathias Fjællegaard Jensen and Cédric Schneider
**Date:** June 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 5)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733326000673
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how firms' investments in digital skills—particularly AI and data science capabilities—shape employment dynamics, using a novel dataset that matches online job posting data to linked employer-employee administrative records from Denmark. The authors construct occupation-level and firm-level measures of digital skill demand from vacancy texts using natural language processing, distinguishing general digital skills from the more specific categories of data science and artificial intelligence. They document a strong positive correlation between AI and data science skill demand and firm-level total factor productivity, with effects substantially larger than those associated with general digital skill demand. Estimating the relationship between digital skill investments and firm-level employment growth, the paper finds positive effects that are statistically robust and economically meaningful, broad-based across worker demographic groups including age and gender, and significantly stronger when the analysis focuses specifically on AI and data science skill categories. The results indicate that AI skill adoption operates through skill complementarity rather than substitution: firms posting more AI-skill vacancies expand their overall workforces relative to firms that do not, rather than substituting AI-capable workers for existing employees. The findings provide linked microdata evidence—in a Nordic institutional context with strong labor market institutions—on how firms' hiring of AI-capable workers shapes aggregate employment, contributing to the empirical literature on technology adoption, labor demand, and the future of work.

## 5. Deep Research on a Loop: Using AI Agents to Construct Economic Datasets [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Santiago Afonso, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro H. Gálvez, and Raul A. Sosa
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35188)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35188
**Abstract:** This paper introduces DRIL (Deep Research on a Loop), a methodology that uses AI research agents to automate the construction of economic datasets from publicly accessible sources, and evaluates its accuracy, coverage, and cost relative to traditional research-assistant-based data collection. The methodology rests on a two-stage architecture that separates the design phase from the implementation phase: a research instrument defines the target variables, measurement protocols, and coding conventions, while an evidence policy governs source selection, attribution standards, and documentation requirements. Quality-control mechanisms continuously monitor data gaps and flag uncertainty for researcher review, creating a human-in-the-loop quality assurance process. The authors validate DRIL by applying it to update the Global Tax Expenditures Database for eight Latin American and Caribbean countries, generating 129 source documents and 136 evidence records, achieving full coverage of 22 qualitative fields and six quantitative estimate categories, and completing the entire process at a cost comparable to a few hours of research-assistant work. The paper argues that even partial automation of dataset construction can materially shift the production function of empirical economics: reducing the time from data generation to publication, enabling researchers to maintain time-sensitive datasets that would otherwise become stale, and democratizing access to data infrastructure for researchers at institutions with limited research-assistant budgets. The findings contribute to a growing literature on AI's role in transforming research methodology and the organization of scientific labor in economics.
