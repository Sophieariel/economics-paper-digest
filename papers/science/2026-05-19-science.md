# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-19

## 1. Attention (And Money) Is All You Need: Why Universities Are Struggling to Keep AI Talent [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ufuk Akcigit, Craig A. Chikis, Emin Dinlersoz, and Nathan Goldschlag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34964
**Abstract:** The researchers constructed a dataset linking academic publications to U.S. Census employer-employee records, tracking approximately 42,000 AI researchers over two decades. Their findings reveal a significant shift in how AI talent is distributed. Industry positions increasingly attract younger and foreign-born researchers. Meanwhile, academia has made more progress improving gender diversity. A striking wage disparity has emerged: the top 1% of publishing industry scientists now earn $1.5 million more annually than comparable academics, a fivefold increase since 2001. This growing compensation gap coincides with researchers concentrating at major established firms. Those transitioning to industry roles demonstrate a notable behavioral change—they produce fewer publications but generate more patents, indicating a movement away from open scientific research toward closed proprietary development.

## 2. Knowledge Spillovers and Local Outcomes: An Existence Proof from the Establishment of the National Labs [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Susan Helper, Resem Makan, and Daniel W. Shoag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35011
**Abstract:** This research examines the establishment of U.S. National Laboratories during the 1940s–1950s to measure local knowledge spillovers from public research infrastructure. The study identifies causal effects because lab sites were selected for security and political reasons, rather than existing or potential innovative capability, and the researchers identified runner-up locations through archival research. Key findings include substantial and sustained increases in patenting by non-laboratory inventors in lab counties, with non-lab patents shifting toward laboratories' research fields. Using digitized county data from 1936–1970, researchers documented persistent gains in retail sales and household income. Census records linking 1940–1950 data reveal wage increases for pre-existing residents, particularly college-educated workers. School-age cohorts exposed to laboratory establishment achieved higher educational attainment. Notably, these spillovers occurred despite extensive secrecy around early nuclear research, indicating that proximity to public R&D generates local benefits even with restricted information access.

## 3. Artificial Intelligence, Productivity, and the Workforce: Evidence from Corporate Executives [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Salomé Baslandze, Zachary Edwards, John Graham, Ty McClure, Brent H. Meyer, Michael Sparks, Sonya R. Waddell, and Daniel Weitz
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34984
**Abstract:** The researchers surveyed approximately 750 corporate executives about AI's impact on their organizations. Key findings include: over half of firms have already invested in AI, though smaller companies are slower to adopt. Productivity improvements are positive and expected to grow in 2026, with the largest effects concentrated in high-skill services and finance. Gains stem from total factor productivity rather than capital deepening, linked to innovation and demand channels. A productivity paradox exists where executives perceive larger gains than measurable outcomes suggest. Little evidence indicates near-term aggregate job losses, though larger firms anticipate workforce reductions while smaller firms expect modest employment gains. Labor is shifting within and across firms, with routine clerical roles declining and increased demand for technical skilled positions. The authors developed an index identifying job functions most vulnerable to AI displacement.

## 4. Firm Data on AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ivan Yotzov, Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Philip Bunn, Steven J. Davis, Kevin M. Foster, Aaron Jalca, Brent H. Meyer, Paul Mizen, Michael A. Navarrete, Pawel Smietanka, Gregory Thwaites, and Ben Zhe Wang
**Date:** February 2026 (revised March 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836
**Abstract:** The researchers surveyed approximately 6,000 senior executives across US, UK, German, and Australian firms regarding AI adoption and its consequences. Key findings include: 69% of firms currently utilize AI, with higher adoption among younger and more productive companies. While over two-thirds of executives personally use AI, their average weekly usage is only about 1.5 hours. Regarding recent impacts over the past three years, nine in ten executives reported negligible effects on employment or productivity. However, looking forward three years, these same leaders anticipate significant changes—predicting AI will increase productivity by 1.4%, boost output by 0.8%, and reduce employment by 0.7%. Notably, employees hold more optimistic views, expecting AI to increase employment by 0.5% at their firms, revealing a notable difference in expectations between management and workers.

## 5. Forecasting the Economic Effects of AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ezra Karger, Otto Kuusela, Jason Abaluck, Kevin A. Bryan, Basil Halperin, Todd R. Jones, Connacher Murphy, Philip Trammell, Matt Reynolds, Dan Mayland, Ria Viswanathan, Ananaya Mittal, Rebecca Ceppas de Castro, Josh Rosenberg, and Philip Tetlock (et al.)
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35046
**Abstract:** The study surveyed five distinct groups—academic economists, AI company employees, policy researchers, highly accurate forecasters, and the general public—regarding AI's economic impact. Respondents across all groups anticipate substantial advances in AI capabilities by 2030 and expect annual GDP growth of 2.5%, surpassing baseline government and private forecasts. Under a rapid AI progress scenario, experts project annualized GDP growth of approximately 4% and labor force participation declining to 55% by 2050, with roughly half of this decline attributable to AI (approximately 10 million jobs). Disagreement among experts stems primarily from differing assessments of highly capable AI's economic effects rather than disagreement about technological advancement pace. Policy preferences diverge: experts favor targeted interventions like worker retraining, while the general public supports broader measures including job guarantees and universal basic income.
