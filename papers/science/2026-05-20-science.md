# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-20

## 1. Estimating the Present Value of R&D Tax Benefits in the United States [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Brandon Pecoraro, Nicholas C. Hoffman, Martin Lopez-Daneri, Elena C. Derby, Rachel Moore, and Shannon E. Sledz
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35208)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35208
**Abstract:** This paper uses confidential corporate tax return data to compute the actual present value (PV) of tax benefits that U.S. firms receive from the Research and Experimentation (R&E) tax credit and related deductions, tracking how firms utilize these tax attributes over time as they carry credits and deductions forward or backward across tax years. The study covers the period 2012–2016 and documents that, on average, firms realized approximately $0.36 in present value per dollar of R&D investment, compared with the statutory benefit of $0.41—a gap of roughly 12 percent driven by timing frictions and constraints on utilization. Heterogeneity across firm types is large: young, small firms reporting tax losses realized only $0.23 per dollar, a 44 percent reduction relative to the statutory amount, reflecting the asymmetric treatment of losses under the U.S. tax code and the limited ability of pre-revenue firms to use non-refundable credits immediately. Firms with consistently positive taxable income realize benefits close to the statutory benchmark, while large firms that consistently exhaust credits realize amounts closest to the statutory level. By quantifying the wedge between intended and realized R&D subsidies, the paper provides a more accurate basis for evaluating the effectiveness of R&D tax incentives—a central instrument of U.S. science and innovation policy—and highlights the extent to which current credit design systematically disadvantages the youngest and smallest innovators.

## 2. Old Space, New Space: A Commercial Revolution in Innovation? [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ruben Gaetani and Alexander T. Whalley
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35212)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35212
**Abstract:** This paper uses detailed patent data to examine when and how the commercial space industry underwent its innovation revolution, testing the popular narrative that the emergence of New Space entrants such as SpaceX and Blue Origin after 2005 constituted the decisive inflection point. The authors find that the largest surge in space-related innovation activity occurred in the 1990s rather than in the post-2005 era, coinciding with the period of government-sponsored market creation through programs such as commercial launch and satellite communications deregulation—well before the high-profile startup entries that dominate public discourse. Despite the prominence of new entrants, incumbent aerospace and defense firms continue to account for the majority of space-related patenting, with entrants contributing a growing but still minority share of the patent stock. The geographic distribution of space innovation has remained concentrated in the same metropolitan areas that dominated during the post-Apollo era, indicating substantial path dependence in the regional clustering of aerospace innovation. The paper interprets these findings through directed technical change theory, arguing that the primary mechanism through which government shapes commercial innovation is not merely through R&D subsidies or procurement, but through creating accessible market demand that redirects private-sector inventive effort. The results bear on ongoing policy debates about the optimal role of government in fostering commercial space, advanced manufacturing, and other strategic technology sectors.

## 3. Artificial Intelligence Tools Expand Scientists' Impact but Contract Science's Focus [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Qianyue Hao, Fengli Xu, Yong Li, and James Evans
**Date:** January 16, 2026 (Nature, vol. 649, pp. 1237–1243; DOI: 10.1038/s41586-025-09922-y)
**Source:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09922-y
**Abstract:** This paper examines how the adoption of AI tools reshapes both the productivity and the collective direction of scientific research, using a dataset of 41.3 million papers published from 1980 to 2024 across the natural sciences. The authors identify AI-adopting scientists through textual analysis of paper content and acknowledgment sections, and compare their career trajectories and research choices against matched non-adopters. At the individual level, AI adoption is associated with striking gains: scientists who use AI tools publish 3.02 times more papers, accumulate 4.84 times more citations, and attain principal-investigator status 1.37 years earlier than observably comparable non-adopters. However, these individual productivity gains are accompanied by significant collective costs: AI adoption is associated with a 4.63 percent reduction in the breadth of scientific topics studied across the field and a 22 percent decrease in scientist-to-scientist collaboration and cross-citation. The mechanism is that AI tools excel at automating established research paradigms and data-rich subfields, creating incentives for scientists to concentrate in already well-mapped areas rather than explore data-scarce or conceptually distant frontiers. The authors interpret this as a structural tension between private returns to AI adoption—which are concentrated in high-citation, high-productivity niches—and social returns to science, which depend on breadth, novelty, and the exploration of under-studied problems. The findings have direct implications for science policy, grant design, and the organization of research institutions seeking to harness AI's productivity benefits without sacrificing the diversity of the knowledge-production portfolio.

## 4. Early AI Adoption and Firm Productivity Growth in a Middle-Income Economy [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Juan Duran-Vanegas
**Date:** April 3, 2026 (SSRN Working Paper No. 6515438)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6515438
**Abstract:** This paper estimates the causal effect of early AI adoption on firm-level labor productivity growth in Colombia, a middle-income economy, combining data on AI use from the 2019 Colombian Enterprise ICT Survey with longitudinal manufacturing census data covering 2016–2019. The author uses entropy balancing to construct a weighted control group of non-adopting firms that closely matches adopting firms on a rich set of pre-adoption characteristics and productivity trajectories, thereby addressing selection concerns that would bias naive comparisons. The main finding is that AI adoption is associated with a 16 percent cumulative increase in labor productivity over the three-year post-adoption window, equivalent to approximately 5 percent annualized growth—a substantially larger effect than estimates from high-income economies at similar stages of adoption. Decomposing the sources of the productivity premium, the author finds that the gains are driven primarily by higher sales and value added per worker rather than by reductions in employment or intermediate input costs, suggesting that AI enhances output rather than substitutes for existing factor inputs. The productivity premium is similar in magnitude across firms that develop AI capabilities in-house and those that outsource AI development to third-party vendors, ruling out own-R&D intensity as the sole mechanism. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that the productivity gains are systematically larger for firms that had higher pre-adoption technical capabilities, as measured by prior ICT investment and skilled-labor intensity, consistent with a complementarity between existing organizational capabilities and AI-enabled productivity improvements. The results provide rare firm-level causal evidence from outside the OECD on how AI shapes productivity in the early adoption phase.

## 5. The Labor Market Effects of Generative Artificial Intelligence [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Jonathan Hartley, Filip Jolevski, Vitor Melo, and Brendan Moore
**Date:** January 2026 (SSRN Working Paper No. 5136877)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5136877
**Abstract:** This paper assesses the labor-market consequences of generative AI by combining a novel longitudinal survey of U.S. workers with administrative employment and earnings records, measuring both AI adoption rates and outcomes across occupations from the public release of ChatGPT in November 2022 through late 2025. The survey documents that large language model (LLM) adoption among employed U.S. workers rose from 30.1 percent in December 2024 to 38.3 percent in December 2025, with adoption concentrated among younger workers, college graduates, higher-income employees, and workers in customer service, marketing, and information technology. Linking occupation-level survey data on AI exposure and adoption to Current Population Survey earnings records and Bureau of Labor Statistics vacancy data, the authors estimate difference-in-differences models comparing labor market outcomes in high- versus low-LLM-exposure occupations before and after the generative AI shock. The main findings are that AI exposure is associated with small but statistically significant positive wage effects in highly exposed occupations and has had no significant effect on job openings or total employment levels across those occupations through early 2026. The absence of broad-based displacement effects is consistent with an adjustment path operating primarily through task reallocation—with workers in exposed occupations reporting time savings and quality improvements in retained tasks—and through within-firm productivity gains that partially offset automation pressure on labor demand. Heterogeneity analysis finds larger positive wage effects in occupations where AI functions as a complement to skilled judgment rather than a substitute for routine task execution, and the paper identifies a growing divergence in adoption rates between firms that have institutionalized AI workflows and those that have not yet integrated AI tools into standard practice.
