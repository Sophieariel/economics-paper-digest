# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-21

## 1. Old Space, New Space: A Commercial Revolution in Innovation? [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ruben Gaetani and Alexander T. Whalley
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35212)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35212
**Abstract:** This paper asks whether the much-celebrated New Space commercial revolution—marked by the rise of firms such as SpaceX and Planet Labs after 2005—represents a genuine discontinuity in the history of space innovation, or whether it is better understood as a continuation of patterns set in motion much earlier. The authors construct a comprehensive dataset of space-related patents and trace the timing and organizational structure of innovation in the space sector from the post-Apollo era to the present. Using patent data and a classification approach that distinguishes space-specific inventions from adjacent technologies, the study finds that the major surge in space-related innovation occurred in the 1990s—well before the emergence of the celebrated new entrants—and coincided with government-created market demand for commercial satellite launches and related services rather than with any obvious inflection in private venture activity. Incumbent aerospace firms account for most space-related patenting throughout the sample period, with new entrants contributing a growing but still minority share of inventive output. Geographic concentration of space innovation has proved highly persistent: the same metropolitan regions that dominated patenting in the post-Apollo era remain the centers of space-related invention today, suggesting strong localized agglomeration effects and little spatial rebalancing despite the structural changes in industry organization. The paper argues that government's most consequential role in stimulating space innovation may lie in constructing appropriable markets—that is, creating demand conditions under which private investment in innovation becomes commercially viable—rather than in the direct funding of research and development. This finding generalizes to other complex, capital-intensive sectors in which public procurement and regulatory policy shape incentives more powerfully than R&D subsidies alone. The authors make their patent classification data available for future research use.

## 2. AI in Science: When and Where It Makes a Difference [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Stefano Bianchini, Valentina Di Girolamo, Julien Ravet, and David Arranz
**Date:** Available online April 2026; Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 6 (July 2026), Article 105478
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733326000697
**Abstract:** This paper provides large-scale empirical evidence on whether and how the diffusion of artificial intelligence reshapes the production of scientific knowledge across academic disciplines. The authors draw on a dataset of over 80 million publications spanning more than 170 scientific fields from 2005 to 2023, constructing measures of AI adoption at the paper level by identifying use of AI methods and tools, and assessing AI's contribution to knowledge creation through two bibliometric indicators: scientific novelty (measured by the unusualness of reference combinations relative to field norms) and citation impact (measured by forward citations relative to field averages). The central finding is that AI use is, on average, associated with significantly more novel and more highly cited research, and that these positive effects have strengthened with the emergence of transformer-based architectures and large language models in recent years. However, the effects are strongly heterogeneous across fields in ways consistent with a "combinatorial" theory of AI's scientific value: the contribution of AI to novelty intensifies with the depth of AI penetration within a field and is largest in fragmented knowledge spaces—fields in which ideas are scattered across many subdomains and combinatorial synthesis is difficult—rather than in fields with well-integrated theoretical and methodological traditions. The paper provides the most comprehensive cross-field empirical accounting to date of how AI reshapes the knowledge production function in science, and informs ongoing debates about whether AI's scientific benefits are broadly distributed or confined to a narrow set of data-intensive disciplines.

## 3. Artificial Intelligence Tools Expand Scientists' Impact but Contract Science's Focus [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Qianyue Hao, Fengli Xu, Yong Li, and James Evans
**Date:** January 2026 (Nature, Vol. 649, pp. 1237–1243; published online 16 January 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09922-y
**Abstract:** This paper presents large-scale bibliometric evidence on the individual and collective consequences of AI tool adoption in scientific research. The authors analyze approximately 41.3 million papers across the natural sciences, matching scientists who have adopted AI tools—including large language models, computer vision systems, and automated data analysis pipelines—to observationally similar non-adopters, and tracking a battery of productivity and scope outcomes over subsequent publication years. At the individual level, the results document dramatic productivity gains: AI-adopting scientists produce 3.02 times more papers per year, receive 4.84 times more citations, and attain leadership of research projects an average of 1.37 years earlier than comparable non-adopters. These gains are widespread and appear across disciplines and career stages. At the collective level, however, the findings reveal a concerning contraction: the set of distinct scientific topics actively investigated shrinks by 4.63% in fields with higher AI adoption, and the frequency of scientist-to-scientist collaboration declines by 22%. The authors attribute this dual pattern to AI's propensity to automate incremental and replicative work within established, data-rich research trajectories, which directs scientific effort toward well-charted territory rather than toward novel questions requiring human exploration. The paper demonstrates that AI tools simultaneously expand individual impact and contract the collective diversity of scientific inquiry, raising fundamental questions about the long-run consequences of AI adoption for scientific breadth, resilience to blind spots, and the rate at which genuinely new knowledge is generated.

## 4. Job Transformation, Specialization, and the Labor Market Effects of AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Lukas B. Freund and Lukas F. Mann
**Date:** February 2026 (SSRN Working Paper No. 6320018; Minneapolis Fed Institute Working Paper; CES Working Paper No. 12072)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6320018
**Abstract:** This paper examines how large language models and other AI automation tools alter the task composition of jobs and generate heterogeneous earnings effects across the wage distribution. The authors develop a quantitative task-based framework in which occupations bundle multiple tasks and workers with heterogeneous skill portfolios sort into occupations by comparative advantage; automation by AI shifts the relative importance of tasks within existing occupations, changing the task content of jobs for workers who remain in their current roles rather than switching occupations. To implement the model empirically, the authors measure the task content of occupations using natural language processing applied to detailed occupational descriptors from the O*NET database, and estimate the distribution of workers' task-specific skill endowments from the joint distribution of wages and occupational choices. LLM-based automation exposure is assigned at the task level using capability assessments from frontier models. The framework is used to project the earnings effects of automation across the wage distribution. Key empirical findings include: workers with moderate AI exposure gain on average, while those with high AI exposure face earnings losses, with substantial within-occupation heterogeneity in outcomes; the return to social and interpersonal skills rises relative to the return to analytical and routine cognitive skills; and lower-earning workers gain proportionally more than higher-earning workers from moderate automation, suggesting potential compression of wage inequality at the lower tail, though this reverses for workers with very high task exposure. The paper's central contribution is to show that job transformation—shifts in the task composition within an occupation rather than occupational switching—is the dominant adjustment margin for AI's labor-market effects, and that ignoring within-occupation task reallocation leads standard exposure-based models to mischaracterize both the direction and distribution of earnings consequences.

## 5. AI Is Already Eroding Wages: Quasi-Experimental Evidence From Occupational Exposure [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** José Azar, Mireia Gine, and Javier Sanz-Espín
**Date:** December 2025 (SSRN Working Paper No. 5842084; IESE Business School)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5842084
**Abstract:** This paper provides quasi-experimental evidence on whether the arrival of generative AI has already begun to reduce wages for workers in exposed occupations. The authors exploit the November 2022 public release of ChatGPT as an exogenous shock to the availability of high-capability AI tools and construct a firm-level pre-treatment measure of AI exposure based on each firm's occupational workforce composition before the shock. Using a matched administrative wage registry covering approximately 138 million realized worker–firm employment spells in the United States, the authors implement a difference-in-differences design comparing wage outcomes at high-exposure firms to low-exposure firms over the 2021–2023 period. The main finding is that the average wage at firms with above-median AI occupational exposure fell by approximately 4.5% relative to below-median firms in the two years following ChatGPT's release. Decomposing this aggregate effect by seniority level reveals a sharply heterogeneous pattern: starting wages for new hires into junior and mid-level positions at exposed firms fell by 6.3% and 5.9%, respectively, relative to unexposed firms, while starting wages for senior-level positions remained stable or increased slightly. Firms with higher AI exposure also hired new workers at lower average educational attainment for junior and mid-level positions, consistent with a decline in the task complexity required for entry-level roles. The results are robust to controlling for industry trends, establishment size, and pre-period wage dynamics. The paper provides among the first large-scale causal evidence that generative AI has already begun to exert downward pressure on entry- and mid-level wages in exposed occupations, with wage adjustment rather than employment displacement as the early dominant channel through which AI's labor-market effects are manifesting.
