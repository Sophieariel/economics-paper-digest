# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-22

## 1. The Microstructure of AI Diffusion: Evidence from Firms, Business Functions, and Worker Tasks [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Kathryn Bonney, Cory L. Breaux, Emin Dinlersoz, Lucia S. Foster, John C. Haltiwanger, and Aditya A. Pande
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35141)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35141
**Abstract:** This paper provides the first comprehensive, multi-level examination of AI diffusion within U.S. firms, using 2026 Census Bureau survey data collected between November 2025 and January 2026 covering a nationally representative sample of businesses. The authors examine AI adoption simultaneously at three organizational levels: aggregate firm use, deployment across specific business functions, and individual worker task use. Results show that 18% of firms used AI in at least one business function during the reference period, rising to 32% when weighted by employment, with adoption concentrated among large, knowledge-intensive firms in Information, Professional Services, and Finance. Within adopting firms, AI use is highly selective rather than pervasive, with sales, strategy, and IT being the most commonly affected functions. At the worker level, 66% of AI users apply the technology for task augmentation rather than full task replacement, and employment reductions attributable to AI are rare, affecting only 2% of responding firms. Broader functional AI deployment correlates positively with firm performance measures—revenue, employment, and labor productivity—though operationally intensive AI investments show some negative associations with employment levels, suggesting early-stage labor substitution concentrated in specific high-adoption configurations. The paper contributes micro-level evidence on the pace and pattern of AI diffusion, documenting substantial heterogeneity in both adoption and deployment that aggregate statistics obscure.

## 2. The Rise of Large Language Models and the Direction and Impact of US Federal Research Funding [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Yifan Qian, Zhe Wen, Alexander C. Furnas, Yue Bai, Erzhuo Shao, Dashun Wang
**Date:** January 2026; revised April 25, 2026 (arXiv:2601.15485)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15485
**Abstract:** Using confidential NSF and NIH grant proposal submissions from two large U.S. research universities—linked to publicly released award data—the authors provide the first systematic empirical evidence on how large language model (LLM) adoption is reshaping federal research funding. LLM use in proposals increased sharply beginning in 2023 and is measurable via textual indicators including reduced semantic distinctiveness and stylistic homogenization. The effects on funding outcomes are agency-specific: at NIH, higher LLM involvement in proposals is positively associated with both award probability and subsequent publication output, although this publication boost is concentrated in work that does not achieve high citation counts, suggesting LLMs may raise average output while attenuating the production of highly cited, frontier contributions. At NSF, no comparable positive association with award probability or subsequent research impact appears. The authors also show that LLM adoption shifts the direction of proposals toward topics better represented in training data, narrowing the diversity of research themes submitted to federal agencies. The findings raise questions about how AI tools interact with peer review evaluation and whether current funding systems are well-calibrated to distinguish LLM-assisted from LLM-dependent proposals.

## 3. Artificial Intelligence Tools Expand Scientists' Impact but Contract Science's Focus [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Qianyue Hao, Fengli Xu, Yong Li, James A. Evans
**Date:** January 14, 2026 (Nature 649: 1237–1243)
**Source:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09922-y
**Abstract:** Drawing on 41.3 million research papers in the natural sciences spanning distinct eras of AI adoption—machine learning, deep learning, and large language models—the authors document a consistent individual-level productivity premium for AI-augmented researchers alongside a collective narrowing of scientific focus. Scientists engaged in AI-augmented research publish 3.02 times more papers, receive 4.84 times more citations, and advance to research leadership roles 1.37 years earlier than comparable peers who do not adopt AI tools. Despite these individual gains, AI adoption is associated with a 4.63% reduction in the collective volume of scientific topics studied and a 22% decline in scientist-to-scientist engagement, as AI-augmented work gravitates toward areas richest in structured data. The authors interpret this pattern as AI accelerating exploitation of established research domains rather than catalyzing exploration of new scientific territory—an individual-collective paradox in which each scientist benefits but the aggregate knowledge frontier contracts. The paper uses a difference-in-differences design that exploits variation in AI adoption timing across subfields and researcher cohorts, and the findings are replicated across distinct AI adoption waves. The results bear directly on science funding policy and the long-run organization of research: AI tools may raise researcher-level output metrics while homogenizing the scientific agenda and reducing the serendipitous cross-disciplinary connections that produce paradigm-shifting discoveries.

## 4. The Politics of AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Nicholas Bloom and Christos Makridis
**Date:** February 2026; revised March 2026 (NBER Working Paper 34813)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34813
**Abstract:** Using the Gallup Workforce Panel—a nationally representative longitudinal survey of U.S. workers—the authors examine whether the apparent partisan gap in workplace AI adoption reflects genuine ideological differences or compositional sorting by education and occupation. Raw data show that Democrats report meaningfully higher rates of workplace AI use than Republicans (30.1% vs. 25%). However, after controlling for educational attainment, this gap disappears entirely, and after additionally controlling for occupational category, the estimated difference reverses sign. These results indicate that the partisan divide in AI adoption is almost entirely an artifact of Democrats being more likely to hold college-educated, knowledge-worker jobs where AI tools are more prevalent. One important exception: by Q1 2026, a 4.2-percentage-point partisan gap in perceived AI-driven job displacement risk has emerged, a gap that persists after compositional controls, suggesting that beliefs about AI's threat to employment may be diverging along ideological lines even as actual adoption patterns remain driven by human capital and occupational exposure. The paper contributes to debates about AI governance and political economy by clarifying that differential AI exposure across partisan groups is a product of the educational and occupational sorting that characterizes the contemporary U.S. labor market rather than partisan technology preferences.

## 5. AI-Augmented Peer Review and Scientific Productivity: A Cross-Country Panel and SEM Analysis [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Dongsoo Han
**Date:** April 7, 2026 (arXiv:2604.05463)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.05463
**Abstract:** This study provides the first cross-national empirical assessment of how AI-augmented peer review systems affect scientific productivity, using an unbalanced panel of OECD member countries observed from 2000 to the present. The author constructs an AI Review Capability Index (AIRC) that aggregates country-level indicators of AI integration into journal peer review and grant evaluation processes. Panel fixed-effects regression and structural equation modeling are used to estimate effects on three outcomes: research productivity (publications and citations per researcher), reproducibility (retraction rates and replication success), and innovation output (patent filings from academic institutions). The headline finding is that a one standard deviation increase in AIRC is associated with an 18–25% increase in scientific productivity, with effects flowing through two mechanisms: enhanced review efficiency that reduces time-to-decision and time-to-publication, and improved reproducibility standards that increase the citation lifetime of published work. Secondary estimates show heterogeneity by field—STEM disciplines exhibit larger productivity gains than social sciences—and by country development level, with high-income OECD nations capturing proportionally greater benefits. The paper contributes empirical evidence on how institutional adoption of AI in the evaluation of science affects knowledge production at the system level, a question distinct from the individual-level effects that prior literature has emphasized.
