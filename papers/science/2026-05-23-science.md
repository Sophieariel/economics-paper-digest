# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-23

## 1. Knowledge Spillovers and Local Outcomes: An Existence Proof from the Establishment of the National Labs [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Susan Helper, Resem Makan, and Daniel W. Shoag
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35011)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35011
**Abstract:** This paper examines how the creation of U.S. National Laboratories during the 1940s–1950s generated local economic benefits through knowledge spillovers. The study exploits the fact that lab locations were determined by security and political factors rather than existing innovation capacity, allowing causal identification of spillover effects. Counties hosting labs experienced sustained increases in patenting by non-lab inventors, with patents increasingly citing laboratory research. Using newly digitized economic data from 1936–1970, the authors document sustained increases in retail sales and household income. Wage gains appeared for existing residents, particularly college-educated workers. Cohorts exposed during school years attained higher education levels. Notably, these sizable local benefits emerged despite extensive secrecy around early nuclear research, indicating that proximity to public research infrastructure alone can drive meaningful outcomes. The paper contributes causal evidence to a long-standing debate on whether and how government-funded basic research generates local spillovers, with direct relevance for the design of place-based science policy.

## 2. Attention (And Money) Is All You Need: Why Universities Are Struggling to Keep AI Talent [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ufuk Akcigit, Craig A. Chikis, Emin Dinlersoz, and Nathan Goldschlag
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper 34964)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34964
**Abstract:** The authors construct a novel dataset linking academic publication records to U.S. Census Bureau employer–employee administrative records, tracking approximately 42,000 AI researchers over two decades. Their findings reveal a growing university–industry divergence: industry increasingly attracts younger and foreign-born researchers, while gender representation improves more in academia. The analysis documents a dramatic widening of the wage gap between sectors: the top 1% of publishing industry scientists now earn $1.5 million more annually than comparable academics, a fivefold increase since 2001. Researchers transitioning to industry publish less but patent more, consistent with a shift from open science toward proprietary innovation. AI talent is also becoming increasingly concentrated at a small number of large incumbent technology firms. The results have direct implications for the supply of AI knowledge as a public good and for university competitiveness in retaining researchers at the frontier of a strategically important technology.

## 3. Still Waters, Rapid Currents: Early Labor Market Transformation under Generative AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard
**Date:** May 2025; revised March 2026 (NBER Working Paper 33777)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777
**Abstract:** The researchers examine early labor market effects of AI chatbots using Danish adoption surveys linked with administrative records. They find that most employers in exposed occupations have adopted chatbot initiatives and workers report productivity gains. However, earnings and hours worked show precise null effects within two years of ChatGPT's launch, with changes ruled out above 2%. The key finding reveals that employers absorb AI through task reorganization—including new tasks in content generation, AI oversight, and AI integration—rather than through wage cuts or employment reductions. While some workers transition to higher-paying roles where AI is more applicable, the numbers remain too small to affect average earnings. The study uses a large-scale nationally representative employer survey linked to individual-level register data, providing unusually clean identification of adoption and its downstream effects. The evidence suggests that technological change fundamentally restructures work before manifesting in measurable wage or hour impacts, and that productivity gains from AI are being captured primarily by employers rather than workers in the short run.

## 4. Artificial Intelligence, Productivity, and the Workforce: Evidence from Corporate Executives [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Salomé Baslandze, Zachary Edwards, John Graham, Ty McClure, Brent H. Meyer, Michael Sparks, Sonya R. Waddell, and Daniel Weitz
**Date:** March 2026 (NBER Working Paper 34984)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34984
**Abstract:** This paper uses novel survey data from approximately 750 corporate executives to study the effects of artificial intelligence on productivity and the workforce. Adoption rates vary significantly by firm size, with larger firms both adopting more and expecting larger future effects. Productivity improvements are positive but sector-dependent, with the strongest gains concentrated in high-skill services and finance; these gains stem from total factor productivity increases rather than capital deepening, closely associated with innovation- and demand-oriented channels. A disconnect exists between perceived and measured productivity gains. While overall employment effects appear modest, labor composition is shifting, with routine administrative roles declining and demand for technical positions rising. The authors develop an analytical tool identifying job functions most vulnerable to AI disruption. The paper contributes structured executive-level evidence to distinguish channels of AI-driven productivity growth and to characterize which segments of the workforce face the greatest near-term adjustment pressures.

## 5. Forecasting the Economic Effects of AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ezra Karger, Otto Kuusela, Jason Abaluck, Kevin A. Bryan, Basil Halperin, Todd R. Jones, Connacher Murphy, Philip Trammell, Matt Reynolds, Dan Mayland, Ria Viswanathan, Ananaya Mittal, Rebecca Ceppas de Castro, Josh Rosenberg, and Philip Tetlock
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35046)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35046
**Abstract:** This paper elicits predictions from five distinct groups regarding artificial intelligence's influence on the American economy: academic economists, AI company staff, policy researchers specializing in AI, highly accurate superforecasters, and everyday Americans. The median forecast across groups anticipates significant AI capability improvements by 2030, modest labor participation decreases aligned with demographic trends, and annual GDP expansion of 2.5%—exceeding typical government and private-sector baseline projections. Under a "rapid" AI advancement scenario where systems achieve superhuman capabilities across numerous cognitive and physical domains, experts project substantial economic transformation: GDP growth accelerating to roughly 4% annually and labor participation contracting from the current 62% to 55% by 2050, with approximately half the decline—roughly 10 million positions—stemming from AI. Disagreement among experts predominantly reflects divergent assessments of highly capable AI's economic consequences rather than differing perspectives on AI development speed. Policy preferences vary substantially: experts prioritize worker retraining initiatives, while the general public endorses both targeted assistance and comprehensive measures like guaranteed employment and unconditional income programs. The paper contributes a structured elicitation methodology that quantifies uncertainty and identifies the specific beliefs driving disagreement about AI's macroeconomic trajectory.
