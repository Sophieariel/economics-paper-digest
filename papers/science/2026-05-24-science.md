# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-24

## 1. Gender Equality and Innovation: Evidence from UK Companies [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pawel Bukowski and Hannah Yi Wei
**Date:** May 2026 (CEP Discussion Paper No. 2179)
**Source:** https://cep.lse.ac.uk/pubs/download/dp2179.pdf
**Abstract:** This paper asks whether within-firm gender inequality—measured by both the gender pay gap and female representation at the top of the wage hierarchy—affects firms' capacity to innovate, as measured by patenting outcomes. The authors construct two unique firm-level datasets: a short panel of large UK companies from 2017 to 2019, which links mandatory gender pay gap disclosures introduced under the UK Equality Act to detailed patent application and grant records, and a longer panel of mid- and large-sized UK firms from 1996 to 2017 covering board-level female representation and patenting activity over two decades. Patent performance is assessed across three dimensions: the headcount of filings, the citation-weighted quality of patents, and the economic value of patent portfolios. The headline finding is that gender parity does not negatively affect innovation outcomes—a result robust to sector and firm-size controls. However, analysis of the more recent 2017–2019 panel, which covers a period of rapidly expanding female representation in top roles, reveals a statistically significant adverse effect of gender inequality on both the quantity and quality of patents: firms with larger gender pay gaps and lower female representation near the top of their wage structures produce fewer patents and patents of lower citation quality. The authors provide evidence that this effect operates through female representation at the upper rungs of the within-firm wage hierarchy, rather than through board-level diversity metrics, suggesting that the innovation dividend from gender equality is concentrated in operational and managerial roles with direct influence over R&D rather than in boardroom composition. The findings inform policy debates about whether gender pay gap reporting mandates and diversity requirements complement or crowd out private investment in innovation.

## 2. Tracking Artificial Intelligence Sentiment in the U.S. Labor Market [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Yuye Ding, Mark (Shuai) Ma, Jun Wu, and Yucheng Yang
**Date:** April 26, 2026 (SSRN Working Paper No. 6652799)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6652799
**Abstract:** This paper develops a comprehensive, multi-source measurement framework for tracking how employees, managers, and firms perceive and respond to artificial intelligence in the U.S. labor market. The authors assemble four large-scale datasets spanning 2021 to 2025: employee job reviews from Glassdoor, executive disclosures from earnings conference calls, individual career profiles from LinkedIn, and firm-level job postings. Applying natural language processing and textual sentiment analysis to each dataset, the authors document a persistent and widening gap between employee and managerial sentiment toward AI: employee sentiment toward AI is significantly more negative than their overall sentiment toward their employers, while management AI sentiment is highly optimistic and forward-looking. Despite this divergence in perceived affect, the paper's central empirical finding is a strong positive association between firm-level productivity and employee AI sentiment—not management AI sentiment—suggesting that worker acceptance and engagement with AI tools is a critical intermediate input in realizing AI-driven productivity gains. Management's optimism, by contrast, does not independently predict productivity improvements after conditioning on employee sentiment, pointing to a potential gap between C-suite expectations and shop-floor implementation realities. The paper also documents that AI sentiment varies substantially across industries, occupations, and firm types, with sentiment most positive in technology and finance and most negative in administrative and services occupations. These patterns provide granular, real-time evidence on the human-side barriers to AI adoption and suggest that the productivity premium from AI investments depends as much on organizational culture and employee buy-in as on the technology itself.

## 3. Parenthood and the Career Ladder: Evidence from Academia [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Sofie Cairo, Ria Ivandic, Anne Sophie Lassen, and Valentina Tartari
**Date:** 2026 (CEP Discussion Paper No. 2160)
**Source:** https://ideas.repec.org/p/cep/cepdps/dp2160.html
**Abstract:** This paper examines how the arrival of children reshapes the career trajectories of academic researchers, with particular attention to gender asymmetries in the consequences of parenthood for employment, tenure, and research output. Using comprehensive administrative data linked to publication and grant records, the authors estimate the causal effects of first childbirth on multiple career outcomes for academic scientists and scholars. The headline finding is that approximately one in three women exit academia following the birth of their first child, while men experience substantially smaller and shorter-lived employment disruptions. Mothers who remain in academia face persistent and compounding penalties: lower rates of tenure and promotion, measurable reductions in annual publication output, and reduced grant success rates—penalties that persist for at least a decade post-birth. Fatherhood, by contrast, does not significantly alter men's publication productivity, tenure trajectories, or grant outcomes, confirming a sharp parenthood penalty asymmetry. The paper identifies two primary mechanisms: the differential burden of childcare responsibilities on women and the geographic inflexibility of academic job markets, which constrains women's ability to relocate for opportunities while managing household duties. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that the parenthood penalty intensifies in highly competitive academic environments—those with longer pre-tenure periods and more demanding publication norms—and is reduced, but not eliminated, at institutions with higher shares of senior female faculty, suggesting role-model effects that partially offset structural disadvantages. The findings contribute causal evidence to the economics-of-science literature on the scientist labor market and carry implications for the design of parental leave, childcare support, and flexible work policies in research universities.

## 4. AI, Output, and Employment [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Andrew C. Johnston and Christos Makridis
**Date:** SSRN Working Paper No. 5375017 (updated through 2024 data; coverage in ProMarket, May 21, 2026)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5375017
**Abstract:** This paper provides large-scale empirical evidence on how artificial intelligence exposure affects output, employment, and wages across U.S. industries and states, using administrative data covering essentially all U.S. private-sector employers over the period 2017–2024. The identification strategy is a difference-in-differences design that exploits variation in occupational AI exposure—constructed from task-content measures combined with AI capability assessments—across industries and states, with identification sharpened by state-by-year and industry-by-year fixed effects. The central finding is that a one standard deviation increase in AI exposure is associated with a 10% gain in output, a 3.9% increase in employment, and a 4.8% rise in the total wage bill by 2024—effects that emerge clearly beginning in 2021, when enterprise AI tools entered widespread commercial deployment, and that predate the public release of ChatGPT, suggesting the productivity channel is not limited to generative AI. Critically, the paper distinguishes between augmentative AI—applications that require ongoing human collaboration to generate value—and automating AI—applications capable of executing tasks largely without human input. In augmentative sectors, employment rises approximately 4% per standard deviation and wage growth is robust; in automating sectors, employment shows no statistically significant change, and wage growth is muted. The labor-share implications are adverse: workers captured approximately 29 cents of every dollar of AI-driven output growth, with the remainder accruing to capital owners. Distributional analysis reveals that wage gains were concentrated among college graduates (1.4%), workers aged 22–30 (1.4%), Black workers (1.4%), and female workers (1.1%). The positive employment effects of AI exposure are concentrated in states with higher labor-market fluidity, suggesting that local labor-market institutions are important moderators of AI's employment impact.

## 5. Early AI Adoption and Firm Productivity Growth in a Middle-Income Economy [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Juan Duran-Vanegas
**Date:** April 3, 2026 (SSRN Working Paper No. 6515438)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6515438
**Abstract:** Most empirical evidence on AI and firm productivity derives from high-income economies with advanced digital infrastructure, leaving the experience of middle-income countries largely uncharted. This paper fills that gap by examining the productivity consequences of early AI adoption among Colombian manufacturing firms, exploiting the 2019 Colombian Enterprise ICT Survey linked longitudinally to establishment-level manufacturing census records spanning 2016 to 2019. The ICT survey identifies firms that adopted AI-enabled tools—including machine learning, computer vision, and natural language processing—in their production and business processes, providing a cross-sectional adoption measure that is combined with the longitudinal productivity data to estimate the dynamic effects of adoption timing. The main finding is that AI adoption is associated with a 16 percent cumulative increase in labor productivity over the three-year post-adoption window, equivalent to approximately 5 percent annualized growth, a magnitude comparable to estimates from U.S. and European contexts despite Colombia's substantially lower baseline levels of digital intensity. The paper documents substantial firm-level heterogeneity: productivity gains are largest among medium-sized firms that combine AI adoption with complementary investments in worker training and data infrastructure, while small firms and those without pre-existing ICT capabilities show muted or statistically insignificant effects. The evidence is consistent with a complementarity view of AI adoption in which the technology's productivity payoff depends on organizational and human capital prerequisites, and suggests that productivity spillovers from AI are accessible to firms in middle-income economies provided those complementary conditions are met. The findings contribute to a growing cross-country literature on AI diffusion and have implications for industrial policy in emerging economies seeking to leverage AI as a tool for productivity catch-up.
