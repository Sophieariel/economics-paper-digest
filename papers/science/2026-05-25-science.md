# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-25

## 1. Estimating the Present Value of R&D Tax Benefits in the United States [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Brandon Pecoraro, Nicholas C. Hoffman, Martin Lopez-Daneri, Elena C. Derby, Rachel Moore, and Shannon E. Sledz
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35208)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35208
**Abstract:** This paper produces the first comprehensive, firm-level estimates of the present value of R&D tax benefits in the United States, using confidential corporate tax return microdata linked to R&D expenditure records. The authors calculate realized tax benefits—combining the R&D tax credit and the deductibility of R&D expenditures—and discount them to present value to capture the timing mismatch between when firms spend on R&D and when they actually use the tax benefits. The headline finding is that firms generated $0.41 in statutory tax benefits per dollar of R&D spending on average, but the true present value was lower at $0.36, reflecting the delay in benefit realization. Crucially, the paper documents extreme heterogeneity by firm characteristics: young, small firms operating at a loss realized only $0.23 per dollar of R&D—a 44% discount relative to the statutory benchmark—because loss-making firms cannot immediately offset tax liabilities and must carry credits forward to future profitable years, which reduces their present value. Large, established firms realize benefits close to the statutory level by using them immediately. The results have direct implications for evaluating the effectiveness of R&D tax policy: current incentives are disproportionately weaker for the young, innovative firms they are ostensibly designed to attract, raising questions about whether the tax code as implemented achieves its stated goal of stimulating frontier research investment.

## 2. Old Space, New Space: A Commercial Revolution in Innovation? [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ruben Gaetani and Alexander T. Whalley
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35212)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35212
**Abstract:** The popular narrative of a "New Space" revolution—led by SpaceX, Blue Origin, and other commercial entrants displacing legacy aerospace incumbents—implies a sharp break in the organization and locus of space-sector innovation. This paper evaluates that claim systematically using comprehensive patent data spanning the post-Apollo era to the present. The central finding challenges the narrative: the largest surge in space-sector innovation occurred in the 1990s, coinciding with government-created demand-side market conditions and predating the prominent startup entries of the post-2005 period. Established aerospace firms maintained dominant shares of space-related patenting throughout the sample, with newer entrants playing an expanding but still secondary role even in recent years. Geographic centers of innovation have remained largely stable since the Apollo era, with regions prominent in the early space program continuing to lead today. The paper interprets these patterns through the lens of directed technical change theory: incumbent firms redirect existing capabilities toward policy-generated market opportunities, while genuine entrants introduce novel scientific approaches in niches demanding fresh knowledge bases. The findings suggest that government market-creation activities—rather than private capital flows to startups—may represent the primary lever for expanding the space innovation frontier. More broadly, the paper contributes to the economics of science and innovation by providing causal evidence on how demand-side policy interacts with incumbent versus entrant firm dynamics in high-technology sectors.

## 3. Does Generative AI Narrow Education-Based Productivity Gaps? Evidence from a Randomized Experiment [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Guillermo Cruces, Diego Fernández Meijide, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro H. Gálvez, and María Lombardi
**Date:** February 2026; revised May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 34851)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34851
**Abstract:** This paper uses a pre-registered randomized online experiment with 1,174 adults aged 25–45 to estimate how access to a generative AI assistant affects productivity across workers with heterogeneous educational attainment. Participants were randomly assigned to complete a workplace-style business problem-solving task either with or without AI assistance, allowing clean identification of AI's causal effect on task performance at different points of the human-capital distribution. The key result is that generative AI raises performance for all participants, but the gains are substantially larger for lower-education workers. The education-based performance gap—measured as the difference in standardized task scores between college-educated and non-college workers—shrank from 0.548 standard deviations in the no-AI condition to 0.139 standard deviations with AI access, a reduction of approximately three-quarters. Analysis of interaction logs reveals that lower-education workers obtained high-value assistance from the AI on tasks they would otherwise struggle with, while higher-education workers tended to use AI for iterative refinement. A key robustness check examines whether gains persist after AI removal: follow-up testing shows that the compressed gap partially re-emerges when AI is withdrawn, indicating that AI largely substitutes for—rather than builds—human capital for lower-education users, though some persistent learning is documented. The findings speak directly to debates about AI and inequality: generative AI appears to function as an equalizer in task execution in the short run, but the persistence of underlying human-capital differences means this compression may erode if access to AI tools becomes uneven.

## 4. Deep Research on a Loop: Using AI Agents to Construct Economic Datasets [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Santiago Afonso, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro H. Gálvez, and Raul A. Sosa
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35188)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35188
**Abstract:** This paper introduces DRIL (Deep Research on a Loop), a methodology that deploys large language model agents to automate the assembly of economic datasets from publicly available sources. The approach separates the research design phase—in which a human economist defines the conceptual unit space (e.g., countries by years) and the research instrument—from the implementation phase, which is executed by AI agents querying sources, extracting data, and populating the dataset. The authors demonstrate the methodology by constructing an updated Global Tax Expenditures Database covering eight Latin American and Caribbean countries, producing 129 primary sources and 136 verified evidence records at a fraction of the cost of traditional research-assistant-based data collection. The paper validates the AI-assembled data against existing manually compiled benchmarks and documents the error rates and source coverage achieved. The central empirical claim is that DRIL meaningfully reduces the marginal cost of dataset construction for variables requiring systematic document review—precisely the inputs that constrain the scope of empirical economics research on lower-income countries and niche policy domains where structured databases do not exist. The paper contributes to the literature on AI and scientific productivity by providing a worked example of AI-augmented knowledge production that is replicable, auditable, and governed by economist-specified research design, offering a model for how AI can expand the empirical frontier of economics without displacing the interpretive and design judgment that economists supply.

## 5. Generative AI and Firm Hiring Demand: Evidence from Advanced and Developing Economies [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Faezeh Khosravi and Elaine M. Liu
**Date:** April 2026 (SSRN Working Paper 6537038)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6537038
**Abstract:** This paper studies how generative AI reshapes labor demand at the firm level, using over 250 million online job postings across advanced and developing economies and exploiting ChatGPT's release in November 2022 as an exogenous shock. The identification strategy constructs a predetermined firm-level AI exposure measure based on each firm's 2019 occupational composition interacted with occupation-level AI substitution scores, and estimates a continuous difference-in-differences design that compares hiring dynamics for high- versus low-exposure firms before and after the ChatGPT shock. The paper contributes a cross-country comparative design that distinguishes advanced from developing economy responses. In advanced economies, the reduction in posting volumes for AI-substitutable vacancies builds gradually, beginning three to four quarters after ChatGPT's release, with compositional shifts—fewer high-substitution postings relative to low-substitution ones—emerging somewhat later. In developing economies, the decline in posting volumes begins earlier in the event window, suggesting faster reallocation, with cleaner evidence on the compositional margin than on aggregate posting volumes. In both groups, more exposed firms reduce the share of high-substitution postings and lower the AI-substitution intensity of their vacancy mix over time, consistent with firms actively reallocating hiring demand away from occupations where generative AI is a viable substitute. The results are robust to controls for macroeconomic conditions, industry-level trends, and firm size, and are not driven by differential firm-level investment in AI infrastructure. The findings imply that generative AI is already reshaping the structure of labor demand globally, with the adjustment occurring faster and in qualitatively distinct ways in developing economies—a pattern with significant implications for labor market policy and worker transition programs in lower-income countries.
