# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-26

## 1. Growth is Getting Harder to Find, Not Ideas [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Teresa C. Fort, Nathan Goldschlag, Jack Liang, Peter K. Schott, and Nikolas Zolas
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35182)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35182
**Abstract:** A prominent strand of the growth literature argues that the economy is running out of new ideas—that it takes ever-more research inputs to achieve a given increment of total factor productivity growth. This paper challenges that interpretation using a comprehensive 45-year panel of U.S. firm patents matched to R&D expenditure data from the Annual Survey of Manufactures and the Business R&D and Innovation Survey. The authors document that average patents per unit of R&D input are increasing over the sample period and find no systematic long-run decline in patenting once research investments are properly accounted for. Their central finding is that the apparent slowdown in economic growth is not attributable to declining idea production at the firm level: firms' innovative efforts continue to drive productivity gains with undiminished effectiveness, and the number of new ideas reaching the patent system per dollar of R&D has not fallen. Instead, the paper locates the bottleneck in the conversion of innovation into broad-based firm and aggregate growth: firm growth rates decline even after controlling for patent output, suggesting that the constraints binding economic expansion lie downstream of the idea-generation stage—in commercialization, diffusion, and market structure—rather than in the innovation process itself. The findings have significant implications for the economics of science and technology policy: if ideas are not in fact getting harder to find, prescriptions for expanding the research workforce or intensifying R&D subsidies on the grounds of idea scarcity require reexamination, and attention should shift toward the organizational and market frictions that impede translating innovation into growth.

## 2. Old Space, New Space: A Commercial Revolution in Innovation? [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ruben Gaetani and Alexander T. Whalley
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35212)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35212
**Abstract:** The rise of private space ventures such as SpaceX and Blue Origin has generated a popular narrative of a sharp discontinuity between an "Old Space" era dominated by government contractors and a "New Space" era driven by entrepreneurial entrants. This paper uses comprehensive patent data spanning the entire history of the commercial space industry to test whether the evidence supports that narrative. The authors find that the largest surge in space-related innovation activity occurred in the 1990s—predating the emergence of high-profile startups by a decade—and coincided precisely with a series of demand-side regulatory and market-creation policies enacted by the federal government, including FCC spectrum allocation for commercial satellites, the 1984 Commercial Space Launch Act, and licensing rules enabling commercial satellite imagery and constellation operations. Throughout this period and since, incumbent aerospace manufacturers—large, established contractors such as Boeing and Lockheed Martin—have continued to account for the majority of space-related patenting, with entrants contributing a growing but minority share. Geographic regions historically central to space innovation remain dominant today, suggesting strong path dependence in the spatial distribution of innovative activity. The findings reframe the standard account of the commercial space revolution: rather than a story of entrepreneurial disruption of government incumbents, the data support a story in which government-created markets generated private returns to R&D investment that subsequently attracted both incumbents and entrants. The paper contributes to the literature on the returns to R&D, demand-side innovation policy, and the role of procurement and market-making in directing innovative activity.

## 3. Generative AI and the Reorganization of Labor Demand [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Fangyan Wang, Zaiyan Wei, and Yang Wang
**Date:** May 22, 2026 (arXiv:2605.23159)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23159
**Abstract:** This paper examines how firms reorganize labor demand as generative AI technology diffuses through the economy, going beyond the binary question of which occupations are exposed to AI to study the organizational mechanisms through which adjustment occurs. Using a nationwide dataset of U.S. job postings matched to a two-stage language model pipeline that constructs a dynamic, task-level measure of generative AI exposure, the authors document that AI exposure itself changes over time as firms adapt—it is not a static property of occupations but evolves as job descriptions and hiring requirements are revised. The central finding is that firms adjust to generative AI through two distinct channels: hiring reallocation, which accounts for the largest share of the aggregate decline in AI-task exposure and explains approximately 52 percent of the total adjustment on average, and within-job redesign—the reorganization of task content within existing job titles—which accounts for approximately 39.5 percent and becomes increasingly important over time. These two mechanisms operate heterogeneously by worker seniority: senior-level positions shift their hiring patterns earlier in response to AI exposure, while junior-level roles employ broader adjustment strategies that span both channels. The results imply that standard occupation-level AI exposure measures miss a substantial portion of adjustment that occurs through internal reorganization of work rather than changes in hiring mix. The paper contributes to the literature on generative AI and labor demand by providing micro-level evidence on the organizational pathways through which AI reshapes jobs, with implications for forecasting displacement versus augmentation at different career stages.

## 4. What Jobs Can AI Learn? Measuring Exposure by Reinforcement Learning [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Philip Moreira Tomei and Bouke Klein Teeselink
**Date:** May 4, 2026 (arXiv:2605.02598)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.02598
**Abstract:** Existing measures of occupational AI exposure assess which tasks current AI systems can already perform, conflating present capability with the potential for AI to acquire new skills through learning. This paper develops a distinct measure—the Reinforcement Learning (RL) Feasibility Index—that captures which occupational tasks are learnable by AI via reinforcement learning, independently of what AI can do today. The authors use large language model annotators guided by a rubric developed with reinforcement learning experts to evaluate all 17,951 tasks recorded in the O*NET database for their suitability as RL training environments, focusing on criteria such as the availability of a reward signal, environmental steerability, and the feasibility of simulated practice. The resulting index reveals striking divergences from conventional AI exposure indices: roles such as power plant operators, railroad conductors, and aircraft cargo handling supervisors rank highly on RL learnability but low on standard AI exposure measures, while creative and interpersonal roles—musicians, physicians, natural sciences managers—show the opposite pattern. These divergences carry policy implications: workers in occupations conventionally classified as low-risk on current-capability metrics may face near-future displacement as AI systems improve through practice, while some occupations flagged as high-risk by current measures may prove more durable. The RL Feasibility Index thus complements existing exposure measures by capturing forward-looking automation risk rather than present-tense substitutability, offering a tool for tracking how the boundary of AI capability is likely to evolve as learning-based AI systems mature.

## 5. Generative AI Fuels Solo Entrepreneurship, but Teams Still Lead at the Top [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Hyunso Kim, Hyo Kang, and Jaeyong Song
**Date:** May 11, 2026 (arXiv:2605.10291)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10291
**Abstract:** This paper studies how the availability of generative AI tools affects entrepreneurial entry and market success, using the release of ChatGPT-3.5 in November 2022 as a quasi-natural experiment. The authors assemble data on over 160,000 product launches on Product Hunt, a major platform for early-stage software and technology products, and exploit the timing of the ChatGPT release in a difference-in-differences framework that compares product categories with differential exposure to generative AI capabilities. The central finding is that solo entrepreneurs—defined as teams of size one—increased their product launch activity sharply and persistently following the AI release, particularly in product categories where team collaboration and breadth of skill had previously been most important. This democratization of entry is interpreted as evidence that generative AI tools lower the fixed costs of product development that had previously made solo ventures uncompetitive with teams in skill-intensive categories. However, the paper documents a counteracting force at the top of the success distribution: team-based ventures grow increasingly dominant in the upper tiers of Product Hunt rankings after the ChatGPT release, suggesting that while generative AI enables solo founders to enter and compete at the median, it does not equalize outcomes at the frontier, where coordinating human capital and complementary expertise continues to confer a decisive advantage. The findings contribute to the economics of AI and entrepreneurship by providing causal evidence that generative AI democratizes entry but reinforces, rather than eliminates, quality stratification across team configurations.
