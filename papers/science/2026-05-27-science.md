# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-27

## 1. Will The University Endowment Tax Slow Scientific Progress? Evidence from Elite Economics PhD Programs [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Joshua Angrist, Marc Diederichs, and Glenn Ellison
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35244)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35244
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether the 2025 university endowment tax, by tightening financial constraints at elite institutions, could reduce graduate enrollment and depress long-run scientific output. The authors use MIT Economics PhD applicants as a novel dataset to estimate the research value-added of the top eight U.S. doctoral programs, controlling for admissions committee rankings and applicant-stated preferences to mitigate selection bias. Their central finding is that graduates of high-tax and other top-eight schools produce 60–75% more impact-adjusted publications than comparable graduates of non-top-eight U.S. programs. The elite advantage is especially pronounced for top-tier journal publications; differences within the elite tier are more modest. The authors rule out editorial connections and peer effects as sole explanations, pointing instead to some combination of mentorship quality, institutional resources, and co-author networks as the source of the premium. The results imply that endowment-tax-driven enrollment cuts at elite programs would reduce the long-run supply of high-impact researchers, raising the private cost of science policy changes enacted through the university tax code rather than through direct research-funding appropriations.

## 2. Estimating the Present Value of R&D Tax Benefits in the United States [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Brandon Pecoraro, Nicholas C. Hoffman, Martin Lopez-Daneri, Elena C. Derby, Rachel Moore, and Shannon E. Sledz
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35208)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35208
**Abstract:** Using confidential corporate income tax return data, this paper delivers the first direct estimates of the realized present value of corporate tax benefits from R&D credits and deductions in the United States. The authors develop a novel firm-level tracking procedure that follows the utilization of R&D tax attributes—credits and deductions—across time as firms carry them forward through periods of insufficient taxable income. From 2012–2016, firms generated an average of $0.41 in statutory tax benefits per dollar of R&D investment, with a realized present value of $0.36 once discounting and utilization lags are accounted for. Young and small firms in a loss position realized only $0.23 per dollar, a 44% shortfall relative to the statutory benchmark, because they cannot immediately offset current-year taxes and must carry forward attributes whose value is eroded by discounting. The results document that the effective R&D subsidy rate is substantially lower than the nominal rate for precisely the early-stage firms most likely to be on the margin of investment decisions, with implications for the optimal design of R&D tax policy and for empirical work that uses statutory rates as proxies for effective incentives.

## 3. Growth is Getting Harder to Find, Not Ideas [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Teresa C. Fort, Nathan Goldschlag, Jack Liang, Peter K. Schott, and Nikolas Zolas
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35182)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35182
**Abstract:** This paper re-examines the hypothesis that ideas are becoming harder to find by constructing a 45-year linked panel of U.S. firm patents, R&D spending, and labor productivity. The authors find that average patents per unit of R&D input are increasing, and that the patent elasticity to research inputs has remained flat or risen over the sample period—evidence directly at odds with the "ideas-becoming-scarcer" account. They further document a positive, significant, and fairly steady relationship between firms' patent and labor productivity growth rates, confirming that innovation continues to translate into productivity at the firm level. The puzzle, however, is that average firm output growth, conditional on patent growth, has declined over time. The bottleneck thus lies not in the production of new ideas but in translating those innovations into economic expansion—a distinction with direct policy implications. Rather than subsidizing R&D inputs to counteract declining research productivity, policy should address commercialization barriers, technology diffusion frictions, and market-structure features that impede the translation of innovative activity into revenue and employment growth.

## 4. Preference for Explainable AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Alex Chan
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35240)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35240
**Abstract:** This paper reports a field experiment in which loan officers make real $10,000 lending decisions assisted by an AI default-risk prediction tool, with random variation in whether AI explanations are provided and in the nature of the bonus scheme. When explanations revealed that the AI had relied on the race or gender of the applicant, officers were more likely to override the AI's recommendation. However, when officer bonuses were conditioned on loan repayment performance, officers actively sought the AI's predictions while deliberately avoiding explanations—behavior consistent with willful ignorance, a strategy of preserving plausible deniability while acting on discriminatory algorithmic signals. This avoidance weakened when explanations were framed in purely financial terms or when demographic information was obscured. A complementary incentivized experiment shows that agents systematically undervalue AI explanations even when those explanations carry information complementary to their own private signals, failing to reason contingently about when explanation-seeking would improve their decisions. The results bear on the design and regulation of explainable AI: mandatory explanation requirements may alter behavior more through social-accountability than informational channels, and aligning incentive structures may be as important as disclosure rules in governing how AI-assisted decisions treat protected classes.

## 5. Forecasting the Economic Effects of AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ezra Karger, Otto Kuusela, Jason Abaluck, Kevin A. Bryan, Basil Halperin, Todd R. Jones, Connacher Murphy, Philip Trammell, Matt Reynolds, Dan Mayland, Ria Viswanathan, Ananaya Mittal, Rebecca Ceppas de Castro, Josh Rosenberg, and Philip Tetlock
**Date:** April 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35046)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35046
**Abstract:** This paper elicits structured probabilistic forecasts of AI's macroeconomic impact from five groups: academic economists, AI company employees, AI policy researchers, highly accurate superforecasters, and the general public. All groups anticipate substantial advances in AI capabilities by 2030, modest declines in labor force participation, and GDP growth of approximately 2.5% annually under baseline conditions. Under a "rapid AI progress" scenario, median forecasts project GDP growth accelerating to roughly 4% and labor force participation falling to 55% by 2050, with approximately half the employment decline attributable to AI—equivalent to on the order of 10 million displaced workers. Expert disagreement about aggregate effects is driven primarily by divergent beliefs about the economic consequences of highly capable systems rather than by disagreement about the pace of capability progress itself. On policy responses, expert groups favor targeted interventions—particularly worker retraining—while the general public disproportionately endorses broader redistributive measures such as job guarantees and universal basic income. The paper's structured multi-group elicitation design separates capability forecasts from impact forecasts, providing a replicable baseline for future forecast evaluation and for identifying the specific belief dimensions on which expert and lay views diverge.
