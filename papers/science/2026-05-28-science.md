# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-28

## 1. Growth is Getting Harder to Find, Not Ideas [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Teresa C. Fort, Nathan Goldschlag, Jack Liang, Peter K. Schott, and Nikolas Zolas
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35182)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35182
**Abstract:** A central puzzle in modern growth accounting is why aggregate productivity growth has remained subdued despite rising R&D expenditure. This paper revisits that puzzle using a 45-year panel of U.S. firm patenting and financial data, decomposing the apparent innovation–growth gap into a knowledge-generation component and a knowledge-to-growth conversion component. The authors find that the knowledge-generation margin is not the source of the slowdown: average patents per dollar of R&D input are increasing over the sample period, and the elasticity of patenting to R&D inputs is flat or rising, directly contradicting the "idea scarcity" hypothesis associated with prior research. Instead, the paper documents that while a consistent and economically significant positive relationship exists between firm-level patent growth and subsequent labor productivity gains, average firm output and employment growth conditional on patent growth has declined systematically over the sample horizon. The results point to a structural change in the mechanism through which innovation translates into market expansion—the conversion of knowledge into commercial scale—rather than a slowdown in the production of ideas itself. The authors propose that increased product market concentration, shorter product life cycles, and the rising cost of reaching customers in digital markets may all contribute to the widening gap between firms' innovative capacity and their realized growth trajectories. The findings reframe the secular productivity slowdown as a commercialization problem rather than a creativity problem, with distinct implications for R&D policy, antitrust enforcement, and the design of innovation incentives.

## 2. Estimating the Present Value of R&D Tax Benefits in the United States [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Brandon Pecoraro, Nicholas C. Hoffman, Martin Lopez-Daneri, Elena C. Derby, Rachel Moore, and Shannon E. Sledz
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35208)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35208
**Abstract:** The United States R&D tax credit and accelerated R&D deduction regime are among the federal government's largest instruments for stimulating private-sector research investment, yet their actual value to firms has never been measured directly from administrative data. This paper provides the first such estimates, using a confidential panel of corporate income tax returns from 2012 to 2016 linked to R&D expenditure data. The authors calculate the present-value tax benefit per dollar of qualifying R&D spending for the full population of U.S. corporations, accounting for the timing of credit and deduction utilization, carryforward provisions, the alternative minimum tax, and firm-specific discount rates. The headline finding is that the average present-value benefit is $0.36 per dollar of R&D—substantially below the statutory-rate benchmark of $0.41—reflecting the significant fraction of firms that cannot immediately use their tax attributes due to current-period losses or alternative minimum tax liability. Heterogeneity in realized benefits is striking: young firms in their first five years of operation that are currently loss-making receive only $0.23 per dollar, while established profitable incumbents capture close to or above the statutory rate. Size also matters, with very small firms receiving systematically lower present-value benefits than large corporations due to their higher probability of loss status and longer expected carryforward delays. These results have direct implications for the design of R&D tax policy: the marginal cost of a dollar of R&D subsidy to the government does not equal the marginal benefit to the firm, and this gap is largest for the early-stage firms that policy is ostensibly designed to encourage, pointing toward refundability provisions or direct grant mechanisms as potentially more efficient instruments for stimulating early-stage research.

## 3. Will The University Endowment Tax Slow Scientific Progress? Evidence from Elite Economics PhD Programs [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Joshua Angrist, Marc Diederichs, and Glenn Ellison
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35244)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35244
**Abstract:** The 2025 federal endowment tax on private university investment returns raised the cost of operating elite research universities and sparked debate about whether fiscal pressure on higher education institutions would reduce graduate program quality and downstream research output. This paper provides the first rigorous empirical evidence on the research value-added of elite economics PhD programs, using detailed administrative data on MIT Economics PhD applicants—including standardized test scores, undergraduate records, and subsequent publication histories—to construct selection-corrected measures of research productivity by program. The authors estimate program-specific value-added by comparing the research output of graduates to the predicted output of matched applicants who enrolled elsewhere. The key finding is that graduates of the eight highest-endowment economics programs produce 60 to 75 percent more impact-adjusted publications over the decade following graduation than do observationally comparable graduates of non-top-eight U.S. programs, and this advantage is concentrated in publications in the five highest-ranked general-interest journals. Within the top tier, differences in value-added are modest, suggesting that the marginal graduate routed away from a top-two program into a top-eight alternative would suffer only a small research output penalty. The elite production advantage does not appear to be attributable solely to editorial favoritism or peer network effects—it persists in citation-based measures that are less susceptible to editorial influence. The results suggest that endowment tax burdens large enough to compress graduate cohorts or reduce research infrastructure at affected institutions could meaningfully slow the production of leading economists, with downstream effects on the pace and direction of economic research.

## 4. Hiring Up, Not Down: Generative AI and the Composition of Labor Demand [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Jeroen Mahieu
**Date:** May 14, 2026 (SSRN Working Paper 6467778)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6467778
**Abstract:** A prominent concern about generative AI is that it will disproportionately displace entry-level workers whose tasks are most automatable. This paper provides the first quasi-experimental evidence on how generative AI adoption reshapes the experience-tier composition of labor demand, using administrative vacancy data from Flanders, Belgium, covering 2021 through 2025. The identification strategy exploits variation across occupations in their predetermined exposure to generative AI, defined prior to the widespread availability of large language models, in a difference-in-differences design that compares within-occupation vacancy patterns across three experience tiers—entry-level, mid-level, and experienced—around the adoption of generative AI tools. The central finding is a stark asymmetry: moving from the 25th to the 75th percentile of generative AI exposure at peak adoption is associated with an approximately 23% reduction in entry-level vacancies within an occupation, while the coefficient on experienced-tier vacancies is small and positive. The pooled, experience-averaged estimate is smaller and statistically imprecise, indicating that standard studies examining total vacancy counts would miss the compositional reallocation that is the paper's main finding. The author interprets this pattern as evidence that rigid labor market institutions—including Belgian employment protection legislation that constrains firms' ability to reduce headcounts through layoffs—channel the employment adjustment induced by AI adoption toward the hiring margin rather than the separation margin, with the burden falling disproportionately on labor-market entrants who lack the protection of existing employment relationships. The results are consistent with generative AI automating the routine cognitive tasks typically assigned to junior workers, raising the effective experience threshold for firm entry and contributing to the hollowing out of entry-level career pathways in AI-exposed occupations.

## 5. Preference for Explainable AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Alex Chan
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35240)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35240
**Abstract:** Regulatory proposals for AI explainability requirements rest on the assumption that decision-makers will use algorithmic explanations when they are available, yet the behavioral economics of explanation use by AI-assisted workers is not well understood. This paper reports the results of two laboratory experiments in which participants make real decisions about $10,000 loans using AI-generated default-risk predictions, with variation in the availability and framing of explanations for those predictions. The first experiment examines how decision-makers respond when explanations reveal that the AI system exhibits discriminatory bias against non-White or female borrowers: participants who received such explanations were substantially more likely to override the AI's recommendation, suggesting that transparency about algorithmic bias activates normative concerns that constrain AI uptake. However, the paper's central finding is that when financial incentives are tied directly to loan repayment outcomes—aligning participants' incentives with accuracy—a large share of participants actively avoided accessing available explanations, a behavior the author characterizes as "willful ignorance." This avoidance is rational if explanations primarily reveal demographic information that triggers normative costs without improving prediction accuracy, but the second experiment shows it is at least partly a cognitive error: participants consistently underestimated the informational value of explanations even when those explanations were presented in purely financial terms and demographics were concealed, failing to reason contingently about how explanations could complement their own private signals. The results have direct implications for AI governance: mandatory explainability provisions may be circumvented when decision-makers face performance-based incentives, and the welfare effects of transparency mandates depend critically on whether users can recognize and exploit the informational content of AI explanations.
