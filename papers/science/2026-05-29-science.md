# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-29

## 1. Generative AI and the Reorganization of Labor Demand [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Fangyan Wang, Zaiyan Wei, Yang Wang
**Date:** May 22, 2026 (arXiv:2605.23159)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23159
**Abstract:** Using a nationwide dataset of job postings across all economic sectors in the United States, this paper investigates how firms reorganize labor demand as generative AI technology diffuses. Rather than focusing solely on job displacement, the authors decompose changes in aggregate AI exposure into two margins: reallocation of demand across jobs and redesign of tasks within existing positions. Their computational method assesses task-level AI substitutability at scale and documents that AI exposure is not static but fluctuates considerably over time as both technology and firm practices evolve. Firms adjust along dual mechanisms: reallocating hiring across jobs accounts for 52% of aggregate change, while redesigning tasks within positions accounts for 39.5% of the adjustment. Critically, adjustment patterns vary by seniority level—senior roles shift earlier through reallocation toward new functions, while junior positions exhibit more heterogeneous adaptation strategies including both task redesign and role elimination. The findings suggest that current AI-driven labor market transitions proceed through fundamental restructuring of hiring patterns and job content rather than simple substitution and elimination, with implications for how workers and firms can expect to adapt to continued AI diffusion.

## 2. AI, Output, and Employment [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Andrew C. Johnston, Christos A. Makridis
**Date:** March 23, 2026; revised April 14, 2026 (SSRN Working Paper 6460358; CESifo Working Paper 12579)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6460358
**Abstract:** This paper provides some of the first causal evidence on whether artificial intelligence increases output and displaces workers, using administrative data covering essentially all U.S. employers. The authors employ a difference-in-differences design that exploits variation in occupational AI exposure, drawing on a measure that captures the degree to which AI systems can substitute for or augment tasks in each occupation. A one standard deviation increase in AI exposure raises output by 7%, with effects emerging in 2021 when enterprise AI tools became widely available—a timing pattern consistent with generative AI driving the effect. Employment responses vary systematically by the type of AI deployment: in occupations where AI likely requires human collaboration for effective use, employment rises by 4%; in occupations where AI can perform tasks more independently, there is no significant employment effect. The paper documents that AI has contributed to declining labor income shares, suggesting that productivity gains are not fully passed through to workers. These findings provide firm-level empirical grounding for debates about whether AI is primarily augmentative or substitutive, with implications for labor market policy and the distribution of AI's gains.

## 3. AI in Science: When and Where It Makes a Difference [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Stefano Bianchini, Valentina Di Girolamo, Julien Ravet, David Arranz
**Date:** May 2026 (Research Policy, DOI: 10.1016/j.respol.2026.105478)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105478
**Abstract:** This paper examines the diffusion and effects of artificial intelligence tools on scientific novelty and citation impact across more than 170 scientific fields, drawing on a dataset of over 80 million scientific publications spanning 2005 to 2023. The authors document that AI positively contributes to scientific novelty and impact on average, but that effects are highly heterogeneous across fields—a pattern that prior aggregate analyses obscure. The contribution of AI intensifies with its penetration within a field, consistent with threshold and network effects in the adoption of new methodologies. The most distinctive finding is that the strongest gains from AI use occur in fragmented or "rough" knowledge spaces, where the landscape of possible scientific contributions is less organized around dominant paradigms and researchers face a wider combinatorial search problem. In these settings, AI's ability to assist in search across large hypothesis spaces and to connect disparate literatures yields the greatest returns to novelty and impact. In more consolidated or "smooth" knowledge spaces, the marginal contribution of AI to novelty is smaller because dominant paradigms already channel research effort efficiently. The results carry direct implications for science funding policy, research organization, and the design of AI support tools tailored to the epistemic structure of specific fields.

## 4. Research Excellence Is Hard to Sustain: Evidence from Japan's WPI Initiative [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Arsev Umur Aydınoğlu, Özgür Kadir Özer
**Date:** April 14, 2026 (Research Policy, DOI: 10.1016/j.respol.2026.105493)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105493
**Abstract:** This study provides the first longitudinal, independent evaluation of Japan's World Premier International Research Center Initiative (WPI), addressing a critical gap in the excellence-initiative literature: whether the performance gains produced by large-scale public investment in research excellence persist once exceptional support ends. Analyzing eight WPI centers across both active funding and post-funding periods using bibliometric indicators, the authors find that WPI funding coincided with significant gains in publication output and citation impact relative to host universities, consistent with program goals. However, post-funding sustainability varied substantially across centers: those that secured alternative resource strategies—through industry partnerships, endowments, or integration into broader institutional priorities—maintained elevated performance, while those without such strategies experienced decline toward pre-program baselines. Interdisciplinary integration, a core WPI design feature, varied considerably across centers despite the common institutional framework. International collaboration proved more durable than other program benefits and persisted robustly into the post-funding period. The study contributes empirical evidence to the question of whether public investments in research excellence produce durable returns or require continuous funding to sustain, with direct implications for the design of excellence-funding initiatives and the conditions under which they generate lasting changes in scientific capacity.

## 5. Government Funding and the Direction of Academic Energy Research [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** David Popp, Myriam Gregoire-Zawilski, Lizhen Liang, Daniel Acuna
**Date:** February 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 34856)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34856
**Abstract:** This paper investigates a central question in the economics of science and innovation policy: does government funding shape the direction of academic scientists' research, or do scientists use grants to fund work they would have pursued regardless? The authors analyze grant data from two major agencies with different funding missions—the Department of Energy (DOE) and the National Science Foundation (NSF)—using text-based methods to measure the similarity between funded scientists' post-award research and their grant topics, comparing them to carefully matched control scientists who applied but were not funded. They find that funded scientists' research moves significantly closer to the grant topic relative to controls, providing causal evidence that public funding shapes research direction. Critically, this effect is strongest for ARPA-E (the Advanced Research Projects Agency–Energy), which explicitly targets the recruitment of new scientists into clean energy fields and takes greater risks on non-incumbent researchers. Traditional NSF grant-making, by contrast, produces smaller shifts in research direction, consistent with NSF's broader scientific-community model that emphasizes investigator-initiated research. The results inform the design of public science funding mechanisms, particularly the tradeoffs between mission-directed funding and investigator-driven models in steering the knowledge frontier toward socially valuable problems.
