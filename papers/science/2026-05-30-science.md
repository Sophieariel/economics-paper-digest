# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-30

## 1. AI in science: When and where it makes a difference [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Stefano Bianchini, Valentina Di Girolamo, Julien Ravet, David Arranz
**Date:** May 2026 (Research Policy, Vol. 55, No. 6, Article 105478)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105478
**Abstract:** This paper examines the diffusion and effects of artificial intelligence across more than 170 scientific fields between 2005 and 2023, drawing on a dataset of over 80 million scientific publications. Using novel indicators of knowledge novelty and citation impact, the authors assess when and where AI use contributes to knowledge creation. The headline finding is that AI use is associated with significantly more novel and highly cited research, and these effects are strengthened with recent AI architectural advances—particularly transformers and large language models. However, effects are sharply uneven across the field distribution: AI's contribution to novelty and impact intensifies with AI penetration within a field and with the "roughness" of the underlying knowledge space, defined as the degree to which the conceptual terrain is fragmented and combinatorially complex. Fields where ideas are distributed across disparate, hard-to-recombine clusters benefit most from AI's capacity to bridge distant knowledge regions, while homogeneous fields with dense internal connections gain less. A minority of fields shows negative effects on novelty, suggesting that AI can also accelerate conventional research trajectories at the expense of exploration. The findings contribute to the debate on whether AI is a general-purpose tool for science or whether its value is domain-contingent, providing the first large-scale cross-field causal evidence on AI's differential impact on the knowledge production function.

## 2. Research excellence is hard to sustain: Evidence from Japan's WPI initiative [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Arsev Umur Aydınoğlu, Özgür Kadir Özer
**Date:** April 14, 2026 (Research Policy, Vol. 55, No. 6, online first)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0048733326000843
**Abstract:** This paper provides the first longitudinal, independent evaluation of Japan's World Premier International Research Center Initiative (WPI), a large-scale excellence funding program launched in 2007 to create globally competitive research hubs. The authors address a critical gap in the excellence initiative literature: whether performance gains achieved under exceptional support persist once that support ends. Using bibliometric data on publication output and citation impact for eight WPI centers, they compare performance trajectories across three periods—pre-funding, active-funding, and post-funding—against matched control units at host universities. Results show that WPI funding coincided with substantial and statistically significant gains in publication volume and citation impact relative to institutional baselines. However, post-funding sustainability diverged markedly across centers: facilities that successfully secured alternative external funding sources (industry partnerships, international grants, successor government programs) maintained elevated performance, while those primarily reliant on host-university institutional support experienced systematic reversion toward pre-funding baselines. The divergence is not explained by field, center age, or initial performance levels, pointing instead to the organizational and resource-mobilization capabilities built (or not built) during the funded period. The findings carry direct implications for the design of excellence initiatives globally, suggesting that sustainability requires building diversified external funding relationships during the funding period rather than assuming institutional absorption post-award.

## 3. Does Generative AI Narrow Education-Based Productivity Gaps? Evidence from a Randomized Experiment [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Guillermo Cruces, Diego Fernández Meijide, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro H. Gálvez, María Lombardi
**Date:** February 2026; revised May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 34851)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34851
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether generative AI reduces or reinforces productivity differences rooted in educational attainment. The authors conduct a randomized online experiment with 1,174 adults aged 25–45 who complete a series of workplace-style business problem-solving tasks with or without access to an AI assistant. The experimental design allows identification of AI's effect on performance, its distributional consequences across education groups, and whether gains persist after the tool is removed. The key findings are threefold. First, AI increases task performance for all participants. Second, gains are substantially larger for individuals with lower educational attainment: the education-based performance gap narrows from 0.548 standard deviations in the no-AI condition to 0.139 standard deviations in the AI condition, closing approximately three-quarters of the initial gap. Third, follow-up tasks conducted without AI show that a portion of the productivity gains persists for lower-education participants, suggesting that AI assistance facilitates genuine learning rather than pure task delegation. Higher-education participants utilize the AI assistant somewhat more effectively across multiple dimensions, but their baseline advantage is compressed by AI's leveling effect. The authors conclude that generative AI narrows effective productivity differences in task execution, though underlying human-capital differences continue to shape unassisted performance—implying AI is a complement to, not substitute for, foundational skills acquisition. The results have implications for workplace inequality and for policies governing AI access in employment settings.

## 4. Labor Demand in the Age of Generative AI: Early Evidence from the U.S. Job Posting Data [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Yan Liu, He Wang, Shu Yu
**Date:** 2025 (World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 11263; SSRN 5504741)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5504741
**Abstract:** This paper examines the causal impact of generative AI on U.S. labor demand using a large dataset of online job postings. The authors exploit the release of ChatGPT in November 2022 as a quasi-experimental shock and apply difference-in-differences and event-study designs, comparing labor demand trajectories across occupations with high versus low AI substitutability scores derived from task-level exposure metrics. The analysis draws on approximately 285 million job postings spanning Q1 2018 through Q2 2025. The main finding is that the number of postings for occupations with above-median AI substitution scores fell by an average of 12 percent relative to less-exposed occupations following ChatGPT's launch, with the effect intensifying over time—from approximately 6 percent in the first year to 18 percent by the third year—consistent with firms progressively reorganizing production toward AI-augmented workflows. The demand decline is sharpest for entry-level positions requiring neither advanced degrees (18 percent decline) nor extensive prior experience (20 percent), and is concentrated in administrative support (40 percent) and professional services (30 percent). The authors run a battery of robustness checks including alternative AI exposure measures, pre-trend falsification tests, and controls for macroeconomic conditions, and the core results are stable throughout. While the paper does not directly observe whether displaced job postings reflect employment losses or productivity-driven output expansion with fewer workers, the evidence is consistent with generative AI acting as a rapid labor demand shock for cognitive, entry-level tasks—even within a period of overall tight U.S. labor markets.

## 5. Preference for Explainable AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Alex Chan
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Working Paper 35240)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35240
**Abstract:** This paper investigates how decision-makers adopt and selectively use AI-generated predictions and explanations in high-stakes settings. The author conducts field and laboratory experiments in which loan officers evaluate real $10,000 loan applications using AI-generated default-risk predictions, with random variation in whether accompanying explanations are provided and in the type of explanation shown. Several findings emerge. First, when explanations reveal that the AI's model systematically disadvantages non-White or female borrowers, participants more frequently override the AI's profit-focused recommendation, indicating that transparency can activate aversion to discriminatory outputs. Second, when participants are compensated based on loan repayment success, they actively seek out predictions but selectively avoid reading explanations—a pattern the author characterizes as "willful ignorance," in which financial incentives generate demand for prediction without accountability. This avoidance weakens when explanations focus on financial risk metrics or when demographic identifiers are removed from the explanation display. Third, a secondary experiment demonstrates that participants systematically undervalue explanations even in conditions where the explanations contain genuinely useful complementary information that would improve decision accuracy—a form of contingent reasoning failure. The results reveal a structural tension in AI governance: the actors most financially incentivized to make accurate AI-assisted decisions are often the same actors with the strongest incentive to avoid transparency about how those AI systems work. This has direct implications for explainable AI regulation and for the design of AI oversight mechanisms in credit markets and other high-stakes institutional settings.
