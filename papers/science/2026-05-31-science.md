# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-05-31

## 1. The Rapid Growth of AI Foundation Model Usage in Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ana Trišović, Alex Fogelson, Janakan Sivaloganathan, Neil Thompson
**Date:** November 21, 2025 (arXiv:2511.21739; MIT FutureTech)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21739
**Abstract:** This paper provides the first large-scale empirical analysis of AI foundation model usage in science, measured through direct adoption in publications rather than citation counts or keyword mentions. Drawing on the MIT FutureTech AI in Science database, the authors document near-exponential growth in adoption across scientific fields, with the highest uptake in Linguistics, Computer Science, and Engineering. Vision models are the most widely used, though language models' share is growing rapidly; open-weight models dominate over proprietary alternatives. A striking finding is the persistent and widening gap between model development and scientific adoption: in 2013 the median model built was 7.7 times larger than the median model adopted in scientific research, and by 2024 this ratio had grown to 26 times, indicating that scientists systematically adopt smaller, older, or more accessible models even as the frontier advances. On the impact side, papers using larger foundation models appear in higher-impact journals and receive more citations, suggesting that model choice is correlated with research output quality. The paper documents adoption patterns by field, country, and model type, providing a baseline dataset for future research on AI's effects on scientific productivity, inequality across fields, and the dynamics of technology diffusion in science.

## 2. Four Facts About U.S.-China AI Competition in Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ana Trišović, Alexander Fogelson, Neil Thompson
**Date:** December 22, 2025 (SSRN 6028854; MIT FutureTech)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6028854
**Abstract:** Using the FutureTech AI in Science database, which tracks AI foundation model adoption in scientific publications from 2012 to 2024, this paper documents four empirical facts about the U.S.-China competition in AI-driven science. First, China and the United States together account for approximately 53 percent of all documented foundation model adoptions in science over the past five years, reflecting the two countries' combined dominance in both AI development and high-output scientific publishing. Second, while each country's volume of AI-adopting publications was historically comparable, China significantly outpaced the United States in 2024, representing a recent shift in relative intensity. Third, the composition of adopted models differs between the two countries, with divergent patterns in open-weight versus proprietary model usage and in domain focus. Fourth, the concentration of adoption across fields and institutions within each country varies, with implications for where AI-driven scientific gains are accruing. The paper uses bibliometric methods applied to a curated database of AI model citations embedded in scientific literature, contributing new evidence to the empirical literature on science and technology competition and the geography of AI-enabled knowledge production.

## 3. The Adoption of Industrial AI in America [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Kristina McElheran, Mu-Jeung Yang, Zachary Kroff, Erik Brynjolfsson
**Date:** May 2026 (AEA Papers and Proceedings, Vol. 116, pp. 20–25)
**Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pandp.20261033
**Abstract:** This paper analyzes AI adoption patterns among U.S. manufacturing establishments using new data from a Census Bureau survey covering approximately 28,500 plants. The headline finding is that AI adoption remains limited: only 22.8 percent of plants report any AI use as of 2021, and intensity-weighted adoption is considerably lower still, indicating that even adopters are using AI in limited operational contexts. The cross-sectional correlates of adoption reveal an important pattern: AI use is more strongly associated with modern digital infrastructure—particularly cloud computing and recent enterprise software—than with older information technology systems, suggesting that digital readiness is a binding prerequisite. The authors document that the most commonly cited barriers to adoption are cost, a lack of applications relevant to the firm's operations, and insufficient in-house expertise, with the latter pointing to human capital as a key constraint. Notably, prior establishment-level productivity does not predict AI adoption, which the authors interpret as inconsistent with a simple learning-by-doing or high-performer advantage story and more consistent with technology availability and complementary capital driving the adoption margin. The results provide a nationally representative baseline for studying AI's economic effects in manufacturing and contribute to ongoing debates about whether AI adoption will be broadly diffused or concentrated among high-capability incumbents.

## 4. AI-Accelerated Occupational Decline and the Mobility Trap [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Xi Song, Jennie E. Brand, Sukie Xiuqi Yang, Michael Lachanski
**Date:** May 2026 (AEA Papers and Proceedings, Vol. 116, pp. 246–250)
**Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pandp.20261004
**Abstract:** This paper examines how AI-driven occupational restructuring affects worker career transitions, with particular attention to whether AI acceleration of occupational decline traps workers in adverse trajectories. Using Bureau of Labor Statistics occupational employment projections and Current Population Survey longitudinal microdata, the authors identify occupations experiencing accelerated decline following the 2018 transformer advances in AI and match these to individual career histories. The key finding is that workers in high-AI-exposed, declining occupations face severely constrained mobility: they are 5.2 times more likely to transition into another declining occupation than into a growing one, and approximately 70 percent experience downward or lateral career movement rather than upward mobility. The mechanism the authors advance is a "mobility trap"—workers displaced from AI-exposed roles lack the credentials, spatial mobility, or employer networks to access growing AI-complementary occupations, causing AI-driven restructuring to reinforce rather than redistribute economic disadvantage. The effects are heterogeneous by education, race, and prior earnings, with workers from disadvantaged groups exhibiting the sharpest mobility constraints. The findings contribute to the empirical literature on technology-driven labor market polarization and carry implications for workforce development policy, particularly the adequacy of retraining programs that assume workers in declining occupations can access growing alternatives.

## 5. AI's Job Shakeup: Analyzing the Uneven Impact of AI Adoption on Labor Demand [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Bin Gu, Mohammad Soltaniehha, Xiupeng Wang
**Date:** March 6, 2026 (SSRN 6528459)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6528459
**Abstract:** This paper examines how firm-level AI adoption affects labor demand across occupations with heterogeneous AI exposure, contributing both a new measurement approach and new causal evidence. The authors construct a text-based measure of operational AI adoption from 10-K annual filings using a pipeline combining natural language processing, large language models, and a neural-network classifier trained to distinguish substantive AI deployment from boilerplate or aspirational AI mentions. This measure is matched to Webb's occupation-level AI exposure scores and Lightcast job posting data for Compustat firms from 2010 to 2022. The core finding is that AI adoption causes a significant and persistent decline in job postings for high-AI-exposure occupations: effects reach −0.14 percent at the two-year horizon and −0.30 percent at the five-year horizon relative to firms that did not adopt AI, while job postings in low-exposure occupations remain near zero. At the same time, AI adoption is positively associated with labor productivity for both large and small firms, indicating that AI raises efficiency while selectively contracting demand in the most-exposed roles—a pattern consistent with partial automation complementing rather than replacing all labor. The authors further document heterogeneity across buy-versus-build AI pathways and firm size categories. The results advance the micro-level evidence on AI's labor demand effects and provide a replicable text-based adoption measure for future firm-level research.
