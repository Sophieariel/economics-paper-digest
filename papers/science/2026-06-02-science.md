# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-06-02

## 1. Growth is Getting Harder to Find, Not Ideas [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Teresa C. Fort, Nathan Goldschlag, Jack Liang, Peter K. Schott, and Nikolas Zolas
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35182
**Abstract:** Relatively flat US productivity growth despite rising R&D expenditures is often interpreted as evidence that ideas are getting harder to find. The authors build a new 45-year panel tracking the universe of US firms' patenting to investigate the micro underpinnings of this conclusion, separately examining the relationships between research inputs and ideas (patents) versus ideas and growth. They find that average patents per R&D input are increasing, the elasticity of patents to R&D inputs is flat or rising, and there is no systematic evidence of a secular decline in patenting after controlling for research inputs. There is a positive, significant, and fairly steady relationship between firms' patent and labor productivity growth rates. Average firm growth after controlling for patent growth declines, however, indicating that innovative efforts are crucial for sustaining growth over the past four decades.

## 2. Estimating the Present Value of R&D Tax Benefits in the United States [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Brandon Pecoraro, Nicholas C. Hoffman, Martin Lopez-Daneri, Elena C. Derby, Rachel Moore, and Shannon E. Sledz
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35208
**Abstract:** Using confidential corporate tax return data, the authors provide the first empirical estimates of the actual present value corporations realize from US R&D tax credits and deductions. Real benefits diverge from statutory amounts because loss-making firms cannot immediately apply credits and deductions, requiring them to carry tax attributes forward. The paper develops a novel methodology to track firm-level use of tax attributes across time. Results show the value of R&D tax benefits fluctuates significantly with firm loss status, age, and scale. Established large enterprises typically claim benefits rapidly, whereas younger firms — particularly smaller ones — frequently operate at losses and realize benefits more gradually. Between 2012–2016, the typical firm gained $0.41 in statutory tax benefits per dollar of R&D investment, with a realized present value of $0.36. Young, small loss-making firms received only $0.23 per dollar — a 44% reduction versus the statutory baseline.

## 3. Old Space, New Space: A Commercial Revolution in Innovation? [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ruben Gaetani and Alexander T. Whalley
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35212
**Abstract:** The researchers examine when the transition from traditional to commercial space innovation occurred using patent data analysis. They find that the largest surge in space innovation occurred in the 1990s, coinciding with demand-side market creation, predating the well-known entry of SpaceX and Blue Origin after 2005. Established aerospace companies remain dominant in space-related patenting, while newer firms contribute a growing but still minority share. Geographic regions that led post-Apollo space innovation continue their dominance today. The authors conclude that the shift toward private enterprise innovation is more interconnected with traditional approaches than commonly understood, and that government's primary contribution involves developing accessible markets through policy initiatives rather than directly sponsoring technological breakthroughs.

## 4. Preference for Explainable AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Alex Chan
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35240
**Abstract:** The study uses a controlled experiment in which participants act as loan officers deciding whether to approve actual $10,000 loans using AI default-risk predictions. Key findings show that when explanations demonstrated the AI penalized non-White or female borrowers, participants more frequently rejected the AI's recommendation. However, when bonuses were tied to repayment success, participants sought predictions while avoiding explanations — consistent with deliberate ignorance — behavior that diminished when explanations emphasized financial aspects or demographic data was withheld. A secondary experiment uncovered that participants failed to think conditionally and systematically undervalued explanations despite their potential to enhance decision-making when combined with personal information, suggesting a robust behavioral bias against AI transparency.

## 5. Firm Data on AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ivan Yotzov, Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Philip Bunn, Steven J. Davis, Kevin M. Foster, Aaron Jalca, Brent H. Meyer, Paul Mizen, Michael A. Navarrete, Pawel Smietanka, Gregory Thwaites, and Ben Zhe Wang
**Date:** May 2026 (NBER Digest feature; working paper originally circulated February 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836
**Abstract:** The authors survey nearly 6,000 senior business executives across US, UK, German, and Australian firms to develop new evidence on AI adoption and its effects on jobs, productivity, and output. Roughly 70 percent of firms have adopted AI, with higher adoption at younger and more productive companies. Text generation via large language models is the most common application (41% of firms). While over two-thirds of executives regularly use AI, their average weekly usage is only 1.5 hours. Executives report minimal AI effects on employment or productivity over the previous three years, but they anticipate substantial impacts ahead — predicting productivity gains of 1.4%, output increases of 0.8%, and employment reductions of 0.7% within three years. Employees, by contrast, anticipate that AI will raise employment 0.5% at their firms, revealing a disconnect between employer and employee expectations about job market effects.
