# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-06-04

## 1. Knowledge Spillovers and Local Outcomes: An Existence Proof from the Establishment of the National Labs [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Susan Helper, Resem Makan, Daniel W. Shoag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35011
**Abstract:** The researchers examine how establishing U.S. National Laboratories during the 1940s–1950s generated local economic benefits through knowledge spillovers. Their analysis reveals that counties hosting these labs experienced significant growth in patenting activity among inventors unaffiliated with the facilities, with patents increasingly focusing on the labs' research areas and citing laboratory work. Using newly digitized historical economic data spanning 1936–1970, they document sustained increases in retail sales and household income. Census records from 1940–1950 show wage improvements for established residents, particularly those with college education. The study found that young people exposed to lab establishment during their school years obtained additional education, suggesting human capital development played a role. Notably, these substantial local gains occurred despite extensive secrecy around early nuclear research, indicating that proximity to public research institutions can generate meaningful community benefits even under restricted information conditions.

## 2. Attention (And Money) Is All You Need: Why Universities Are Struggling to Keep AI Talent [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ufuk Akcigit, Craig A. Chikis, Emin Dinlersoz, Nathan Goldschlag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34964
**Abstract:** The researchers tracked 42,000 AI researchers over two decades using a novel dataset combining academic publication records with U.S. Census employer-employee data. They find that industry increasingly attracts younger and foreign-born researchers, while gender representation improves more in academia. A striking finding shows that the top 1% of publishing industry scientists now earn $1.5 million more annually than comparable academics, a fivefold increase since 2001. The wage gaps correspond with greater concentration of researchers in large incumbent firms. Additionally, researchers transitioning to industry positions publish less but patent more, consistent with a shift from open science toward proprietary innovation.

## 3. Artificial Intelligence, Productivity, and the Workforce: Evidence from Corporate Executives [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Salomé Baslandze, Zachary Edwards, John Graham, Ty McClure, Brent H. Meyer, Michael Sparks, Sonya R. Waddell, Daniel Weitz
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34984
**Abstract:** The research examines nearly 750 corporate executives' perspectives on AI's impact on business operations and employment. The study finds substantial variation in AI adoption across firms, with over half having already invested. Labor productivity improvements are positive and sector-dependent, expected to accelerate in 2026, particularly in finance and high-skill services. Productivity gains stem from innovation and demand channels rather than capital expansion alone. The paper documents a "productivity paradox" where perceived gains exceed measured gains, attributed to delays in revenue realization. There is minimal evidence of near-term aggregate employment losses, though larger firms anticipate reductions while smaller firms expect modest expansion. Labor market shifts favor technical roles while routine clerical positions decline. The paper constructs an index identifying job functions most vulnerable to AI displacement.

## 4. Still Waters, Rapid Currents: Early Labor Market Transformation under Generative AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Anders Humlum, Emilie Vestergaard
**Date:** May 2025 (revised March 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777
**Abstract:** The researchers examine early labor market impacts of AI chatbots using Danish administrative records linked to adoption surveys. They find rapid currents in adoption and reported productivity gains, with new AI-related tasks becoming widespread. However, these changes have not yet affected earnings or recorded hours—the study estimates precise null effects on compensation two years post-ChatGPT launch at both worker and workplace levels, ruling out impacts exceeding 2%. The key finding reveals task reorganization rather than wage disruption: employers integrate AI through reassignment to content generation, oversight, and integration roles. Some workers transition to higher-paying occupations where AI chatbots prove more relevant, though insufficient numbers to shift aggregate earnings. The analysis concludes that technological change reshapes work well before it surfaces in earnings or hours.

## 5. Deep Research on a Loop: Using AI Agents to Construct Economic Datasets [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Santiago Afonso, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro H. Gálvez, Raul A. Sosa
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35188
**Abstract:** The authors propose DRIL (Deep Research on a Loop), a methodology employing AI agents to assemble datasets from publicly available sources. The approach applies a fixed research instrument across a mapped unit space with a two-stage architecture separating design from implementation. The instrument specifies variables and coding rules, while an evidence policy governs sources and citations. Data quality mechanisms track gaps and uncertainty explicitly. The team tested DRIL on a 2025 update of the Global Tax Expenditures Database covering eight Latin American and Caribbean countries. The effort produced 129 sources and 136 evidence records, covering 22 qualitative fields completely and 6 quantitative estimate types with documented gaps. The cost was comparable to a standard LLM subscription, comparable to a few hours of research-assistant work. The authors conclude that even partial automation of dataset construction can shift the production function of empirical economics, potentially reducing costs associated with constructing datasets from primary sources—described as one of the costliest tasks in empirical economics.
