# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-06-05

## 1. Inventing Modern Invention: The Professionalization of Technological Progress in the US [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Matte Hartog, Andres Gomez-Lievano, Ricardo Hausmann, Frank Neffke
**Date:** May 2026 (Research Policy, Vol. 55, Issue 3, Article 105382)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0048733325002112
**Abstract:** Over the mid-nineteenth and early twentieth century, the United States shifted from an agricultural economy to a technological leader. The authors digitized approximately half a million pages of historical patent yearbooks covering over 1.6 million patents from millions of inventors between 1856 and 2000, linking these records to U.S. Census demographic data and surveys of industrial research laboratories. The central finding is that in the early 1920s a new system of innovation — organized around professional engineers working collaboratively in firms — started to rapidly replace the craftsmen- and family-based invention that had dominated the nineteenth century. The key organizational innovation driving this shift was the industrial research laboratory, which enabled high-skill teamwork and replaced the collaborations within families with professional ties inside firms. This systemic transition transformed the division of labor in invention, produced an explosion of novelty and collaborative work, and fundamentally reshaped the geography of innovation across the United States: patents in the new regime combined more and newer technologies than under the artisanal system, and the sources of innovation became more concentrated in urban manufacturing centers with organized corporate research. The paper provides large-scale quantitative evidence on how the organizational form governing invention shapes the rate, direction, and geography of technological progress, with direct implications for modern debates about the design of research institutions, the value of industrial R&D labs, and science policy more broadly.

## 2. Is the Dominance of Graduates from Top-Tier Universities among Tenured Faculty Driven by Prestige or Output? Evidence from 50 Years of University Appointments in Germany [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Stefan Buechele, Guido Buenstorf, Matthias Huegel, Johannes Koenig, Maria Theissen
**Date:** May 2026 (Research Policy, Vol. 55, Issue 3, Article 105411)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733326000028
**Abstract:** Tenured positions in German academia are heavily concentrated among graduates of a small number of elite PhD-granting universities, but this paper asks whether that concentration reflects institutional prestige or simply the unequal distribution of doctoral production. Analyzing the full population of doctoral graduates in Germany since the 1960s, the authors find that professor positions concentrate among graduates of leading universities in a pattern that closely tracks concentration in PhD output — the top five universities account for 17.9% of all appointed university professors, comparable to documented patterns in the United States. Crucially, however, the paper finds no evidence that the prestige of the doctoral degree-granting university systematically affects an individual's odds of being appointed to a professorship, once PhD output is accounted for. The apparent dominance of elite-university graduates in the faculty is instead driven by preferential hiring within prestige groups that reflects return mobility — graduates who secure initial appointments elsewhere and then return to institutions in their own alma mater's peer group — rather than by an external prestige premium operating in the broader academic labor market. The odds of appointment have declined strongly across cohorts since the 1960s, and there is no indication that the importance of the degree-granting university for academic careers has increased over time despite growing stratification in higher education systems worldwide. The findings have direct implications for debates about whether university rankings and prestige hierarchies distort scientific labor markets and for policies designed to broaden geographic and institutional diversity in academic hiring.

## 3. Firm Training, Automation, and Wages: International Worker-Level Evidence [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Oliver Falck, Yuchen Guo, Christina Langer, Valentin Lindlacher, Simon Wiederhold
**Date:** May 2026 (Research Policy, Vol. 55, Issue 3, Article 105424; earlier circulated as IZA Discussion Paper No. 17503 and CESifo Working Paper No. 11533)
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5054301
**Abstract:** Firm-provided training is widely regarded as a mechanism through which workers can adapt to technological change and protect themselves from automation-driven wage depression, yet rigorous large-scale cross-national evidence on this claim has been limited. Using internationally harmonized data from over 90,000 workers across 37 industrialized countries, the authors construct an individual-level measure of automation risk based on tasks actually performed at work, capturing within-occupation variation that occupation-level measures miss. Applying within-occupation and within-industry variation combined with entropy balancing to address selection into training, the analysis finds that training reduces workers' automation risk by 3.8 percentage points — equivalent to 8% of average automation risk — and that approximately 15% of the wage gains associated with firm training operate through this reduction in automation exposure. Importantly, the automation-risk-reducing and wage-enhancing benefits of training are broadly distributed across demographic groups — gender, age, and educational attainment — rather than concentrated among a particular segment of workers, suggesting that employer investment in workforce training can serve as a general mechanism for protecting diverse groups of workers against the wage-depressing effects of technological displacement. The results provide the first large-scale cross-national causal evidence that firm training is an effective adaptation tool in the face of automation, with direct implications for labor market policy in advanced economies contemplating regulatory or subsidy frameworks for employer-provided training.

## 4. Shelter in Scholarship: Evidence from a Global Survey of Hosts for Displaced Ukrainian Scientists [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Michael E. Rose, Katarína Juríková, Marina Pelepets, Olga Slivko, Julia Yereshko
**Date:** May 2026 (Research Policy, Vol. 55, Issue 3, Article 105425)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733326000168
**Abstract:** In response to Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, the global scientific community launched a wide range of emergency support initiatives offering displaced Ukrainian scholars opportunities to continue their work abroad. Using a survey of hosts offering 2,416 support opportunities registered in the #ScienceForUkraine database — a response rate of 22.3% on the host side — the paper provides the first systematic large-scale evidence on the supply-side determinants of demand for emergency scientific mobility programs. Among the support offers that received responses, 56.9% attracted at least one eligible application and 47.4% helped place at least one Ukrainian scientist. Scholarships with direct financial support proved substantially more attractive to applicants than offers of academic positions without stipends or access to laboratory resources, and offers connected to the Humanities attracted more applicants than offers in other disciplines. The paper contributes new evidence on how global scientific labor markets respond to geopolitical shocks, drawing on the largest study of emergency academic relocation of its kind. The findings reveal heterogeneity in demand along both the type-of-support and disciplinary dimensions that has not been documented before, and they carry direct implications for the design of international emergency support schemes for displaced researchers in future crises.

## 5. AI, Output, and Employment [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Andrew C. Johnston, Christos A. Makridis
**Date:** March 2026 (CESifo Working Paper No. 12579; also SSRN Working Paper No. 6460358)
**Source:** https://www.ifo.de/en/cesifo/publications/2026/working-paper/ai-output-and-employment
**Abstract:** Does artificial intelligence increase productivity — and does it displace workers? The authors examine aggregate effects using administrative data covering essentially all U.S. employers in a difference-in-differences design that exploits variation in occupational AI exposure across industries and states. A one standard deviation increase in AI exposure raises output by 7%, with effects emerging in 2021 when enterprise AI tools first entered the market at scale. Employment effects follow the same timing but diverge sharply by exposure type: where AI likely requires human collaboration to function, employment rises by approximately 4%; where AI can perform tasks independently without requiring human co-production, the authors find no significant employment effect. The results are robust to state-by-year and industry-by-year fixed effects, and the evidence as a whole suggests that AI adoption has caused a decrease in the labor share of income. The findings provide one of the first large-scale causal estimates of AI's aggregate effects on output and employment at the firm and industry level in the United States, and the divergence between augmentative and automating AI exposure offers new empirical evidence on the conditions under which AI functions as a complement versus substitute for human labor — a central question for the economics of AI and for labor market policy.
