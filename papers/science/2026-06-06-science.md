# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-06-06

## 1. Knowledge Spillovers and Local Outcomes: An Existence Proof from the Establishment of the National Labs [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Susan Helper, Resem Makan, Daniel W. Shoag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35011
**Abstract:** We study the local effects of public R&D by exploiting the establishment of U.S. National Laboratories during the 1940s–1950s. Because lab sites were chosen largely for security and political reasons rather than existing or potential innovative capability, we use runner-up locations as controls to establish causality. Counties receiving a lab experienced sustained increases in patenting by non-laboratory inventors; local patents shifted toward laboratory research fields and increasingly cited laboratory work. Using newly digitized county economic data spanning 1936–1970, we document persistent gains in retail sales and household income. Census records reveal wage improvements for long-term residents, particularly college-educated workers. Cohorts exposed to laboratory establishment during school years achieved higher educational attainment, suggesting lasting human capital benefits. Notably, spillovers arise despite extensive secrecy around early nuclear research, indicating substantial local economic returns to public R&D proximity even under severe information constraints.

## 2. Attention (And Money) Is All You Need: Why Universities Are Struggling to Keep AI Talent [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ufuk Akcigit, Craig A. Chikis, Emin Dinlersoz, Nathan Goldschlag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34964
**Abstract:** We construct a dataset linking academic publication histories to U.S. Census employment records and track approximately 42,000 AI researchers over two decades. Industry increasingly recruits younger and internationally-educated scientists, while academia shows greater gains in gender diversity. The most striking finding is the widening compensation gap: the top 1% of publishing industry scientists now earn $1.5 million more annually than comparable academics, a fivefold increase since 2001. This wage expansion is concentrated among dominant established firms, producing a clustering of top talent in a small number of companies. Researchers who transition to industry reduce publication output but increase patenting activity, suggesting a shift from freely shared scientific inquiry to proprietary commercial development. Our findings document a structural reallocation of AI research talent away from the public knowledge commons and toward private capture.

## 3. How Artificial Intelligence Shapes Science: Evidence from AlphaFold [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ryan R. Hill, Carolyn Stein
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35143
**Abstract:** We study how the AlphaFold2 algorithm changed scientific discovery in structural biology following its July 2021 release, which made hundreds of thousands of AI-predicted protein structures freely available. Contrary to a simple substitution story, researchers did not reduce experimental structure determination; instead, they used AI predictions to facilitate and complement lab work. Basic research on previously unstudied proteins increased by 15–40% relative to proteins with prior structural data, redirecting inquiry toward less-explored areas of the proteome. We find no evidence so far that more applied, early-stage drug development has followed these newly accessible proteins, though future activity remains possible. The results illustrate that a major AI breakthrough can expand the frontier of basic science while leaving applied translation lags intact — a pattern with implications for how science funders should respond to AI-enabled discovery tools.

## 4. Generative AI and Firm Hiring Demand: Evidence from Advanced and Developing Economies [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Faezeh Khosravi, Elaine Liu
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6537038
**Abstract:** We study how generative AI reshapes firm-level hiring demand across advanced and developing economies. We construct predetermined firm-country AI exposure from 2019 occupational composition and estimate a continuous difference-in-differences design using online job posting data from 2020 to 2025. In advanced economies, total posting volumes decline gradually beginning three to four quarters after ChatGPT's release, with compositional shifts toward fewer high-substitution vacancies emerging later. In developing economies, posting volumes begin to decline earlier in the event window, with compositional changes following by the third quarter. In both groups, more-exposed firms reduce the share of high-AI-substitution postings and lower the posting-weighted AI substitution mix, but adjustment is faster and more front-loaded in developing economies. The results are consistent with generative AI reshaping hiring demand differently across development groups, with developing economies adjusting earlier and showing clearer evidence of compositional change than of aggregate volume effects.

## 5. Deep Research on a Loop: Using AI Agents to Construct Economic Datasets [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Santiago Afonso, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro H. Gálvez, Raul A. Sosa
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35188
**Abstract:** We propose DRIL (Deep Research on a Loop), a methodology that employs AI agents to assemble economic datasets from publicly accessible sources. The design applies a fixed research instrument across a mapped unit space (e.g., countries by years) via a two-stage architecture that separates instrument design from execution. Components include a research instrument specifying variables and coding rules, an evidence policy governing source selection and citation, and data quality mechanisms documenting gaps and uncertainty. We demonstrate DRIL on a 2025 update of the Global Tax Expenditures Database for eight Latin American and Caribbean countries, generating 129 sources and 136 evidence records covering 22 qualitative fields and 6 quantitative estimate types. Total project cost approximated a standard LLM subscription — comparable to a few hours of research-assistant work. We argue that even partial automation of dataset construction can fundamentally reshape the production function of empirical economics, lowering barriers to large-scale cross-country data collection.
