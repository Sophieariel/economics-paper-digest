# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-06-07

## 1. AI as an Innovation in the Method of Innovation: Implications for Productivity Growth [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Filippo Bontadini, Carol Corrado, Jonathan Haskel, Cecilia Jona-Lasinio
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pandp.20261036
**Abstract:** This paper estimates AI's impact on labor productivity growth, treating AI as both a general-purpose technology and an innovation in the method of innovation. The framework distinguishes upstream innovation activities from downstream production and measures how AI reshapes the process by which new ideas are discovered and commercialized. The key finding is that AI is already materially affecting official U.S. productivity measures. Software products and software R&D together contributed approximately 50% of the 2% average nonfarm business labor productivity growth recorded over 2017–2024, and accounted for roughly 50% of the 1.2 percentage point productivity acceleration relative to the preceding five-year period (2012–2017). The authors argue that standard growth accounting frameworks understate AI's contribution by treating it as a conventional capital input rather than as a technology that endogenously shifts the productivity of innovation itself. The paper contributes to debates about whether AI's macroeconomic impact is already visible in productivity statistics and provides a conceptual bridge between the microeconomic literature on AI and task substitution and aggregate productivity measurement.

## 2. Aggregate Productivity Gains from Artificial Intelligence: A Sectoral Perspective [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Francesco Filippucci, Peter Gal, Matthias Schief
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pandp.20261035
**Abstract:** This paper projects AI's productivity gains across 65 U.S. industries using a multi-sector general-equilibrium framework calibrated to industry-level data on task content, capital-labor substitutability, and current AI penetration. The central finding is that AI could contribute up to 0.9 percentage points to annual aggregate TFP growth over the next decade. Knowledge-intensive service sectors show the strongest gains; manual task-intensive industries show weaker improvement. The authors examine whether uneven sectoral gains produce a Baumol cost-disease drag on aggregate productivity and find this effect is quantitatively small when demand substitution is flexible or when labor and capital can shift across industries. The paper provides a calibrated sectoral decomposition that is missing from aggregate AI productivity estimates, and identifies which industries are most likely to drive measured productivity acceleration and which will contribute least, informing sector-specific policy discussions about AI diffusion and investment.

## 3. Is the dominance of graduates from top-tier universities among tenured faculty driven by prestige or output? Evidence from 50 years of university appointments in Germany [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Stefan Buechele, Guido Buenstorf, Matthias Huegel, Johannes Koenig, Maria Theissen
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Vol. 55, Issue 3; online first January 19, 2026)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105411
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether the over-representation of graduates from a small number of elite universities in German academic faculty reflects the causal effect of institutional prestige on hiring outcomes or instead reflects underlying differences in research output. Using the full population of German doctoral graduates since the 1960s and linking them to subsequent appointment outcomes over 50 years, the authors document strong concentration: the top five universities account for 17.9% of all appointed professors. However, the paper finds no systematic evidence that the prestige of the doctoral degree-granting university independently affects individuals' odds of being appointed to professorships, after controlling for publication output and research quality. The observed concentration pattern appears driven primarily by researchers returning to their original institutions following initial appointments elsewhere, rather than by prestige signaling operating as a distinct hiring criterion. Critically, this concentration has not grown over the 50-year observation period, suggesting that the German academic labor market has not become more prestige-stratified despite broader trends toward ranking and evaluation. The findings contribute to debates about meritocracy versus credentialism in academic hiring and have implications for policies targeting geographic and institutional diversity in research systems.

## 4. Inventing modern invention: The professionalization of technological progress in the US [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Matte Hartog, Andres Gomez-Lievano, Ricardo Hausmann, Frank Neffke
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Vol. 55, Issue 3; online first 2025)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2025.105382
**Abstract:** This paper studies a fundamental transition in the organization of American innovation using 1.6 million digitized patents and linked census data spanning from the late nineteenth century to the mid-twentieth century. The central finding is that in the early 1920s a new innovation system based on teamwork and professional engineers rapidly displaced the craftsmanship-based invention that had dominated U.S. technological progress in the nineteenth century. Industrial research laboratories enabled systematic collaboration among credentialed specialists, replacing family-based and apprenticeship-linked invention networks that had been the primary organizational form through the 1910s. This transformation restructured the division of labor in invention — increasing collaborative invention, raising novelty as measured by patent originality, and altering the geography of innovation as laboratory-based systems concentrated in certain industrial regions. The paper provides direct empirical evidence for a structural break in the innovation production function that economists have long hypothesized but rarely measured at the micro level. The findings have implications for understanding how R&D institutions shape the rate and direction of technological change and for historical debates about the origins of twentieth-century U.S. productivity growth.

## 5. The Software Complexity of Nations [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Sándor Juhász, Johannes Wachs, Jermain Kaminski, César A. Hidalgo
**Date:** 2026 (Research Policy, Vol. 55, Issue 3; online first early 2026)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105422
**Abstract:** This paper introduces a software economic complexity index constructed from the geographic distribution of programming language specialization in open-source software repositories on GitHub. The authors extend the economic complexity framework — originally developed using trade data — to the digital economy, arguing that software production capabilities are an increasingly important dimension of national economic sophistication that administrative and trade records miss. The index is built by identifying countries' revealed comparative advantage across programming languages and applying the standard complexity methodology. Programming language adoption follows the principle of relatedness: countries' entries into and exits from specific languages are predicted by existing specialization patterns, validating that the index captures genuine productive capabilities rather than random specialization. Software complexity is a significant predictor of GDP per capita, income inequality, and emissions intensity, adding explanatory power beyond trade-based complexity indexes alone. The paper contributes both a new measure — made available for subsequent research — and new evidence on the link between digital capabilities and economic development, with implications for industrial policy and for understanding why AI and software adoption may proceed so unevenly across countries.
