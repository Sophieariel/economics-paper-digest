# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-06-08

## 1. The Growth and Performance of Artificial Intelligence in Asset Management [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Shuang Chen, Clemens Sialm, David X. Xu
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35273
**Abstract:** This paper examines the rise of artificial intelligence in investment management using regulatory filings, labor market data on AI-related job postings, and fund prospectus text to identify and track AI-driven investment funds. AI hedge funds grew substantially over the sample period and initially earned significant risk-adjusted outperformance relative to non-AI peers. However, this performance advantage has declined over time as AI adoption spread and competition among AI-based strategies intensified. AI funds also exhibit lower return comovement with one another than with non-AI funds, suggesting that AI-driven strategies are more heterogeneous and less herding-prone than commonly assumed. The paper contributes to the literature on financial innovation and delegated asset management by providing the first systematic empirical account of how AI technologies are adopted in the hedge fund industry and how their performance implications evolve as a technology matures and diffuses.

## 2. AI for Structural Estimation [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Victor Duarte, Julia Fonseca
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35283
**Abstract:** Structural estimation of dynamic equilibrium models is computationally intensive, often requiring days or weeks of computation to solve and fit a single model. This paper proposes and validates an approach in which neural networks trained on large libraries of simulated model outcomes replace conventional solution and estimation routines, reducing estimation time from days to minutes without material loss of accuracy. The approach allows simultaneous enforcement of market-clearing conditions and estimation of structural parameters, tasks that typically require sequential iteration. The authors validate the method on canonical macro-finance and IO models and demonstrate that it recovers both parameters and counterfactual predictions with high fidelity. They also introduce an AI agent that applies the methodology to new model specifications described in natural language, lowering the barrier to structural analysis for applied researchers. The paper contributes to the growing literature on machine learning methods in economics by showing that neural-network surrogates can serve as credible substitutes for computationally demanding structural routines.

## 3. Automation, Learning, and Career Dynamics [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Hassan Afrouzi, Andres Blanco, Andrés Drenik, Erik Hurst
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35157
**Abstract:** This paper develops a continuous-time general equilibrium model in which workers accumulate human capital by performing tasks, and automation technologies reduce the set of tasks available for human workers to perform. The model generates two stable equilibria that differ in their aggregate rates of human-capital accumulation: a high-learning equilibrium in which workers invest in skill formation and a low-learning trap in which reduced task opportunities undermine human-capital investment incentives. Reducing the cost of automation technology improves welfare substantially in the high-learning equilibrium but can push the economy toward the low-learning trap when the initial equilibrium is fragile. The paper characterizes optimal policy in this environment and finds that the best combination involves taxing profits from automation while subsidizing spending to maintain a frontier of tasks accessible to human workers. The results provide a dynamic general-equilibrium rationale for active labor market interventions when automation and on-the-job learning interact.

## 4. The Shape of Economics: Semantic Structure, Knowledge Flows, and Specialization [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Tom Harris
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6549201
**Abstract:** This paper applies natural language processing to map the intellectual structure of economics and trace how it has changed over the past quarter century. The author embeds approximately 50,000 papers from 24 leading economics journals and the NBER working paper series (2000–2026) using the SPECTER2 transformer model, constructing semantic representations that capture conceptual proximity between papers and subfields. The headline finding is that economics has been specializing in place: within-field homogeneity has increased while between-field semantic distances have remained roughly stable, implying that fields are each becoming more internally coherent without drifting further apart from one another. Applied empirical fields have expanded their footprint in the semantic space, while theoretical and financial-economics clusters have contracted. An analysis of knowledge flows shows that fields whose researchers and citation networks reach into intellectually distant regions of the discipline grow faster in terms of publications and citations, consistent with returns to intellectual diversification at the field level. The paper contributes to the economics of science literature by using large-scale semantic embedding as an alternative to citation-based measures of knowledge structure and disciplinary change.

## 5. Beyond Demo Day: Sorting and Value Added in Startup Accelerators [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Youn Baek, Deepak Hegde
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35063
**Abstract:** Startup accelerators have proliferated as institutions that claim to select and develop high-potential early-stage ventures, yet systematic evidence on whether they create value — as distinct from merely identifying it — is scarce. This paper constructs a novel dataset covering approximately 750,000 U.S. startups and 329 accelerator programs and applies an education-economics value-added framework to separately identify accelerator selectivity (sorting) and causal effects on venture outcomes. The authors document substantial positive sorting: better-quality ventures disproportionately enter higher-ranked programs. After controlling for sorting, the average accelerator generates a negative value-added estimate — accepted ventures do no better, and sometimes worse, than comparable non-accepted firms. However, the distribution of program-level value added is highly right-skewed: a small subset of accelerators produces large and statistically significant gains across multiple outcome measures including acquisition rates, employment, revenue, and investor valuation. These high-value-added programs share observable characteristics including strong mentor networks, alumni quality, and connections to downstream venture capital. The findings reconcile mixed prior evidence on accelerator effectiveness by showing that aggregate null results mask substantial heterogeneity, with implications for entrepreneurs choosing programs and for policymakers designing accelerator support schemes.
