# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-06-10

## 1. Will The University Endowment Tax Slow Scientific Progress? Evidence from Elite Economics PhD Programs [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Joshua Angrist, Marc Diederichs, and Glenn Ellison
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35244
**Abstract:** The paper examines whether financial pressures on universities, including a 2025 endowment tax, might reduce graduate enrollment at elite institutions and affect research output. Using MIT Economics PhD applicants, the researchers estimate how eight top schools contribute to long-run research success. By controlling for admissions rankings and applicant qualifications, they find that graduates from high-tax and other top-eight schools produce "60-75% more impact-adjusted publications than do comparable graduates from non-top-eight US schools," with particularly strong advantages in top-tier journals. Notably, differences among elite schools themselves are modest, and the advantage appears unrelated to editorial connections or peer effects.

## 2. Growth is Getting Harder to Find, Not Ideas [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Teresa C. Fort, Nathan Goldschlag, Jack Liang, Peter K. Schott, and Nikolas Zolas
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35182
**Abstract:** The researchers examined a 45-year panel of US firms' patents to understand why productivity growth has remained relatively flat despite increasing R&D spending. Their findings challenge the common assumption that "ideas are getting harder to find." Instead, they discovered that "average patents per R&D input are increasing" and patent elasticity to research inputs remains steady or rises. The study found a consistent positive relationship between firms' patents and productivity growth. However, firm growth declines when controlling for patent expansion, suggesting that "firms' innovative efforts play a key role in sustaining growth that has not diminished over the last four decades." The implication is that innovation remains robust; rather, growth itself has become more difficult to achieve across the economy.

## 3. Writing Code vs. Shipping Code: Productivity Effects Across Generations of AI Coding Tools [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Mert Demirer, Leon Musolff, and Liyuan Yang
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35275
**Abstract:** The researchers examine how productivity gains from successive AI coding tools translate through the software development pipeline. Using data from over 100,000 GitHub developers combined with AI usage telemetry in a matched event-study design, they find that autocomplete, interactive agents, and autonomous agents boost coding activity by 40%, 140%, and 180% respectively. However, these gains diminish significantly downstream: the 180% activity increase drops to 50% for project count and 30% for actual releases. The authors attribute this attenuation to human bottlenecks, estimating a substitution elasticity of 0.25 between AI and human effort. Across four app marketplaces, they observe modest increases in new apps but no growth in overall usage. The findings suggest that large task-level AI productivity gains have so far translated only partially into final shipped output.

## 4. What Investment Data Implies about the AI Transition [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Jessica Wachter and Jonathan Wachter
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35290
**Abstract:** The paper analyzes massive capital expenditures by major technology firms—$380 billion in 2025 with projected doubling in 2026—using an open-economy macroeconomic model calibrated to observed investment patterns. The researchers estimate that AI-sector productivity could increase by a factor of roughly 2.7. Key findings include cumulative GDP growth projections ranging from 5 to 58 percentage points by 2030, with AI comprising 8–39% of the economy. Long-term annual growth expectations reach approximately 7%, though with substantial uncertainty. The analysis projects the risk-free rate rising by about 0.5 percentage points and the equity premium increasing roughly 3 percentage points. The work directly addresses the economics of the AI transition, technological productivity gains, and their macroeconomic implications.

## 5. Preference for Explainable AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Alex Chan
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35240
**Abstract:** The study examines how loan officers use AI-generated default-risk predictions when deciding on real $10,000 loans. Key findings include: when explanations revealed bias against non-White or female borrowers, participants were more likely to override the AI's profit-maximizing recommendation. Under financial incentives tied to repayment, participants exhibited willful ignorance—seeking predictions while avoiding explanations. This avoidance pattern weakened when explanations were reframed as financial-only or when demographics were hidden. A secondary experiment identified a novel bias: participants failed to reason contingently and undervalued explanations despite their potential to improve decision accuracy. The results have direct implications for AI regulation, algorithmic accountability, and the design of explainability requirements in high-stakes decision environments.
