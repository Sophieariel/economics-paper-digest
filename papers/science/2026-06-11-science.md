# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-06-11

## 1. Is the Dominance of Graduates from Top-Tier Universities among Tenured Faculty Driven by Prestige or Output? Evidence from 50 Years of University Appointments in Germany [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Stefan Buechele, Guido Buenstorf, Matthias Huegel, Johannes Koenig, and Maria Theissen
**Date:** May 2026 (Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 3, Article 105411)
**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2026.105411
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether the concentration of tenured faculty among graduates of a small number of prestigious universities reflects genuine competitive advantages or simply mirrors the larger PhD output from those institutions. Using a comprehensive dataset covering 50 years of professorial appointments at German universities, the authors link doctoral graduation records to full appointment histories and apply duration models that compare appointment probabilities across PhD-granting institutions conditional on observable research output. The top five doctoral-granting universities supply 17.9 percent of all tenured professors. However, after controlling for institutional PhD production volumes, the authors find no evidence of a systematic hiring premium for graduates of prestigious institutions—concentration in faculty appointments closely tracks concentration in the output of early-career researchers. Graduates of top-tier programs who obtain initial appointments elsewhere are more likely to return to institutions of similar prestige over time, a pattern driven by network and reputation effects rather than research productivity differentials. The results indicate that the prestige stratification visible in German academic hiring reflects supply-side concentration rather than demand-side bias, with direct implications for policies aimed at diversifying the academic labor market.

## 2. Characterizing the File Drawer: Evidence from a Meta-Analysis of Parent-Interventions Around the World [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Peter Bergman and Nat Chowanajin
**Date:** June 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35299)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35299
**Abstract:** This paper conducts a meta-analysis of 82 randomized controlled trials across more than 20 countries examining low-cost parental engagement interventions delivered through text messages, phone calls, and apps. The authors estimate a joint likelihood function that incorporates both published and unpublished studies identified through trial registries, funder records, and research labs. By recording sample sizes for unpublished studies, the model estimates standard error distributions and write-up probabilities conditional on statistical significance, directly characterizing the shape and magnitude of the file drawer. Bias-corrected effect estimates are modest (0.03–0.07 SD across outcomes). Statistically insignificant results show surprisingly high write-up rates in test score domains, suggesting this literature has less publication bias than commonly assumed for education research. The analysis also identifies that larger studies estimate smaller effects, consistent with true effects varying with study precision rather than simple sampling error. The authors conclude that future research value lies primarily in explaining heterogeneity across settings rather than producing additional individual trials, and that journal acceptance of null results in this area is higher than the field-drawer model predicts. The paper contributes to the economics-of-science literature on publication bias, peer review, and the self-corrective mechanisms of empirical social science.

## 3. What Investment Data Implies about the AI Transition [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Jessica Wachter and Jonathan Wachter
**Date:** June 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35290)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35290
**Abstract:** Major U.S. technology firms spent $380 billion on capital expenditure in 2025, with projections to double in 2026. This paper asks what these massive investments imply about the expected productivity gains from artificial intelligence. The authors embed the observed investment surge in a two-sector open-economy model and calibrate a productivity boom of approximately 2.7 times AI-sector output to match observed investment patterns through 2027. Simulated scenarios generate cumulative additions to GDP by 2030 ranging from 5 to 58 percentage points, with AI comprising 8 to 39 percent of the economy under the most expansive scenario. Long-term annual growth expectations average roughly 7 percent under rapid AI progress, though with considerable uncertainty. The model also generates financial market predictions: the risk-free rate increases by approximately half a percentage point, while the equity premium rises by roughly 3 percentage points under standard assumptions about risk preferences and elasticity of substitution. The paper provides a disciplined, data-grounded framework for interpreting the AI investment boom as a revealed-preference signal about aggregate productivity expectations, contributing to empirical evidence on how markets are pricing the AI transition.

## 4. Innovation without Borders? The Geography of Technological Diffusion [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ursel Baumann, Zoë B. Cullen, Ester Faia, Annalisa Ferrando, Ricardo Perez-Truglia, and Judit Rariga
**Date:** June 2026 (NBER Working Paper No. 35314)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35314
**Abstract:** This paper uses a large-scale field experiment to study how information about competitors' AI adoption affects firms' own investment decisions. A sample of 3,300 firms across twelve EU countries was surveyed about their perceptions of AI investment among domestic and foreign competitors; half the sample was provided with accurate data on actual AI adoption rates. Firms significantly underestimated competitor AI adoption both domestically and internationally and updated their expectations substantially upon receiving the information treatment. The treatment caused a large increase in firms' own planned AI investment rates. Examining strategic complementarities, the authors find strong domestic spillovers: a one percentage point increase in peers' expected domestic AI adoption raised individual firm investment rates by 0.570 percentage points. By contrast, cross-border effects proved statistically insignificant, indicating that information frictions and competitive dynamics in AI adoption function much more robustly within national boundaries than across international borders. The results shed light on why AI adoption may cluster geographically and suggest that national-level policy interventions—subsidies, information campaigns, or coordination mechanisms—can be particularly effective in accelerating diffusion relative to supranational approaches.

## 5. AI as an Innovation in the Method of Innovation: Implications for Productivity Growth [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Filippo Bontadini, Carol Corrado, Jonathan Haskel, and Cecilia Jona-Lasinio
**Date:** May 2026 (AEA Papers and Proceedings, Volume 116, pp. 36–40)
**Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pandp.20261036
**Abstract:** This paper examines how artificial intelligence affects labor productivity growth by treating AI simultaneously as a general-purpose technology and as a transformative force in the innovation process itself. The authors develop an analytical framework that distinguishes upstream innovation activities from downstream production operations, allowing AI's contributions to be decomposed into two channels: raising total factor productivity in upstream R&D and knowledge creation, and increasing intangible capital use in downstream production. Using national accounts data and R&D investment series for the United States, the authors find that software-related contributions—encompassing both software products and software R&D—accounted for approximately 50 percent of the 2 percent average growth rate in nonfarm business labor productivity observed between 2017 and 2024, and similarly explain about half of the 1.2 percentage point acceleration relative to the preceding 2012–2017 period. The paper demonstrates that AI is already materially affecting official productivity statistics and argues that standard growth accounting frameworks undercount AI's contribution by omitting its role in accelerating innovation-method productivity. The findings have implications for how economists measure intangible investment, attribute productivity growth across sectors, and evaluate the macroeconomic returns to AI-intensive R&D.
