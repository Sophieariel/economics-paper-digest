## 1. Writing Code vs. Shipping Code: Productivity Effects Across Generations of AI Coding Tools [Economics of AI]

**Authors:** Mert Demirer, Leon Musolff, Liyuan Yang
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35275 (NBER Working Paper 35275)
**Abstract:** This paper examines how productivity gains from AI coding tools evolve across successive tool generations and whether task-level improvements translate into final output. Using data on more than 100,000 GitHub developers combined with AI usage telemetry, the study employs a matched event-study design to compare three tool categories: autocomplete tools, interactive coding agents, and autonomous coding agents. The findings reveal substantial increases in coding activity—cumulative effects of 40%, 140%, and 180%, respectively. However, these gains attenuate sharply as output moves through the production hierarchy: the 180% gain falls to 50% for project counts and just 30% for actual software releases. The authors attribute this pattern to the "weak-link hypothesis": human bottlenecks in the production chain constrain AI-driven gains, with an estimated elasticity of substitution of 0.25 between AI and human effort, indicating strong complementarities. Results are validated across four major app marketplaces, finding a moderate increase in new apps but no growth in total app usage. The authors conclude that "large task-level AI productivity gains have therefore translated only partially into shipped and used software thus far."

## 2. The Adoption of Industrial AI in America [Economics of AI]

**Authors:** Kristina McElheran, Mu-Jeung Yang, Zachary Kroff, Erik Brynjolfsson
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fpandp.20261033 (AEA Papers and Proceedings, vol. 116, pp. 20–25)
**Abstract:** This paper examines industrial AI adoption across U.S. manufacturing plants using Census Bureau survey data from approximately 28,500 establishments. Only 22.8 percent of plants report any AI use as of 2021, with intensity-weighted adoption substantially lower still. Adoption correlates strongly with newer digital infrastructure—cloud computing and predictive analytics—rather than legacy on-premises IT or descriptive analytics. Structured production-process management and firm size are significant positive predictors of adoption, while cost, lack of applicable use cases, and insufficient internal expertise are the most frequently cited barriers. Notably, prior productivity levels do not predict AI adoption, pointing to organizational readiness rather than productive capacity as the binding constraint on AI diffusion. The findings suggest that complementary organizational investments may be as critical as the AI technology itself for realizing productivity gains.

## 3. How Artificial Intelligence Shapes Science: Evidence from AlphaFold [Economics of AI]

**Authors:** Ryan R. Hill, Carolyn Stein
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35143 (NBER Working Paper 35143)
**Abstract:** This paper studies how a frontier AI model—AlphaFold2—shapes scientific discovery by examining its release in July 2021 and subsequent impact on structural biology and adjacent fields. In July 2021, researchers gained access to hundreds of thousands of AI-predicted protein structures virtually overnight. Yet experimental structure determination rates remained almost unchanged: scientists use predicted structures to complement and facilitate experimental work rather than to substitute for it. The study finds that AlphaFold2 redirected basic research toward previously understudied proteins, with research activity on such proteins increasing 15–40%. However, the authors find no evidence so far that more applied, early-stage drug development is targeting these newly illuminated proteins, though the authors note this may change as downstream scientific and regulatory bottlenecks are worked through. The results illustrate how AI can expand the frontier of scientific inquiry through complementarity with human expertise rather than simple automation.

## 4. Attention (And Money) Is All You Need: Why Universities Are Struggling to Keep AI Talent [Economics of Science]

**Authors:** Ufuk Akcigit, Craig A. Chikis, Emin Dinlersoz, Nathan Goldschlag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34964 (NBER Working Paper 34964)
**Abstract:** This paper tracks 42,000 AI professionals over two decades by linking academic publication data to U.S. Census Bureau employment records. The authors document a deepening divide between university and industry career paths for AI scientists. Industry increasingly attracts younger and foreign-born researchers, while academia has improved gender representation more substantially. A striking wage gap has emerged at the top of the earnings distribution: the top 1% of publishing industry scientists now earn $1.5 million more annually than comparable academic scientists, a fivefold increase since 2001. This compensation advantage is concentrated at a small number of large, established technology corporations. The study also documents a shift in research orientation: scientists who move to industry publish less but patent more, consistent with a transition from open science to proprietary innovation. These findings suggest that the competition for AI talent between universities and firms has intensified sharply, with significant implications for the openness of AI research and the long-run direction of scientific progress.

## 5. Knowledge Spillovers and Local Outcomes: An Existence Proof from the Establishment of the National Labs [Economics of Science]

**Authors:** Susan Helper, Resem Makan, Daniel W. Shoag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35011 (NBER Working Paper 35011)
**Abstract:** This paper estimates the local economic effects of knowledge spillovers by examining U.S. National Laboratories established during the 1940s and 1950s. The authors exploit archival evidence identifying runner-up candidate locations as counterfactuals, since lab sites were selected for security and political reasons rather than for existing or potential innovative capacity—providing plausibly exogenous variation in lab placement. Using newly digitized historical economic data spanning 1936–1970, the study finds significant increases in patenting activity by non-laboratory inventors in host counties, with patents increasingly concentrated in laboratory research domains and citing lab work more frequently. These knowledge spillovers translated into sustained growth in retail sales and household income in host communities. Census record analysis shows wage improvements for long-term residents, particularly college-educated workers. Additionally, cohorts of young people who were of school age at the time of laboratory establishment achieved higher educational attainment, identifying human capital accumulation as a key spillover mechanism. Notably, these substantial local benefits emerged despite extensive government secrecy around early nuclear research at many of the labs.
