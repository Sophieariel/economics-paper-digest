## 1. Attention (And Money) Is All You Need: Why Universities Are Struggling to Keep AI Talent [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ufuk Akcigit, Craig A. Chikis, Emin Dinlersoz, Nathan Goldschlag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34964
**Abstract:** We construct a novel dataset linking academic publication records to U.S. Census Bureau employer-employee data to track 42,000 AI researchers over two decades. We document systematic changes in the allocation of AI talent: industry increasingly attracts younger and foreign-born researchers, while gender representation improves more in academia. A striking and widening compensation gap has emerged—the top 1% of publishing industry scientists now earn $1.5 million more annually than comparable academics, a fivefold increase since 2001. Researchers who move to industry publish fewer papers but generate more patents, suggesting a shift from open science toward proprietary innovation. Our findings reveal a structural realignment of the scientific workforce with implications for the pace and direction of AI research and for the public accessibility of scientific knowledge.

## 2. The Economics of Postdoctoral Researcher Positions [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Donna K. Ginther, Joshua L. Rosenbloom
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35014
**Abstract:** We examine postdoctoral researchers in the U.S. scientific workforce from 1979 to 2023, documenting a fourfold increase in postdoc numbers that outpaced growth in graduate students and faculty. We attribute this expansion to the fragmented nature of science funding and the NIH budget doubling in the early 2000s, which increased demand for postdocs while simultaneously worsening their employment conditions. Using linked administrative and survey data, we show that the economic returns to postdoctoral training outside academia are limited, and that transitions to tenure-track faculty positions have declined sharply. Recent trends suggest the U.S. may have reached "peak postdoc," potentially leading to reduced future research output. We argue that improved data collection and further study of postdocs' roles in scientific productivity are urgently needed.

## 3. Preference for Explainable AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Alex Chan
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35240
**Abstract:** I examine how decision-makers use AI predictions and explanations when the stakes are real. Participants act as loan officers deciding whether to approve actual $10,000 loans using an AI's default-risk predictions. When explanations reveal that the AI penalizes non-White or female borrowers, participants are more likely to override the AI's profit-maximizing recommendation. When bonuses depend on repayment outcomes, however, decision-makers seek predictions but actively avoid explanations—behavior consistent with strategic willful ignorance. This avoidance diminishes when explanations emphasize financial rather than demographic factors. A secondary experiment identifies a novel bias: participants fail to reason contingently and systematically undervalue explanations even when these insights complement their private information and would improve decision accuracy. The findings have implications for the design of AI governance policies and for understanding why firms may rationally limit the explainability of consequential automated decisions.

## 4. Firm Data on AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ivan Yotzov, Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Philip Bunn, Steven J. Davis, Kevin M. Foster, Aaron Jalca, Brent H. Meyer, Paul Mizen, Michael A. Navarrete, Pawel Smietanka, Gregory Thwaites, Ben Zhe Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836
**Abstract:** We survey approximately 6,000 senior executives at firms in the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, and Australia to provide new, globally comparable data on how businesses implement AI technologies. We find that 69% of firms actively use AI, with higher adoption rates among younger and more productive companies. Despite widespread adoption, over nine-in-ten executives reported no effect on employment or productivity over the previous three years. Looking forward, executives anticipate more substantial changes: AI is projected to increase productivity by 1.4% on average, raise output by 0.8%, and reduce employment by 0.7% over the next three years. Notably, employees expect employment to rise 0.5%, revealing a significant expectations gap between management and workers. The expected productivity impact of AI over the next three years increased from +1.5% to +1.9% between early and late 2025, suggesting accelerating optimism about AI's near-term effects.

## 5. Artificial Intelligence, Productivity, and the Workforce: Evidence from Corporate Executives [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Salomé Baslandze, Zachary Edwards, John R. Graham, Ty McClure, Brent H. Meyer, Michael Sparks, Sonya R. Waddell, Daniel Weitz
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34984
**Abstract:** We use novel survey data from approximately 750 corporate executives to study how AI adoption affects organizational productivity and workforce composition. We document substantial heterogeneity in AI adoption across firms, with more than half having already invested, while many smaller firms are only beginning to do so. Labor productivity gains are positive and vary across sectors, with the largest anticipated increases concentrated in high-skill services and finance. Crucially, these gains are not primarily driven by capital deepening but reflect increases in revenue-based total factor productivity, closely associated with innovation- and demand-oriented channels. We identify a productivity paradox: perceived productivity gains are consistently larger than measured productivity gains, likely attributable to delays in revenue realizations. On the workforce margin, large firms anticipate employment reductions while smaller firms expect modest expansion, and occupational composition is shifting away from routine clerical roles toward advanced technical positions.
