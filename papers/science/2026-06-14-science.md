## 1. What Investment Data Implies about the AI Transition [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Jessica Wachter, Jonathan Wachter
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35290
**Abstract:** The five largest U.S. technology firms spent $380 billion on capital expenditure in 2025, with projections to roughly double that figure in 2026. We argue these firms face financial risk unless profits increase proportionally. Using a two-sector general equilibrium model, we calibrate the size of a productivity boom that would justify observed investment levels, finding it implies that AI-sector productivity rises by a factor of approximately 2.7. Under our baseline calibration, scenarios for cumulative GDP growth by 2030 range from 5 to 58 percentage points, with AI economy shares between 8 and 39 percent. Long-term annual growth expectations reach roughly 7 percent, though with significant uncertainty. The risk-free rate would rise approximately 0.5 percentage points and equity premiums would increase by roughly 3 percentage points under standard risk preferences. Our framework allows investment data to discipline quantitative assessments of the macroeconomic transition underway.

## 2. Innovation without Borders? The Geography of Technological Diffusion [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ursel Baumann, Zoë B. Cullen, Ester Faia, Annalisa Ferrando, Ricardo Perez-Truglia, Judit Rariga
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35314
**Abstract:** We conduct a large-scale field experiment with 3,300 firms across twelve EU countries to examine how technological innovation—specifically AI investment—diffuses geographically. We find that firms substantially underestimate their competitors' AI investment both domestically and internationally. When provided with accurate information about peer investment, firms update their own expectations about future AI adoption. The experiment reveals strong strategic complementarities within national borders: a one-percentage-point increase in expected domestic AI adoption by peers raises a firm's own expected AI investment by 0.57 percentage points. Foreign peer effects, by contrast, are statistically insignificant. These findings suggest that AI adoption may cluster within rather than across borders, with implications for the international distribution of productivity gains and for cross-border coordination of technology policy.

## 3. The Growth and Performance of Artificial Intelligence in Asset Management [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Shuang Chen, Clemens Sialm, David X. Xu
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35273
**Abstract:** We examine the growth and performance of AI-driven investing using investment advisers' regulatory disclosures, labor market data, and fund strategy descriptions. We document that AI-driven investing has grown steadily since the early 2010s and is concentrated among hedge funds. AI hedge funds initially outperformed their non-AI counterparts by a statistically and economically significant margin, but this performance advantage diminished over time as more funds adopted similar AI strategies. Contrary to concerns about herding, AI-driven funds display lower return comovement than traditional alternatives, suggesting their strategies remain differentiated. Our results illuminate how AI adoption evolves within a competitive industry, the conditions under which early movers capture rents, and the mechanisms by which those advantages erode as technology diffuses.

## 4. Person and Place Effects in Scientific Discovery [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Amitabh Chandra, Connie Xu
**Date:** July 2025 (revised April 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33996
**Abstract:** We decompose the variation in scientific output into contributions from individual scientists and from the institutions at which they work, using citation-weighted publications in leading life-science journals for nearly 40,000 scientists who changed institutions over our sample period. We find that 50 to 60 percent of the variation in a scientist's research output is attributable to institutional factors rather than to the individual scientist's portable human capital. Positive assortative matching between scientists and institutions is pervasive, with roughly two-thirds of the institution fixed effect explained by the presence of star researchers at the same institution. Scientists respond in their output to their institution's total research output and total NIH funding, but not to the size of their local research cluster, suggesting that agglomeration economies in science operate through observable institutional outputs rather than through proximity per se. Institutional advantages do not permanently transfer when scientists relocate—productivity reverts toward the destination institution's average after a move—and these patterns persist despite the rise of remote collaboration technologies.

## 5. Lottery before Peer Review Is Associated with Increased Female Representation and Reduced Estimated Economic Cost in a German Funding Line [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Finn Luebber, Sören Krach, Frieder M. Paulus, Lena Rademacher, Rima-Maria Rahal
**Date:** November 2025
**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12592401/ *(Nature Communications)*
**Abstract:** Research funding is a key determinant of scientific progress, but current grant allocation procedures are criticized for high administrative costs and systematic biases in reviewer assessments. We study a German funding organization's implementation of a lottery-first approach in which applicants submit brief expressions of interest (1,500 characters); a random lottery then selects approximately 15 percent to advance to full proposal submission and conventional peer review. Comparing outcomes before and after the reform, we find a 10 percent increase in total applications and a 23 percent increase in funded projects from female applicants under the new procedure, relative to the previous purely peer-reviewed allocation. Estimated administrative costs fall by approximately 68 percent compared to conventional peer review alone. Our results indicate that partial randomization can simultaneously reduce gender gaps in funding access and substantially lower the burden on applicants and reviewers, with implications for optimal design of competitive science funding.
