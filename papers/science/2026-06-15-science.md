## 1. Estimating the Present Value of R&D Tax Benefits in the United States [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Brandon Pecoraro, Nicholas C. Hoffman, Martin Lopez-Daneri, Elena C. Derby, Rachel Moore, and Shannon E. Sledz
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35208
**Abstract:** We estimate the present value of R&D tax benefits realized by U.S. corporations using confidential corporate income tax return data. The statutory rate of R&D tax benefit is approximately $0.41 per dollar of R&D investment, but the realized present value is only $0.36—a discount driven by the timing of tax attribute utilization. The gap is substantially larger for young and unprofitable firms, which realize just $0.23 per dollar of R&D investment, a 44 percent reduction relative to the statutory rate, because unused tax attributes must be carried forward to future profitable years when they can be applied against positive tax liability. These firms face the largest discount precisely when early-stage R&D investment is typically most valuable. Our findings imply that the existing U.S. R&D tax incentive framework provides substantially weaker support to young innovative firms than statutory rates suggest, with direct implications for the design and evaluation of science funding policy and for understanding the effective cost of capital for research-intensive startups.

## 2. The Microstructure of AI Diffusion: Evidence from Firms, Business Functions, and Worker Tasks [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Kathryn Bonney, Cory L. Breaux, Emin Dinlersoz, Lucia S. Foster, John C. Haltiwanger, and Aditya A. Pande
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35141
**Abstract:** We characterize the adoption of artificial intelligence across three organizational levels—firms, business functions, and worker tasks—using a new AI supplement to the Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey conducted in late 2025 and early 2026. Approximately 18 percent of firms deploy AI in at least one business function (32 percent weighted by employment), with adoption highly concentrated among large firms in knowledge-intensive sectors: 50–60 percent of very large firms in Information, Professional Services, and Finance report AI use. Among adopters, 57 percent deploy AI in three or fewer functions, most commonly Sales and Marketing (52 percent), Strategic Planning (45 percent), and IT (41 percent). Worker-level AI use is reported by 23 percent of firms (41 percent employment-weighted), primarily for writing and document analysis. We find evidence of both top-down organizational adoption and bottom-up worker-driven adoption occurring independently, with different correlates and consequences. Notably, firm-level and functional-level AI deployment correlates with subsequent employment reductions, while worker-task-level AI use does not, suggesting that organizational integration of AI carries more disruptive labor market implications than individual-level technology adoption.

## 3. Old Space, New Space: A Commercial Revolution in Innovation? [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ruben Gaetani and Alexander T. Whalley
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35212
**Abstract:** We use patent data spanning 1963–2022 to study how innovation in the space sector evolved across the transition from government-dominated to commercially-driven activity. A substantial wave of space-related patenting began in the 1990s, well before widely recognized commercial entrants appeared after 2005. Incumbent aerospace firms account for the majority of space-related patenting throughout the sample, with entrants contributing a growing but still minority share. The geographic concentration of space innovation has proved remarkably persistent: regions that led innovation in the post-Apollo era continue to dominate commercial space activity today. Our findings challenge the prevailing narrative of a clean break between "Old Space" and "New Space," suggesting instead that new entrants are more closely connected to established incumbents and to the government-sponsored research base than is commonly acknowledged. Government's primary role in enabling the commercial revolution appears to be the construction of appropriable markets rather than direct support of new entrants—a finding with broad implications for technology transition policy and for the economics of public R&D investment.

## 4. AI-Accelerated Occupational Decline and the Mobility Trap [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Xi Song, Jennie E. Brand, Sukie Xiuqi Yang, and Michael Lachanski
**Date:** May 2026 (AEA Papers and Proceedings, vol. 116, pp. 246–250)
**Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pandp.20261004
**Abstract:** Economic transformation challenges workers not only through job creation and loss but through reshaping the feasibility of occupational transitions. We examine whether AI-driven occupational restructuring—which accelerated after 2018 as transformer-based AI technologies entered widespread deployment—has created a "mobility trap" for workers in declining, high-AI-exposure occupations. Using BLS administrative data on occupational employment trends and Current Population Survey data on individual worker transitions, we track where workers in declining AI-exposed occupations move and with what earnings outcomes. Workers in these occupations are 5.2 times more likely to transition into another declining occupation than into a growing one. Approximately 70 percent of occupational movers from declining high-exposure occupations experience downward or lateral earnings mobility. These findings indicate that AI-driven restructuring entrenches economic disadvantage for workers in the most exposed declining occupations rather than facilitating upward mobility, with implications for labor market policy targeting technological displacement.

## 5. Does the Import Invasion Explain the Mysterious Disappearance of Productivity Growth in U.S. Manufacturing? [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Robert J. Gordon and Kenneth Ryu
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35285
**Abstract:** We investigate the collapse of U.S. manufacturing productivity growth from +3.3 percent per year during 1987–2010 to −0.3 percent per year during 2010–2023. We trace the roots of this reversal to 2000, when import competition—particularly from China following its WTO accession—caused manufacturing output to stagnate. Stagnating output triggered a cascade of consequences: capacity utilization fell, capital investment contracted, and R&D investment declined sharply, depressing the rate of process and product innovation. We decompose the productivity slowdown into demand-side effects (import competition reducing output and capacity utilization) and supply-side effects (reduced R&D and capital investment lowering innovation rates), finding that both channels are quantitatively important. Additional contributing factors include corporate share buybacks diverting funds from productive investment, regulatory constraints on manufacturing operations, and a reduction in public research funding. Our evidence provides a unified account of why American manufacturing innovation stalled, linking trade exposure to the contraction of business R&D investment and documenting the resulting attenuation of productivity growth—with direct implications for the economics of R&D policy and the design of public science funding in an era of intensified global competition.
