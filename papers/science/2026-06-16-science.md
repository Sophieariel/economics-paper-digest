## 1. AI in Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ajay K. Agrawal, John McHale, Alexander Oettl
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34953
**Abstract:** This paper examines how artificial intelligence affects the production of scientific knowledge. The authors characterize AI as an augmentation tool that enables "enhanced search over combinatorial spaces," increasing research productivity without fully automating inquiry. Drawing on a task-based economic model, they decompose knowledge production into multiple workflow stages to illuminate a "jagged frontier": AI tools deliver large returns in some domains and stages (such as AlphaFold for protein structure design) and more modest returns elsewhere (such as question generation in data-sparse fields). Human judgment remains critical for tasks requiring abductive reasoning, and AI expertise among researchers generates nonlinear productivity gains. The paper predicts that fields and institutions fastest to build AI-complementary human capital will experience the steepest increases in scientific output, with implications for research policy, graduate training, and the geographic concentration of scientific discovery.

## 2. The Social Returns to Public R&D [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Andrew J. Fieldhouse, Karel Mertens
**Date:** May 2025
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33780
**Abstract:** This paper examines the causal link between federal nondefense R&D spending and private-sector productivity growth. Using a fiscal policy shock approach, the authors identify plausibly exogenous variation in public R&D outlays and trace their downstream effects on total factor productivity in the private sector. Their estimates point to large social returns: a sustained increase in nondefense R&D spending raises private-sector productivity substantially, with peak effects materializing over a seven-to-ten-year horizon. Full appropriation of the R&D expansions authorized under the CHIPS and Science Act would, under their estimates, raise productivity by 0.2–0.4 percent after seven years, with the direct productivity effects of the implied expansion in nondefense R&D alone exceeding $40 billion in annual output at their peak—more than the total CHIPS Act R&D provisions over a decade. The authors also assess the fiscal tradeoffs of R&D cuts, finding that deficit reduction achieved by trimming public research investment would carry more severe long-run productivity costs than previous fiscal consolidations. The paper contributes causal empirical evidence to the longstanding debate over the magnitude of social returns to publicly funded science.

## 3. Does Generative AI Narrow Education-Based Productivity Gaps? Evidence from a Randomized Experiment [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Guillermo Cruces, Diego Fernández Meijide, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro H. Gálvez, María Lombardi
**Date:** February 2026 (revised May 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34851
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether generative AI reduces or reinforces the productivity gap between workers with different levels of educational attainment. The authors conduct a randomized online experiment with 1,174 adults aged 25–45 who complete workplace-style business problem-solving tasks with or without access to a generative AI assistant, followed by non-AI follow-up tasks. The experiment finds that AI access raises performance for all participants, with substantially larger gains for lower-education individuals. The baseline education gap of 0.548 standard deviations narrows to 0.139 with AI access—closing approximately three-quarters of the initial gap. Analysis of chat logs reveals that lower-education workers successfully leveraged AI assistance, though higher-education participants used the tool somewhat more effectively at the margin. Importantly, follow-up testing without AI shows that treated participants retained some gains, indicating the benefits were not purely due to task delegation; however, underlying human-capital differences reasserted themselves when AI was removed. The findings suggest that generative AI has meaningful equalizing potential for task-level productivity, while also underscoring that durable human-capital gaps persist and will shape the distribution of long-run AI benefits.

## 4. How (un)Stable Are LLM Occupational Exposure Scores? Evidence from Multi-Model Replication [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Michelle Yin, Hoa Vu, Claudia Persico
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35110
**Abstract:** A large empirical literature uses large language model (LLM) outputs to measure the exposure of occupations to AI and studies the downstream labor market consequences. This paper challenges the measurement validity underlying that literature. The authors replicate a widely-used occupational exposure scoring methodology across three advanced LLMs applied to identical tasks and find substantial inconsistency: mean exposure scores diverge 3.6-fold across models, with inter-model agreement as low as 57 percent. These measurement discrepancies propagate into downstream empirical analysis: coefficient estimates on AI exposure in labor demand regressions vary 2.4-fold depending on which LLM generated the scores, and county-level estimates of employment effects shift from statistically significant and negative to statistically insignificant and positive depending on model choice. The authors attribute these instabilities to rapid capability changes across LLM generations, non-classical measurement error structures, and the sensitivity of natural-language-based assessments to prompt framing. The findings raise significant concerns about the reproducibility of AI exposure research and argue for standardized measurement protocols, model-version disclosure, and robustness checks across multiple LLMs as minimum standards for empirical work in this literature.

## 5. Forecasting the Economic Effects of AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ezra Karger, Otto Kuusela, Jason Abaluck, Kevin A. Bryan, Basil Halperin, Todd R. Jones, Connacher Murphy, Philip Trammell, Matt Reynolds, Dan Mayland, Ria Viswanathan, Ananaya Mittal, Rebecca Ceppas de Castro, Josh Rosenberg, Philip Tetlock
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35046
**Abstract:** This paper elicits and compares AI economic impact forecasts from five distinct groups: academic economists, AI company employees, AI-focused policy researchers, professional forecasters with strong track records, and the general public. Respondents anticipate substantial advances in AI capabilities by 2030 and project annual GDP growth near 2.5 percent, exceeding typical baseline forecasts. Under rapid AI progress scenarios, experts project GDP growth approaching 4 percent annually and labor force participation falling from 62 to 55 percent by 2050, with approximately half of projected job losses attributed to AI. Disagreement among groups stems primarily from differing views on the economic consequences of highly capable AI systems rather than from conflicting predictions about the pace of AI development itself. Policy preferences diverge across groups: academic economists favor worker retraining and wage subsidies, while the general public more strongly supports broader structural interventions such as job guarantees and universal basic income. The paper contributes new quantitative evidence on expert and public expectations about AI's labor market and macroeconomic consequences, providing a benchmark for evaluating how well forecasters are tracking AI's actual economic trajectory.
