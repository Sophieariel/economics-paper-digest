## 1. Before the Exodus? Young Scientists and the Future of US Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Raffaella Sadun, Daniela Scur
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35330
**Abstract:** Shortly after major policy changes to US science funding began in early 2025, we surveyed 916 young biomedical scientists—PhD students and postdoctoral researchers—about their career intentions and expectations. The results document a dramatic shift in sentiment. Barely half of respondents now say they are likely to remain in academia, down 22 percentage points from how they felt six months earlier. The fraction likely to stay in the United States fell by 21 percentage points. Even satisfaction with having pursued a PhD in science declined by 16 percentage points. These shifts in attitudes and intentions are present across gender, race, citizenship status, degree type, and career stage. They represent concerns about the future of the next generation of principal investigators in the United States.

## 2. Merit or Networks? What Decides Where Research Is Published [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ning Li
**Date:** June 2, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03763
**Abstract:** Does scientific publishing reward idea quality or professional connections? We deploy an LLM evaluator to assess paper quality from text alone and analyze 6,208 economics working papers. Execution quality sets a meritocratic floor and is the largest single input to publication outcomes overall. Connections primarily influence placement at top-tier journals, where they provide a statistically and economically significant advantage even after conditioning on measured quality. Idea originality matters most for placement in the middle tier of journals. We conclude that merit and networks are both operative: execution quality establishes baseline standards that must be met for any placement, idea quality determines intermediate rankings, and connections confer advantages—especially at elite venues—without making excellence irrelevant. The results are consistent with a model of peer review in which reviewer effort is rationally limited and social signals serve as a low-cost shortcut for editors making acceptance decisions.

## 3. The Privilege of Exposure: Caste and Generative AI in India's Graduate Labour Market [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Kaibalyapati Mishra
**Date:** June 11, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13314
**Abstract:** I examine generative AI exposure disparities among India's educated workforce using data on approximately 83,000 employed graduates. Workers from Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (SC/ST) are 0.24–0.37 standard deviations less exposed to generative AI than upper-caste graduates. Two mechanisms account for this gap: first, a large share of SC/ST graduates work in occupations with negligible AI penetration (farm and elementary work); second, among white-collar workers, SC/ST graduates remain underrepresented in the high-exposure roles—management, software development, and finance—where generative AI is most widely deployed. Because AI exposure carries an estimated wage premium of up to 20 percent, the uneven distribution of AI access risks expanding rather than narrowing existing caste-based earnings inequality in India. The findings caution against assuming that AI diffusion will be equality-enhancing in contexts where baseline occupational segregation is severe.

## 4. When the Scaffold Stays On: AI, Practice Style, and Screening in Elite Skill Formation [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Song Yao
**Date:** June 4, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06253
**Abstract:** I examine whether generative AI erodes frontier skill development by substituting for deliberate practice, and whether institutional screening can distinguish between substitute-users—who use AI in place of practicing—and complement-users—who use AI to accelerate learning. I analyze programming competitions: ICPC and IOI prohibit AI assistance during contests and screen participants through qualifying rounds, whereas open Codeforces contests are unproctored. I find that Codeforces practice shifted toward an "AI-prompt signature" across successive cohorts; this signature predicts weaker non-AI-aided performance gains for unscreened users, but predicts stronger gains for participants who subsequently qualified for AI-prohibited elite competitions. The same observed practice behavior yields opposite skill outcomes depending on the institutional screening regime, indicating that AI screening selects on the intensive margin of how AI is used, not merely whether it is used. These results suggest that the human-capital consequences of AI access depend critically on the incentive structures and credentialing institutions that surround it.

## 5. U.S. Policies Unintentionally Accelerated China's Open AI Ecosystems [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Wang Jin, Nadav Kunievsky, Bowen Lou, Tianshu Sun, James Evans
**Date:** June 14, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15999
**Abstract:** We examine how American efforts to maintain AI dominance through domestic market competition and export controls on advanced semiconductors inadvertently strengthened China's open-source AI development. Following the imposition of chip export restrictions, Chinese policymakers increasingly integrated open-source AI into national strategy through ecosystem development and standards coordination. Using data on developer activity across open-source LLM repositories, we find that Chinese developers increased engagement with open-source large language model repositories substantially more than US developers did over the same period. Chinese open models subsequently gained traction across international research communities and open-source platforms, though they remain underrepresented in American patent filings. Notably, American companies utilize these models in publicly available research despite limited disclosure in commercial applications. Our findings suggest that technological containment measures may paradoxically accelerate open innovation as a competitive adaptation strategy, with implications for the global distribution of AI capabilities and for the design of future technology policy.
