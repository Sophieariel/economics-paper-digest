## 1. Before the Exodus? Young Scientists and the Future of US Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Raffaella Sadun, Daniela Scur
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35330
**Abstract:** Following substantial policy shifts in early 2025 that disrupted federal research funding and immigration pathways, the authors survey 916 young biomedical scientists to document how these changes are reshaping career intentions for the next generation of researchers. The results are striking: only half of respondents now plan to remain in academia, a 22-percentage-point decline relative to six months earlier. The share intending to stay in the United States dropped by 21 percentage points, and reported satisfaction with pursuing a science PhD fell 16 points. The authors emphasize that these responses reflect "the stated intentions of the next generation—the scientists who would, in ordinary times, become the principal investigators of the future," rather than complaints from established researchers with established positions. Because these early-career scientists represent the pipeline for the next wave of principal investigators, the documented shift in career expectations has long-run implications for U.S. scientific capacity, the location of frontier research activity, and the returns to investments in training programs. The paper contributes to the literature on scientist labor markets, the determinants of scientific career choice, and the sensitivity of human-capital allocation in science to public policy.

## 2. Deep Research on a Loop: Using AI Agents to Construct Economic Datasets [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Santiago Afonso, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro H. Gálvez, Raul A. Sosa
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35188
**Abstract:** This paper proposes and demonstrates DRIL (Deep Research on a Loop), an AI-agent-based methodology for assembling large economic datasets from publicly available primary sources at a fraction of the traditional research-assistant cost. The framework employs a two-stage architecture that separates research design from execution: a fixed research instrument defines the variables and coding rules, while an autonomous AI agent collects, evaluates, and documents evidence according to a pre-specified evidence policy governing sources, citations, and quality controls. The approach explicitly tracks data gaps and uncertainty rather than silently imputing. The authors test DRIL on a 2025 update of the Global Tax Expenditures Database for eight Latin American and Caribbean countries, generating 129 primary sources and 136 evidence records—covering 22 qualitative fields fully and 6 quantitative estimate types with documented gaps—at a cost equivalent to a standard LLM subscription, comparable to a few hours of research-assistant time. The paper contributes to the economics of scientific research by demonstrating how AI-assisted data collection can reshape the production process of empirical economics, lowering barriers to constructing novel, country-level datasets and enabling more systematic evidence standards in comparative policy research.

## 3. Data-Driven Automation [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Maryam Farboodi, Andrew J. Koh, Anchi Xia
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35320
**Abstract:** This paper develops a dynamic general-equilibrium model of automation in which data—rather than conventional capital—is the engine of task displacement. The authors treat data as heterogeneous and task-specific, accumulating endogenously as a byproduct of production activity; crucially, data generated in automating one task can spill over and lower the cost of automating related tasks, creating an endogenous diffusion process. The model yields a rich typology of long-run outcomes: depending on the breadth of spillovers and initial conditions, the economy may converge to partial or full automation; under full automation, the share of tasks performed by labor decays as a power law in time, implying gradual rather than sudden displacement. A key macroeconomic finding is that data accumulation generates rapid near-term output growth but leads to stagnant long-run wages, because the gains from automation accrue overwhelmingly to data owners rather than workers. The authors show the decentralized economy is generically inefficient and characterize the social planner's optimal redirection of data accumulation. The paper contributes to the empirical and structural literature on AI, automation, and inequality by providing a micro-founded mechanism linking data economies of scale to the long-run distribution of income between labor and capital.

## 4. AI for Structural Estimation [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Victor Duarte, Julia Fonseca
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35283
**Abstract:** This paper develops and demonstrates an AI-powered framework that dramatically accelerates the solution and estimation of dynamic structural economic models, reducing computation time from days to minutes. The authors extend the state space of such models to incorporate equilibrium prices and structural parameters, then train neural networks on simulated data to approximate the mapping from structural parameters to empirical moments. This learned mapping is then used for fast, simulation-based estimation in place of repeated model solution. The authors demonstrate the method on a complex dynamic model of firm leverage and investment, completing estimation in under 20 minutes where traditional numerical methods failed to converge after four days. They also develop an AI agent that reads model specifications from natural language prompts and automatically applies the estimation framework, lowering the technical barrier to structural methods. The paper contributes to the economics of AI by documenting a concrete and large productivity gain from AI in research: quantifying how LLM-backed agents can reshape the econometric workflow, with implications for which questions become tractable and for the allocation of research effort across methods.

## 5. When Does Automating AI Research Produce Explosive Growth? Feedback Loops in Innovation Networks [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Tom Davidson, Basil Halperin, Thomas Houlden, Anton Korinek
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35155
**Abstract:** This paper asks under what conditions AI systems used to automate AI research itself could trigger superexponential (explosive) economic growth. The authors develop a semi-endogenous growth model structured as an innovation network, in which advances in one research area raise productivity in others. They identify two mutually reinforcing feedback mechanisms: a technological feedback loop, in which higher research productivity in AI accelerates innovation in the sectors AI targets, and an economic feedback loop, in which the resulting output growth funds expanded AI research investment. The paper derives analytical conditions under which these loops overcome diminishing returns to innovation and generate explosive, self-reinforcing growth trajectories. Simulations calibrated to observed AI capability progress suggest that fully automating software research, combined with only modest (5%) automation in other sectors, could produce a "singularity"—output growing without bound—within six years. The authors demonstrate that even substantial production bottlenecks in physical inputs may not prevent explosive growth if task automation advances rapidly enough. The paper contributes to the empirical literature on the returns to R&D and to debates about whether AI represents a general-purpose technology capable of endogenously accelerating its own development, with direct implications for science policy, competition policy, and long-run growth accounting.
