## 1. Before the Exodus? Young Scientists and the Future of US Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Raffaella Sadun, Daniela Scur
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35330
**Abstract:** We survey 916 PhD students and postdoctoral researchers in the United States to document shifts in career intentions following policy changes to US science funding in early 2025. Barely half of respondents now say they are likely to remain in academia, a decline of 22 percentage points from how they felt six months earlier. Interest in remaining in the United States decreased by 21 percentage points, and satisfaction with pursuing a PhD in science fell by 16 percentage points. These changes are concentrated among researchers in the biological, physical, and social sciences and are not explained by differences in advisor quality, funding status, or career stage. The findings suggest that the cohort of young scientists most likely to become future research leaders is reconsidering both its commitment to academic science and its presence in the United States, with potential long-run consequences for US scientific output and competitiveness.

## 2. What Makes New Work Different from More Work? [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** David Autor, Caroline Chin, Anna M. Salomons, Bryan Seegmiller
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34986
**Abstract:** We study the characteristics and wage dynamics of new work—tasks and occupations that did not previously exist—using historical Census data from 1940–1950 and contemporary American Community Survey records. We document that new work systematically attracts younger and more educated workers and offers economically significant wage premiums relative to existing work at the time of emergence. These premiums persist beyond workers' initial entry into new occupations but decline across vintages as expertise diffuses and the work becomes more routinized. We also show that new work tends to emerge in response to specific demand shocks in particular locations and time periods. Our findings suggest that new work, including the work created by AI and automation, functions as a counterbalance to automation-driven labor displacement by generating new domains of human expertise that command sustained market premiums—at least for workers with the skills to enter these domains early.

## 3. The Skill Premium in Times of Rapid Technological Change [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Tarek Alexander Hassan, Aakash Kalyani, Pascual Restrepo
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34939
**Abstract:** We develop a theory in which the pace of technology creation is the primary driver of the college wage premium. Skilled workers hold a comparative advantage in adopting newly created technologies; as technologies mature and become standardized, their use spreads to less-skilled workers, eroding the initial wage gap. Calibrating the model to US data, we can account for a projected 32 percent increase in the college premium beginning in the 1970s and its subsequent reversal during the 2010s, without relying on changes in the supply of skilled workers or in the skill-intensity of specific technologies. The framework also accounts for higher skill premiums in dense urban areas—where technology creation is faster—and for differential timing of premium changes across age groups. Consistent with the mechanism, 57 percent of jobs associated with a newly created technology require a college degree at emergence, declining to only 34 percent eighty years later as technology standardizes. The results imply that the current rapid pace of AI development could renew upward pressure on skill premiums.

## 4. Merit or Networks? What Decides Where Research Is Published [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ning Li
**Date:** June 2, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03763
**Abstract:** I study the determinants of publication outcomes for 6,208 economics working papers using a language model evaluator to measure idea quality and execution quality directly from paper text before publication outcomes are known. I find that these three factors—merit (idea quality and execution quality) and coauthor networks—operate sequentially rather than competitively. Execution quality establishes a merit-based threshold that papers must cross to be publishable at all; idea quality determines rankings among papers that clear this threshold; and coauthor connections provide advantages primarily at top-tier journals by raising placement odds across tiers but not guaranteeing elite journal acceptance for ordinary ideas. The findings offer the first large-scale quantitative decomposition of the publication process in economics and suggest that quality-based and network-based mechanisms reinforce rather than substitute for each other in determining where research is published.

## 5. The Privilege of Exposure: Caste and Generative AI in India's Graduate Labour Market [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Kaibalyapati Mishra
**Date:** June 11, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13314
**Abstract:** I study caste-based inequality in exposure to generative AI using data from 83,000 employed graduates in India's Periodic Labour Force Survey (2025). I find that graduates from Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes experience approximately 0.24–0.37 standard deviations lower AI exposure than upper-caste counterparts within the same region, industry, and occupation. This gap is not explained by differences in educational attainment, employer characteristics, or field of study; it persists within fine-grained occupational cells and appears to reflect differential access to AI-enabled tasks conditional on job title. Because AI exposure is associated with wage premiums of up to 20 percent, the technology is likely to exacerbate rather than reduce India's existing caste-based earnings inequality. The results contribute to a growing literature on distributional consequences of AI adoption and highlight how pre-existing social hierarchies shape which workers benefit from technological change.
