## 1. Before the Exodus? Young Scientists and the Future of US Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Raffaella Sadun, Daniela Scur
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35330
**Abstract:** Following policy shifts to US science funding in early 2025, we surveyed 916 young biomedical scientists regarding their career paths. The findings reveal a significant deterioration in career sentiment among this cohort: barely half of respondents now say they are likely to remain in academia, down 22 percentage points from six months prior. Similarly, the proportion planning to stay in the United States declined by 21 percentage points, while satisfaction with pursuing a PhD in science dropped 16 percentage points. These responses come from early-career scientists who would typically become the next generation of principal investigators—not from established scientists protecting current resources. The results raise concerns about a potential exodus of young talent from the US academic research pipeline at a moment when federal science funding faces heightened uncertainty.

## 2. AI, Output, and Employment [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Andrew C. Johnston, Christos A. Makridis
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5375017
**Abstract:** We ask whether artificial intelligence increases productivity—and whether it displaces workers. Using administrative data covering over 95% of U.S. employers across all industries from 2017–2024, we employ a difference-in-differences design that exploits occupational AI exposure across industries and states. A one standard deviation increase in AI exposure raises output by 7% and employment by 4%, with effects emerging in 2021 when enterprise AI tools entered the market. Distinguishing by task type, augmentative AI exposure—tasks requiring human partnership—raises employment; automating AI exposure has no statistically significant effect on employment. We also find that AI adoption has caused a decrease in the labor share of income, consistent with technology-driven shifts in factor returns.

## 3. Forecasting the Economic Effects of AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ezra Karger, Otto Kuusela, Jason Abaluck, Kevin A. Bryan, Basil Halperin, Todd R. Jones, Connacher Murphy, Philip Trammell, et al.
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35046
**Abstract:** We survey five distinct groups—academic economists, AI company employees, policy researchers specializing in AI, highly accurate superforecasters, and the general public—regarding expectations for artificial intelligence's economic impact on the United States. Median respondents across groups anticipate significant AI capability improvements by 2030, modest labor force participation declines consistent with demographic trends, and annual GDP growth of 2.5%, surpassing baseline government and private forecasts of 2.0–1.7%. Under a rapid AI advancement scenario in which AI systems exceed human performance on numerous cognitive and physical tasks, experts project GDP growth accelerating to approximately 4% annually and labor force participation declining from its current 62% to 55% by 2050. Roughly half of this decline—approximately 10 million lost jobs—is attributed to AI displacement. Analysis reveals that expert disagreement primarily reflects divergent perspectives on highly capable AI's economic consequences rather than differing views on technological development timelines. Policy preferences vary notably across groups, with experts favoring targeted interventions such as worker retraining while the general public supports broader approaches including job guarantees and universal basic income.

## 4. Does Peer Review Penalize Scientific Risk Taking? Evidence from NIH Grant Renewals [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Wesley H. Greenblatt
**Date:** February 2025 (revised March 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33495
**Abstract:** We analyze the determinants of renewal for more than 100,000 R01 grants from the National Institutes of Health between 1980 and 2015. Using four distinct proxies to measure risk taking—extreme tail outcomes in citation distributions, disruptiveness of published work, pivoting from an investigator's prior research agenda, and standing out from the crowd in one's field—we document that risky grants are renewed at markedly lower rates than less risky ones, after controlling for investigator fixed effects, grant characteristics, and institutional factors. The penalty for risk taking is especially pronounced for grants in novel research areas and for early-career scientists, suggesting that current scientific institutions may inadequately incentivize exploratory investigations. These findings have implications for the design of grant peer review systems and the long-run direction of publicly funded research.

## 5. Female Promotions and the Academic Pipeline: Evidence from a Natural Experiment [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Manuel Bagues, Milan Makany, Giulia Vattuone, Natalia Zinovyeva
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18477/female-promotions-and-the-academic-pipeline-evidence-from-a-natural-experiment
**Abstract:** We examine how faculty promotion decisions shape women's careers and the broader academic pipeline, using data from 4,000 Spanish university departments across all disciplines. We identify exogenous variation in promotion outcomes by exploiting the random assignment of evaluators to promotion committees between 2002 and 2008: applicants whose committees included a co-author or institutional colleague were significantly more likely to qualify for promotion. Two key findings emerge. First, failing to obtain tenure has asymmetrically lasting consequences for women: female applicants who narrowly miss tenure are 57 percentage points less likely to be tenured fifteen years later, compared to 29 percentage points for men. Second, when women do obtain tenure, the effects extend well beyond their own careers—promoting a woman to Associate Professor increases female faculty by approximately 1.5 members after 15 years, generates six additional female PhD graduates over the following decade, and raises the number of women who subsequently achieve tenured academic positions. These results document the compounding, pipeline-level benefits of gender equity in early promotion decisions.
