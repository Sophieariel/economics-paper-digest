## 1. Before the Exodus? Young Scientists and the Future of US Science [Economics of Science]

**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Raffaella Sadun, and Daniela Scur

**Date:** June 2026

**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35330

**Abstract:** The researchers surveyed 916 young biomedical scientists regarding their career trajectories following major US science funding policy changes in early 2025. Key findings reveal significant shifts in sentiment: only barely half now anticipate remaining in academia, representing a 22 percentage-point decline from six months prior. The proportion likely to stay in the United States dropped 21 percentage points, and satisfaction with pursuing a PhD in science fell 16 percentage points. The authors characterize these as concerns from "the next generation – the scientists who would, in ordinary times, become the principal investigators of the future," rather than established researchers protecting existing budgets. NBER Programs: Productivity, Innovation, and Entrepreneurship; Innovation Policy.

## 2. Writing Code vs. Shipping Code: Productivity Effects Across Generations of AI Coding Tools [Economics of AI]

**Authors:** Mert Demirer, Leon Musolff, and Liyuan Yang

**Date:** May 2026 (NBER release: June 2026)

**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35275

**Abstract:** The study examines how productivity gains from successive generations of AI coding tools translate through the software development pipeline. Using GitHub data from over 100,000 developers, researchers employed a matched event study design to measure impacts across three tool types. Results show cumulative effects of 40%, 140%, and 180% on coding activity for autocomplete, interactive agents, and autonomous agents respectively. However, these gains diminish significantly downstream — falling to 50% for project count and just 30% for actual releases. The findings suggest "strong complementarities" between AI and human effort, with an elasticity of substitution of 0.25. Analysis across four app marketplaces revealed modest increases in new applications but no growth in overall usage, indicating that substantial coding-level productivity improvements have only partially converted into shipped and utilized software. NBER Programs: Industrial Organization; Labor Studies; Productivity, Innovation, and Entrepreneurship.

## 3. Estimating the Present Value of R&D Tax Benefits in the United States [Economics of Science]

**Authors:** Brandon Pecoraro, Nicholas C. Hoffman, Martin Lopez-Daneri, Elena C. Derby, Rachel Moore, and Shannon E. Sledz

**Date:** May 2026

**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35208

**Abstract:** This study analyzes corporate tax returns to measure the realized value of R&D tax credits and deductions. The researchers found that "the present value of R&D tax benefits varies substantially with firms' loss status, age, and size." During 2012–2016, companies generated $0.41 in statutory tax benefits per dollar of R&D spending, but the realized value was only $0.36. Young, small firms in loss positions realized just $0.23 per dollar — a significant 44% reduction compared to statutory rates. The findings have direct implications for policy design: the gap between statutory and realized incentives is largest precisely for the early-stage firms that R&D tax policy is often intended to encourage. NBER Programs: Productivity, Innovation, and Entrepreneurship; Public Economics; Microeconomics.

## 4. Attention (And Money) Is All You Need: Why Universities Are Struggling to Keep AI Talent [Economics of Science]

**Authors:** Ufuk Akcigit, Craig A. Chikis, Emin Dinlersoz, and Nathan Goldschlag

**Date:** March 2026

**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34964

**Abstract:** The researchers tracked 42,000 AI scientists over twenty years using linked academic and employment data. They found that "industry increasingly attracts younger and foreign-born researchers" while academia improved gender diversity. A striking finding notes that "the top 1% of publishing industry scientists now earn $1.5 million more annually than comparable academics," representing a fivefold increase since 2001. The study documents that researchers moving to industry "publish less but patent more," suggesting a shift from open science toward proprietary innovation. The wage gap between industry and academia has grown dramatically, raising concerns about the long-run supply of AI talent to universities and the implications for open scientific knowledge production. NBER Programs: Economics of Education; Economic Fluctuations and Growth; Labor Studies; Productivity, Innovation, and Entrepreneurship.

## 5. Artificial Intelligence, Productivity, and the Workforce: Evidence from Corporate Executives [Economics of AI]

**Authors:** Salomé Baslandze, Zachary Edwards, John Graham, Ty McClure, Brent H. Meyer, Michael Sparks, Sonya R. Waddell, and Daniel Weitz

**Date:** March 2026

**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34984

**Abstract:** The researchers surveyed approximately 750 corporate executives to examine how artificial intelligence affects productivity and employment. Key findings include: significant variation in AI adoption rates across firms, with over half already investing; positive labor productivity gains that differ by sector, with the strongest effects in high-skill services and finance; productivity improvements driven primarily by revenue-based total factor productivity rather than capital deepening; a documented gap where "perceived productivity gains are larger than measured productivity gains"; limited evidence of near-term aggregate employment declines, though larger companies anticipate workforce reductions while smaller firms expect modest growth; and observable labor reallocation toward skilled technical roles and away from routine clerical positions. The survey evidence suggests that AI's macroeconomic footprint remains smaller than headline adoption numbers imply, but that effects are accelerating into 2026. NBER Programs: Corporate Finance; Labor Studies; Productivity, Innovation, and Entrepreneurship; International Finance and Macroeconomics.
