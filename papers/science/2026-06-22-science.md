## 1. A Double Bind: Gendered Funding, Research Topics, and Academic Performance in the Social Sciences [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Yang Ding, Ning Zhang, Helen Bao, Yu Jin, Jiang Wu, Lianlian Wu, Norman Weitemeier, Meng Huang, Alejandro Otazu Solorzano, Ana Paula Pineda Iriarte, Yunfeng Gao, Lok Man Michelle Tong, Nancy Mukalayi, Pengfei Yin, Shuyu Hu, Yuxuan Xiao, Yarong Song, Jiajing Xu, Chenxu Li, and Yi Bu
**Date:** June 2, 2026 (revised June 15, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03742
**Abstract:** We analyze 12,945 NSF-funded principal investigators across two decades to examine how gender shapes the intersection of research funding, topic selection, and academic performance in the social sciences. Despite comparable overall funding success rates, women are concentrated in a narrower range of research areas—primarily children, family, cognition, and health—while men dominate technology- and methodology-focused topics. A performance paradox emerges from this distribution: female scholars outperform their male counterparts in male-dominated research areas, while males excel in female-dominated ones. Postdoctoral experience produces asymmetric career effects, benefiting men's performance in male-dominated fields while correlating with lower outcomes for women in those same fields. These patterns constitute a double bind in which gender disparities in topic access and mentorship pathways interact to constrain women's academic advancement even when grant funding is nominally comparable. The findings suggest that equalizing funding rates alone is insufficient to close gender gaps in research output when underlying structural disadvantages in topic access and professional development remain unaddressed.

## 2. Declines in Research Funding and Science Ecosystem Fragility [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Anbang Du, Beining Zhang, Rifat Atun, Michael G. Head, Markus Brede
**Date:** June 8, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09387
**Abstract:** We examine how sharp reductions in U.S. federal research funding propagate through the global science ecosystem, using cancer research as a case study. We model the cancer science ecosystem as a five-layer multiplex network of collaborative linkages spanning grants, clinical trials, publications, patents, and inventions across 233 countries and territories. Applying a compensation analysis framework to simulate federal funding shocks, we find that U.S. funding cuts substantially degrade global scientific collaboration, reducing cross-border information exchange and placing disproportionate compensatory pressure on the European Union, BRICS nations, and other major funding blocs. The disruption is not confined to domestic output: the interconnected structure of global science means that reductions in one major funding source reduce effective research capacity across the system. We show, however, that targeted increases in international collaboration support can partially offset these losses by restructuring the network toward greater resilience. Our results contribute to the economics of science by documenting that science operates as a fragile interdependent ecosystem in which national funding policy has global spillovers, and that funding-shock resilience depends critically on the topology of collaborative linkages rather than aggregate funding levels alone.

## 3. Chaining Tasks, Redefining Work: A Theory of AI Automation [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Mert Demirer, John J. Horton, Nicole Immorlica, Brendan Lucier, Peyman Shahidi
**Date:** June 14, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15960
**Abstract:** We develop a theory of AI automation in which production steps can be handled through manual execution, AI-assisted methods, or fully automated sequences—"chains"—in which AI executes multiple steps end-to-end without human involvement. Firms optimally bundle steps into tasks and then jobs, trading off specialization gains against coordination costs. A key result is that comparative advantage principles can break down when AI chaining is feasible: AI does not necessarily substitute first for tasks in which it holds the largest quality advantage. The model yields non-linear productivity improvements from marginal increases in AI quality and provides a CES representation suitable for embedding in macroeconomic analyses of automation. We validate three central predictions empirically: AI-executed steps cluster together within jobs; job-level AI deployment is lower when AI-affected steps are dispersed rather than adjacent; and proximity to already-automated steps increases the probability that neighboring steps are also automated. These findings imply that the organization of work—not merely the quality of AI—shapes which tasks and jobs are displaced, with implications for how AI adoption restructures occupational structure and labor demand. This paper is accepted at the 2026 ACM Conference on Economics and Computation (EC '26).

## 4. AI Diffusion Gaps: Unequal Integration of AI Across K-12 Schools [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Christopher Campos, John D. Singleton
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35347
**Abstract:** We conduct a national survey of K-12 school principals to measure how schools institutionalize artificial intelligence through formal policies, teacher preparation programs, student guidance, and availability of AI-enabled tools. While AI adoption has accelerated rapidly—primarily for productivity tasks such as lesson planning and administrative efficiency—institutional infrastructure supporting that adoption has lagged substantially. We document two significant and persistent integration gaps. First, schools serving higher proportions of economically disadvantaged students exhibit substantially weaker AI integration scores across all dimensions measured, even after controlling for school resources. Second, and unexpectedly, private and charter schools lag behind traditional public schools in AI integration despite comparable or higher resource levels. Common school-level characteristics explain little of either gap; district size accounts for approximately one-third of the disadvantage differential. These findings suggest that AI adoption in education is not converging toward equity but instead tracking and potentially amplifying existing institutional hierarchies, with implications for the distributional consequences of AI diffusion and for education technology policy.

## 5. How Adaptable Are American Workers to AI-Induced Job Displacement? [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Sam J. Manning, Tomás Aguirre
**Date:** January 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34705
**Abstract:** We construct an occupation-level adaptive capacity index for 356 U.S. occupations, covering 95.9 percent of the workforce, to measure the degree to which workers in AI-exposed jobs possess the skills, education, and transferable human capital required to manage a displacement-driven job transition. Adaptive capacity is distinct from AI exposure: the two are positively correlated, meaning that many occupations highly exposed to AI also contain workers with relatively strong means to navigate a transition. Of the 37.1 million workers in the highest AI-exposure quartile, 26.5 million work in occupations with above-median adaptive capacity. However, approximately 6.1 million workers—roughly 4.2 percent of the covered workforce—face the combination of high AI exposure and low adaptive capacity, constituting a concentrated pocket of elevated vulnerability if displacement occurs. These workers are primarily employed in clerical and administrative roles. Our framework separates the question of who is likely to experience AI-driven task change from the question of who is least able to manage that change, providing a more targeted basis for identifying populations where labor market policy intervention is most warranted.
