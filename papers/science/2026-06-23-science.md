## 1. OPT Reforms and the Relative Supply of STEM Skills [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Catalina Amuedo-Dorantes and Kevin Shih
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pandp.20261100
**Abstract:** Since the late 2000s, reforms to the optional practical training (OPT) program have allowed international STEM graduates to work in the United States for up to three years after graduation. We exploit the staggered OPT expansions of 2008, 2011, 2012, and 2016 as a natural experiment to estimate causal effects on the domestic supply of STEM skills. OPT reforms increased STEM degree completions by 14–15 percent, with gains evident at the bachelor's and master's levels. Critically, domestic student enrollment also rose in exposed fields, indicating that OPT expansions may have crowded in domestic STEM students rather than crowding them out—consistent with scale economies and peer effects in STEM programs. The findings suggest that immigration policy shaping the post-graduation work prospects of international students has meaningful spillover effects on the native STEM labor supply pipeline.

## 2. R&D Uncertainty and Cycles [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Nicolas Crouzet and Janice Eberly
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pandp.20261081
**Abstract:** Investment in equipment and structures is one of the most cyclical components of GDP, collapsing when uncertainty rises during recessions. Yet research and development spending exhibits only mild cyclicality, often holding up or even rising during downturns. We reconcile these patterns using a real options framework: uncertainty deters irreversible capital commitments but simultaneously creates option value from experimentation, generating a positive response of R&D investment to uncertainty. The mechanism is illustrated most starkly during the COVID-19 pandemic, when equipment investment plummeted while R&D was broadly maintained or increased across firms. These findings carry implications for how uncertainty shocks propagate through the economy and for the design of fiscal and monetary policies aimed at sustaining innovative activity through recessions.

## 3. Quality Adjustment in Industry Deflators Strengthens Estimated Innovation-Productivity Relationships [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Enghin Atalay, Ali Hortaçsu, Nicole Kimmel, and Chad Syverson
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pandp.20261042
**Abstract:** This paper studies how industry-level innovation translates into subsequent productivity growth. We leverage gaps between consumer-facing and producer-facing price indices to detect and correct mismeasurement in industry TFP growth. Measured productivity growth is understated in manufacturing, especially in durable goods, with the largest mismeasurement concentrated in Computer and Electronic Product Manufacturing. Correcting for this quality-adjustment problem roughly doubles the estimated slope of the relationship between innovation proxies—R&D intensity and patents per employee—and subsequent TFP growth. The findings imply that conventional industry deflators substantially understate the private and social returns to R&D in rapidly innovating sectors, with important consequences for assessments of the innovation-productivity nexus and for science and technology policy.

## 4. Generative AI and the Redefinition of Entry-Level Software Work [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Samuel Westby, Alicia Sasser Modestino, and Peiran Cheng
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18723
**Abstract:** We examine how the public release of ChatGPT in November 2022 altered employer hiring standards for junior software developers in the United States. Using a large sample of online job vacancy postings, we document a 14–15 percent relative decline in junior versus senior software developer vacancies following the AI release, consistent with generative AI substituting for entry-level coding tasks. Contrary to the hypothesis that remaining junior roles would shift toward AI-specific skills, surviving entry-level postings instead emphasize problem solving, communication, and attention to detail—suggesting firms reorganize work to leverage human comparative advantage in nontechnical competencies. The results demonstrate that generative AI is compressing the traditional apprenticeship model in software by raising the effective skill threshold for entry-level hires and contracting the bottom of occupational career ladders.

## 5. AI and Labor Market Outcomes: Evidence from China [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Tony Fang, Carl Lin, and Qing Liu
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18740
**Abstract:** We study the causal effect of AI-driven labor demand on individual wages using 1.6 million online job postings from 2016–2024 merged with repeated household survey data from China. Exploiting cross-city and cross-industry variation in local AI job demand as a shift-share instrument, we find that a one-unit increase in AI demand raises individual wages by 0.2–0.3 percent on average. Effects are strikingly heterogeneous by gender: women experience wage gains of 0.5–0.7 percent while the effect for men is statistically indistinguishable from zero. Decomposition analysis reveals that women's gains are concentrated in service-oriented, less skill-intensive occupations where AI complements interpersonal and coordination tasks rather than substituting for them. Wage gains are largest in Western provinces and major AI hubs, highlighting the spatial dimension of AI-driven wage inequality. The findings document that AI can reduce gender wage gaps under certain conditions while simultaneously generating geographic divergence.
