## 1. A Missed Opportunity? Labor Demand and Workforce Diversity [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Anna Bindler, Barbara Boelmann, Lena Janys, Luisa H. Santiago Wolf
**Date:** June 22, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23150
**Abstract:** We study how labor demand shocks affect workforce diversity in academia, using German reunification as a natural experiment. The collapse of East German research institutions and the subsequent rebuilding of academic hiring created plausibly exogenous variation in institutional demand for new faculty. While institutional quality dispersion among new hires increased in response to these demand shocks, female representation did not improve despite the presence of qualified women in the pipeline. Using administrative data and a regional difference-in-differences design, we find that labor market expansions in academia do not automatically translate into diversity gains: hiring bottlenecks and institutional screening practices continue to disadvantage women even when demand conditions are favorable. Our results suggest that passive demand-side improvements are insufficient to close gender gaps in the academic workforce and that targeted supply- and process-side interventions may be necessary.

## 2. A Double Bind: Gendered Funding, Research Topics, and Academic Performance in the Social Sciences [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Yang Ding, Ning Zhang, Helen Bao, Yu Jin, Jiang Wu, et al.
**Date:** June 2, 2026 (revised June 15, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03742
**Abstract:** We analyze the intersection of gender, research topic choice, and academic performance using data on 12,945 NSF-funded principal investigators in the social sciences from 2000 to 2019. Female scholars remain systematically underrepresented in high-impact, traditionally male-dominated research areas. Within male-dominated topics, female PIs underperform relative to male PIs on standard bibliometric measures; yet in female-dominated research areas, male PIs outperform female PIs. This double bind implies that women face a performance penalty regardless of their topical specialization. We further find that postdoctoral experience has asymmetric effects across gendered research domains: it improves outcomes more strongly for researchers in male-dominated fields, providing another pathway through which early-career structural conditions compound gender inequality in science funding and productivity.

## 3. Empowering Inclusive Work [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Yanyou Chen, Mitchell Hoffman, Huilan Xu, Zhe Yuan
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35372
**Abstract:** We examine whether AI tools can enhance workplace performance for workers with disabilities. Using administrative data from a large Chinese food-delivery platform, we study deaf and hard-of-hearing (DHH) delivery workers whose customer-facing interactions were augmented by an AI-powered communication assistant. A difference-in-differences design exploiting the staggered rollout of the tool finds that AI adoption substantially increases the speed and productivity of DHH workers, particularly on tasks involving customer interaction. Negative customer ratings fall sharply following adoption, and DHH workers increase labor supply and improve retention rates. AI assistance also narrows approximately one-third of the disability wage gap for this population. These findings suggest that AI can serve as an equalizing technology in labor markets, raising the relative productivity of workers who face barriers in otherwise able-bodied task environments.

## 4. Human Capital, AI, and Labor Commoditization [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Auyon Siddiq, Niuniu Zhang
**Date:** June 20, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21880
**Abstract:** We study whether AI lowers the premium that buyers of labor place on worker human capital—a process we term labor commoditization. Using detailed data from Upwork and text embeddings to construct job-level measures of AI exposure, we estimate how the predictive importance of human capital versus price for winning contracts changed following the release of ChatGPT in late 2022. In more AI-exposed job categories, the importance of human capital for contract success declines while the importance of price rises. Workers with strong credentials and track records—who previously commanded significant demand premiums—experience diminished advantages in AI-exposed fields relative to their peers in less-exposed categories. These results are consistent with AI reducing the effective cost of capability, flattening the human capital gradient in labor markets, and pushing competition toward price rather than quality.

## 5. Mind the Gap: AI Adoption in Europe and the U.S. [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Alexander Bick, Adam Blandin, David J. Deming, Nicola Fuchs-Schündeln, Jonas Jessen
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34995
**Abstract:** We document substantial disparities in AI adoption between the United States and Europe, and across European nations, using harmonized surveys of workers and firms conducted in 2025 and 2026. The U.S. leads European countries by a substantial margin on nearly all adoption measures, with cross-European variation also large. Decomposition analysis indicates that workforce demographics and organizational structure explain a meaningful but incomplete share of adoption gaps. AI adoption correlates strongly with specific management practices—particularly organizational encouragement and training provision—suggesting that firm-level organizational capital is a key complement to AI diffusion. Workers who use AI report substantial time savings, and industries with greater AI integration show stronger productivity growth. These effects are comparably sized in the U.S. and Europe, implying that adoption gaps—not returns to adoption—drive the transatlantic productivity divergence attributable to AI.
