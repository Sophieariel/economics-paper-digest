## 1. Before the Exodus? Young Scientists and the Future of US Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Raffaella Sadun, and Daniela Scur
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35330
**Abstract:** Shortly after major policy changes to US science funding began in early 2025, the researchers surveyed 916 young biomedical scientists—PhD students and postdoctoral researchers—regarding their career intentions and expectations. The findings reveal a significant shift in outlook. Fewer than half of participants indicated they would likely pursue careers in academia, representing a 22 percentage point decline from their views six months prior. The proportion intending to remain in the United States dropped by 21 percentage points. Additionally, satisfaction with pursuing a PhD in science fell by 16 percentage points. The authors characterize these results not as complaints from established researchers protecting their funding, but rather as the expressed career plans of the emerging generation—those who would ordinarily become future principal investigators.

## 2. How Artificial Intelligence Shapes Science: Evidence from AlphaFold [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ryan R. Hill and Carolyn Stein
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35143
**Abstract:** The researchers examined how AlphaFold2's release in July 2021 impacted structural biology. Despite gaining access to hundreds of thousands of AI-predicted protein structures virtually overnight, experimental structure determination rates remained largely stable. Instead of replacing experimental work, researchers leveraged predictions to enhance and support traditional methods. The study found that basic research on previously unstudied proteins increased by 15 to 40% relative to proteins that already had a structure, redirecting scientific focus toward less-explored proteins. However, the findings reveal no evidence so far that more applied, early-stage drug development is targeting these proteins, though future developments remain possible.

## 3. Attention (And Money) Is All You Need: Why Universities Are Struggling to Keep AI Talent [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ufuk Akcigit, Craig A. Chikis, Emin Dinlersoz, and Nathan Goldschlag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34964
**Abstract:** The researchers constructed a novel dataset tracking 42,000 AI researchers over two decades by linking academic publication records to U.S. Census employer-employee data. They found significant shifts in talent allocation: industry increasingly recruits younger and foreign-born researchers, while academia shows greater gender diversity gains. The study reveals that the top 1% of publishing industry scientists now earn $1.5 million more annually than comparable academics, a fivefold increase since 2001. Higher wage premiums correlate with increased concentration at large incumbent firms. Notably, researchers moving to industry roles publish less frequently but generate more patents, suggesting a transition from publicly-available research toward proprietary development.

## 4. Firm Data on AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ivan Yotzov, Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Philip Bunn, Steven J. Davis, Kevin M. Foster, Aaron Jalca, Brent H. Meyer, Paul Mizen, Michael A. Navarrete, Pawel Smietanka, Gregory Thwaites, and Ben Zhe Wang
**Date:** February 2026 (revised March 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836
**Abstract:** The research team surveyed approximately 6,000 business executives across the US, UK, Germany, and Australia to examine AI adoption and workplace effects. Key findings include: 69% of firms actively use AI, with higher adoption among younger and more productive organizations. Executive usage averages only 1.5 hours a week, though more than two-thirds regularly employ the technology. Over the past three years, executives reported little own-firm impact, with 90% noting no employment or productivity changes. However, looking ahead three years, executives anticipate AI will increase firm productivity by 1.4% on average, boost output 0.8%, and reduce employment 0.7%—contrasting sharply with employee expectations of 0.5% employment growth. National AI surveys will be run twice yearly from 2026 onwards to track the evolution of AI adoption across these four advanced economies.

## 5. Still Waters, Rapid Currents: Early Labor Market Transformation under Generative AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard
**Date:** May 2025 (revised March 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777
**Abstract:** This research examines how AI chatbots have affected labor markets by analyzing adoption surveys linked with Danish administrative labor records. The study reveals a paradox: while employers in vulnerable occupations have rapidly implemented chatbot initiatives and workers report productivity gains, these changes have not yet translated into measurable effects on earnings or work hours within two years of ChatGPT's launch. The researchers estimate precise null effects on compensation and hours at both individual and organizational levels, excluding changes exceeding 2%. The key finding centers on task restructuring rather than earnings shifts. Organizations are integrating AI through reorganizing work assignments, introducing new roles in content generation and AI management. Workers adopting these tools transition toward higher-paying positions where AI tools prove more applicable, though the numbers remain insufficient to impact overall earnings. The research demonstrates that structural workplace transformations precede observable financial outcomes.
