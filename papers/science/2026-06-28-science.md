# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-06-28

## 1. Before the Exodus? Young Scientists and the Future of US Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Raffaella Sadun, Daniela Scur
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35330
**Abstract:** Shortly after major policy changes to US science funding began in early 2025, we surveyed 916 young biomedical scientists – PhD students and postdoctoral researchers – about their career intentions and expectations. The results document a dramatic shift in sentiment. Barely half of respondents now say they are likely to remain in academia, down 22 percentage points from how they felt six months earlier. The fraction likely to stay in the United States fell by 21 percentage points. Even satisfaction with having pursued a PhD in science declined by 16 percentage points. These are not the complaints of established scientists defending their budgets, but rather the stated intentions of the next generation – the scientists who would, in ordinary times, become the principal investigators of the future.

## 2. Empowering Inclusive Work [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Yanyou Chen, Mitchell Hoffman, Huilan Xu, Zhe Yuan
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35372
**Abstract:** This paper examines whether AI technology can enhance workplace performance for deaf and hard of hearing (DHH) workers on a major Chinese food-delivery platform. Prior to AI implementation, DHH workers demonstrated slower performance and lower customer ratings compared to non-disabled colleagues, though they contributed more hours and showed higher retention. The platform deployed an AI-powered calling system to assist DHH workers with customer communication. Using a difference-in-differences methodology, the researchers found the technology substantially improved speed, productivity, customer satisfaction, labor supply, and worker retention among DHH employees. AI eliminates one-third of the disability hourly pay gap and significantly increases the profitability of DHH workers for the platform.

## 3. Writing Code vs. Shipping Code: Productivity Effects Across Generations of AI Coding Tools [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Mert Demirer, Leon Musolff, Liyuan Yang
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35275
**Abstract:** This paper examines how productivity effects from AI tools evolve across successive generations and whether task-level gains translate into final output. Using data from over 100,000 GitHub developers and their AI telemetry, the authors employ a matched event study design. The findings show that autocomplete, interactive coding agents, and autonomous coding agents increased coding activity with cumulative effects of 40%, 140%, and 180% respectively. However, these gains diminished significantly across the production hierarchy: the 180% cumulative effect dropped to 50% for projects and 30% for releases. Results align with the weak-link hypothesis, indicating strong complementarities between AI and human effort with an elasticity of substitution of 0.25. Across four major app marketplaces, they found moderate increases in new apps but no growth in total usage, suggesting large task-level AI productivity gains have translated only partially into shipped and used software thus far.

## 4. Growth is Getting Harder to Find, Not Ideas [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Teresa C. Fort, Nathan Goldschlag, Jack Liang, Peter K. Schott, Nikolas Zolas
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35182
**Abstract:** This paper investigates why US productivity growth remains relatively flat despite rising R&D expenditures. Using a 45-year panel of US firms' patenting data, the authors examine research inputs versus innovation outputs and their connection to growth. Key findings include: patents per R&D input are increasing, the elasticity of patents to R&D remains flat or rising, and there is no systematic evidence of a secular decline in patenting when accounting for research investments. While firms maintain a positive relationship between patent growth and productivity gains, average firm growth declines after controlling for patent expansion. The authors conclude that innovative efforts continue sustaining growth without diminishment over four decades, challenging the popular "ideas are getting harder to find" thesis by redirecting the diagnosis: the bottleneck lies in translating innovation into economy-wide growth, not in the rate of idea generation itself.

## 5. What Investment Data Implies about the AI Transition [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Jessica Wachter, Jonathan Wachter
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35290
**Abstract:** This paper examines massive capital expenditures by five major US technology firms — $380 billion on capital expenditure in 2025 — to assess implications for AI productivity growth. Using a two-sector open-economy model with rare productivity booms, the authors calibrate scenarios where AI-sector productivity could increase by a factor of roughly 2.7. Their analysis suggests cumulative GDP growth ranges from 5 to 58 percentage points by 2030, with AI potentially comprising 8% to 39% of the economy. Long-term annual growth expectations reach approximately 7% with substantial volatility. The research also models financial market impacts, indicating the risk-free rate could rise by half a percentage point and the equity premium by roughly 3 percentage points. The paper's empirical grounding in observed investment flows provides a market-based discipline on AI transition scenarios, offering a complement to survey- and text-based estimates of AI's macroeconomic footprint.
