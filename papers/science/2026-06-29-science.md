## 1. Empowering Inclusive Work [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Yanyou Chen, Mitchell Hoffman, Huilan Xu, and Zhe Yuan
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35372
**Abstract:** This research examines whether AI technology can enhance workplace performance for deaf and hard of hearing (DHH) workers on a major Chinese food-delivery platform. Before AI implementation, DHH workers demonstrated lower productivity and customer satisfaction than their non-disabled counterparts, though they contributed more hours and showed higher retention rates. Using a difference-in-differences methodology, the researchers find that an AI-based calling system—introduced to facilitate customer communication for DHH employees—increases the speed and productivity of DHH workers on customer-facing tasks, substantially reduces negative ratings, and boosts labor supply and retention. The intervention closed approximately one-third of the disability wage gap between disabled and non-disabled workers, while simultaneously improving platform profitability. The study provides causal evidence that targeted AI tools can materially reduce disability-related labor market gaps, with implications for inclusive employment policy and AI deployment strategies in low-wage service sectors.

## 2. AI in Science: When and Where It Makes a Difference [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Stefano Bianchini, Valentina Di Girolamo, Julien Ravet, and David Arranz
**Date:** June 2026 (Research Policy, Vol. 55, Issue 6)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733326000697
**Abstract:** This study examines the diffusion and effects of AI across more than 170 scientific fields between 2005 and 2023, using a dataset of over 80 million scientific publications. The researchers assess AI's contribution to knowledge creation using bibliometric indicators of novelty and impact. Key findings reveal that AI use is associated with more novel and more highly cited research, with these effects strengthened by recent technological advances, particularly transformers and large language models. However, effects are markedly uneven across disciplines: the strongest gains occur in fragmented ("rough") knowledge spaces—fields where knowledge integration is more challenging and where AI's combinatorial capacities are most valuable. The contribution of AI also intensifies with its penetration within a field, suggesting complementarity between adoption scale and scientific returns. These results carry direct implications for science policy, research infrastructure investment, and field-specific AI adoption strategies.

## 3. More Versus Better: Artificial Intelligence, Incentives, and the Emerging Crisis in Peer Review [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Claudine Gartenberg, Sharique Hasan, Alex Murray, and Lamar Pierce
**Date:** April 27, 2026 (Organization Science, Vol. 37, No. 3, pp. 795–812)
**Source:** https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2026.ed.v37.n3
**Abstract:** This is the first study to quantitatively document AI's impact on submissions and peer reviews at a major academic journal. Analyzing 6,957 submissions by 11,887 authors, reviewed 10,389 times by 2,519 unique reviewers across five years, the researchers find that submission volume to Organization Science rose 42% following ChatGPT's late 2022 release, while overall writing quality declined. AI-generated content accounts for nearly all the growth in submissions; by early 2026, the majority of manuscripts contain detectable AI-generated writing, with the fastest-growing category being manuscripts where 70% or more of the text is AI-generated. AI-generated peer reviews show lower writing quality, greater difficulty in readability, and less topical diversity—particularly less engagement with data and empirical evidence—compared with human-generated reviews. The authors conclude that the current landscape of AI tools, amplified by publish-or-perish incentives, is pushing academia "toward an equilibrium of more rather than better research." They argue that adapting institutional incentive structures will be necessary if AI is to serve as a genuine engine of scientific innovation rather than volume inflation.

## 4. Artificial Intelligence, Productivity, and the Workforce: Evidence from Corporate Executives [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Salomé Baslandze, Zachary Edwards, John Graham, Ty McClure, Brent H. Meyer, Michael Sparks, Sonya R. Waddell, and Daniel Weitz
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34984
**Abstract:** Drawing from surveys of approximately 750 corporate executives, this study examines AI's impact on organizational performance and employment across firm sizes and sectors. The researchers document substantial heterogeneity in adoption rates and identify positive labor productivity improvements, particularly in financial services and high-skill service industries, with gains projected to increase in 2026. Contrary to a simple capital-deepening narrative, these gains reflect improvements in total factor productivity tied to innovation and demand channels. A notable "productivity paradox" emerges: executives' perceived gains from AI exceed measured outcomes, potentially reflecting delayed revenue effects and attribution difficulties. Regarding employment, the analysis finds little near-term aggregate job loss, though larger firms anticipate workforce reductions while smaller firms expect modest expansion. The research documents a reallocation of labor toward skilled technical positions while routine administrative roles face growing pressure, and develops an exposure index identifying job functions most susceptible to AI displacement.

## 5. Who Uses AI in Research, and for What? Large-Scale Survey Evidence from Germany [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Marina Chugunova, Dietmar Harhoff, Katharina Hölzle, Verena Kaschub, Sonal Malagimani, Ulrike Morgalla, and Robert Rose
**Date:** March 2026 (Research Policy, Vol. 55, Issue 2)
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733325002100
**Abstract:** This research surveys researchers at two of Germany's leading research organizations—representing natural, life, engineering, and social sciences—to systematically document AI adoption patterns, barriers, and perceived impacts on scientific work. The findings reveal widespread and varied use of AI tools, often for primary and creative research tasks rather than merely administrative ones, with many researchers expecting AI to be transformative for research productivity and direction. Effective use is strongly associated with training: both hands-on experience and dedicated learning resources. A persistent gender gap in AI adoption is identified but explained largely by differences in prior familiarity with the technology, pointing to a tractable opportunity for organizational intervention. Legal uncertainty and data privacy concerns emerge as the most prominent institutional barriers, with researchers calling for clear, high-level regulatory guidance. The results underscore the importance of institutional investment in equitable and effective AI adoption across research organizations.
