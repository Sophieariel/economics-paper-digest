# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-06-30

## 1. Before the Exodus? Young Scientists and the Future of US Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Raffaella Sadun, and Daniela Scur
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35330
**Abstract:** The researchers surveyed 916 early-career biomedical scientists following policy changes to US science funding in early 2025. Key findings reveal significant shifts in career sentiment: barely half of respondents now say they are likely to remain in academia, down 22 percentage points from how they felt six months earlier. Additionally, intentions to stay in the United States declined by 21 percentage points, and satisfaction with pursuing a PhD in science dropped 16 percentage points. The authors emphasize these represent concerns from the next generation of potential principal investigators rather than established scientists protecting existing budgets.

## 2. Attention (And Money) Is All You Need: Why Universities Are Struggling to Keep AI Talent [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ufuk Akcigit, Craig A. Chikis, Emin Dinlersoz, and Nathan Goldschlag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34964
**Abstract:** The researchers analyzed 42,000 AI scientists tracked across two decades using linked academic publication and Census employer-employee records. They find that the top 1% of publishing industry scientists now earn $1.5 million more annually than comparable academics, a fivefold increase since 2001. The study documents how industry increasingly recruits younger and international-born researchers, while academia shows greater gender diversity gains. Scientists moving to industry publish less but patent more, consistent with a shift from open science toward proprietary innovation.

## 3. The Economics of Postdoctoral Researcher Positions [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Donna K. Ginther and Joshua L. Rosenbloom
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35014
**Abstract:** The paper examines postdoctoral researcher growth in the U.S. scientific workforce from 1979 to 2023, documenting a fourfold increase that exceeded growth in graduate students and faculty. The authors attribute this expansion to fragmented science funding, particularly the NIH budget doubling in the early 2000s, which stimulated both supply and demand for postdocs but ultimately deteriorated employment conditions. The research finds limited economic returns for postdocs outside academia and declining transitions to faculty positions. The authors suggest the U.S. may have reached "peak postdoc" given recent numerical declines, stricter immigration policies, and rising compensation, potentially reducing future research productivity.

*Note: Published in March 2026, outside the strict 30-day window.*

## 4. Artificial Intelligence, Productivity, and the Workforce: Evidence from Corporate Executives [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Salomé Baslandze, Zachary Edwards, John Graham, Ty McClure, Brent H. Meyer, Michael Sparks, Sonya R. Waddell, and Daniel Weitz
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34984
**Abstract:** The researchers surveyed approximately 750 corporate executives to examine AI's impact on business productivity and employment. Key findings include significant variation in AI adoption across companies, with over 50% already investing; positive productivity gains concentrated in high-skill services and finance; productivity improvements driven by innovation and demand factors rather than capital investment; a "productivity paradox" where executives perceive larger gains than measured data shows; and minimal near-term aggregate employment losses, though larger firms anticipate workforce reductions. The study also develops an index ranking job functions most vulnerable to AI displacement.

*Note: Published in March 2026, outside the strict 30-day window.*

## 5. Forecasting the Economic Effects of AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ezra Karger, Otto Kuusela, Jason Abaluck, Kevin A. Bryan, Basil Halperin, Todd R. Jones, and Philip Tetlock (et al.)
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35046
**Abstract:** The research compares economic forecasts across five groups—academic economists, AI company employees, AI policy researchers, highly accurate forecasters, and the general public—regarding AI's impact on the U.S. economy. Respondents expect substantial advances in AI capabilities by 2030 and project annual GDP growth reaching 2.5%. Under a "rapid" AI progress scenario, forecasters anticipate GDP growth around 4% and labor force participation declining to 55% by 2050, with roughly half attributable to AI. The analysis reveals that disagreement among experts stems primarily from differing views on how capable AI systems will affect the economy rather than differences in assessing AI development timelines. Policy preferences diverge notably, with experts favoring targeted worker support programs while the public backs broader interventions such as universal basic income.

*Note: Published in April 2026, outside the strict 30-day window.*
