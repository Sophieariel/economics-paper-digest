# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-07-01

## 1. Empowering Inclusive Work [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Yanyou Chen, Mitchell Hoffman, Huilan Xu, and Zhe Yuan
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35372
**Abstract:** This study investigates whether artificial intelligence can enhance job outcomes for workers with hearing disabilities. The researchers examined deaf and hard of hearing (DHH) workers on a major Chinese food-delivery platform. Initially, these workers demonstrated lower speed and customer satisfaction compared to non-disabled colleagues, though they contributed more hours and showed greater retention. The platform deployed an AI-powered calling system mid-study to improve customer communication for DHH workers. Using a difference-in-differences methodology, the authors found that the AI tool increases the speed and productivity of DHH workers, especially on tasks involving customer interaction, substantially reduces negative customer ratings, and increases labor supply and retention. The analysis reveals that the technology eliminates one-third of the disability hourly pay gap and significantly increases the profitability of DHH workers for the platform, suggesting AI interventions may effectively narrow performance disparities and improve workplace equity.

## 2. Knowledge Spillovers and Local Outcomes: An Existence Proof from the Establishment of the National Labs [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Susan Helper, Resem Makan, and Daniel W. Shoag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35011
**Abstract:** The researchers examine how the creation of U.S. National Laboratories during the 1940s–1950s generated local economic benefits. They leveraged archival records identifying runner-up locations to establish causality, since lab sites were selected for security and political reasons rather than existing or potential innovative capability. Key findings include that lab counties experienced large and persistent increases in patenting by non-lab inventors, and non-lab patents shifted toward laboratory research fields and more frequently cited laboratory work. Using newly digitized county-level data spanning 1936–1970, the team documented sustained growth in retail sales and household income. Census records revealed wage gains for college-educated workers who remained in lab counties, and school-age cohorts exposed to lab establishment attained higher education levels. The study demonstrates that spillovers arise despite extensive secrecy around early nuclear research, indicating substantial local benefits from proximity to public research infrastructure.

## 3. AI in Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ajay K. Agrawal, John McHale, and Alexander Oettl
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34953
**Abstract:** The paper explores how artificial intelligence impacts knowledge production in science. The authors characterize AI as an augmentation tool that enhances search over combinatorial spaces rather than a full automation mechanism, leading to increased scientific productivity. A multi-stage knowledge production model reveals a jagged frontier with differential AI returns across domains — data-rich fields like biology versus anomaly-sparse physics — and across workflow stages. Human judgment remains essential for abductive inference, contextual nuance, and trade-offs, particularly in data-sparse environments. Using a task-based model distinguishing ordinary from AI-expert scientists, the authors show exogenous AI improvements yield nonlinear productivity gains amplified by the proportion of AI-expert scientists in a field. Complementary investments in AI skills training and organizational design play crucial roles in realizing these gains.

## 4. Firm Data on AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ivan Yotzov, Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Philip Bunn, Steven J. Davis, Kevin M. Foster, Aaron Jalca, Brent H. Meyer, Paul Mizen, Michael A. Navarrete, Pawel Smietanka, Gregory Thwaites, and Ben Zhe Wang
**Date:** February 2026 (Revised March 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836
**Abstract:** The researchers surveyed approximately 6,000 senior business executives across US, UK, German, and Australian firms to examine AI adoption patterns and workplace consequences. Key findings include that 69% of firms actively use AI, particularly younger and higher-productivity organizations. While over two-thirds of executives personally use AI, their average usage is only 1.5 hours per week. Regarding recent impact, executives report minimal changes, with nine-in-ten reporting no impact on employment or productivity in the past three years. However, looking forward, respondents anticipate substantial shifts — predicting productivity gains of 1.4%, output increases of 0.8%, and employment reductions of 0.7%. Notably, employees hold more optimistic expectations than employers, forecasting employment growth of 0.5%, revealing a significant divergence between employer and worker perspectives on AI's labor-market trajectory.

## 5. Does Generative AI Narrow Education-Based Productivity Gaps? Evidence from a Randomized Experiment [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Guillermo Cruces, Diego Fernández Meijide, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro H. Gálvez, and María Lombardi
**Date:** February 2026 (Revised May 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34851
**Abstract:** The study investigates whether generative AI reduces or reinforces productivity gaps between workers with different educational backgrounds. Through a randomized online experiment with 1,174 participants aged 25–45, researchers had participants complete a business problem-solving task either with or without an AI assistant, followed by a non-AI follow-up module. AI increases performance for all participants, with substantially larger gains for lower-education individuals. With AI, the education-based performance gap falls to 0.139 standard deviations, closing about three-quarters of the initial gap. The research demonstrates that productivity gains persist beyond AI availability and that intensive AI use produces strong task performance regardless of how much time and effort participants devote themselves, though underlying educational differences continue influencing unassisted performance. These findings suggest generative AI may function as an equalizing technology across skill groups.
