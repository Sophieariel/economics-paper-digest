# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-07-03

## 1. Before the Exodus? Young Scientists and the Future of US Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Raffaella Sadun, Daniela Scur
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35330
**Abstract:** The researchers surveyed 916 young biomedical scientists—PhD students and postdoctoral researchers—following major policy changes to US science funding in early 2025. Their findings reveal a significant decline in career sentiment among the next generation of potential research leaders. Barely half of respondents now say they are likely to remain in academia, down 22 percentage points from how they felt six months earlier. The fraction likely to stay in the United States fell by 21 percentage points, with departures concentrated among foreign-born scientists. Even satisfaction with having pursued a PhD in science declined by 16 percentage points. The authors emphasize these represent concerns from the emerging generation, not established scientists protecting existing budgets, and validate survey responses against contemporaneous behavioral signals. The results raise concerns about a potential "brain drain" from US academic science at a critical juncture in global scientific competition.

## 2. Immigration, Innovation, and the Geography of Growth [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Costas Arkolakis, Sun Kyoung Lee, Michael Peters
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35392
**Abstract:** The paper examines how mass immigration between 1880 and 1920 affected US innovation and economic growth. Using newly linked data connecting census records, immigration records, and patent information, the researchers find that immigrants disproportionately settled in urban innovation hubs. Their spatial growth model indicates that post-1880 arrivals increased US income per capita by 8.2% by 1940. The authors conclude that immigrants' skill composition and their concentration in urban hubs are key drivers of this productivity-enhancing effect, with immigrants substantially raising local patent rates and diffusing knowledge spillovers across the innovation network. The counterfactual analysis suggests that eliminating the 1920s immigration restrictions could have further boosted income by 1.7% by 2000, with implications for modern debates over high-skilled immigration and science policy.

## 3. Innovation without Borders? The Geography of Technological Diffusion [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ursel Baumann, Zoë B. Cullen, Ester Faia, Annalisa Ferrando, Ricardo Perez-Truglia, Judit Rariga
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35314
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a large-scale field experiment with 3,300 firms across twelve EU countries to examine how AI adoption diffuses across borders. They measured firms' perceptions of AI investment rates both domestically and among peers in Germany, France, and Italy. Half the sample received accurate information about domestic and foreign AI adoption rates. Key findings indicate firms substantially underestimate competitor AI investments at home and abroad. The information treatment significantly increased expected AI investment rates within countries. Strong strategic complementarities emerged domestically—a one percentage point increase in expected domestic AI adoption raised individual firms' adoption expectations by 0.57 percentage points. However, the effect of expected foreign peer AI adoption produced no statistically significant response, demonstrating that AI diffusion operates primarily along domestic rather than international lines, with important implications for coordinated industrial policy.

## 4. AI Diffusion Gaps: Unequal Integration of AI Across K-12 Schools [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Christopher Campos, John D. Singleton
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35347
**Abstract:** The paper examines how generative AI has spread through K-12 schools via a national survey of principals. AI use has spread rapidly across schools, largely as a productivity aid—students primarily use it for homework and writing while educators leverage it for lesson planning and administrative tasks. The research identifies two significant disparities: schools serving higher proportions of disadvantaged students show substantially lower AI integration scores, and private and charter schools lag considerably behind traditional public schools in integration measures. While specific school-level factors predict AI adoption, they fail to explain these equity gaps, creating what the authors term "AI diffusion gaps." District size accounts for approximately one-third of the disadvantage disparity among public schools, suggesting that the factors associated with greater AI integration differ systematically from those needed to narrow disparities. The findings have implications for understanding how AI may reinforce or offset existing educational inequalities.

## 5. Task-Specific Technical Change and Comparative Advantage [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Lukas Althoff, Hugo Reichardt
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35353
**Abstract:** The paper examines how artificial intelligence reshapes workers' comparative advantage through task-level productivity changes. The authors develop a dynamic task-based model incorporating multidimensional worker skills, occupational choices, and on-the-job skill accumulation, allowing occupations to combine tasks where productivity depends on skill-task alignment. Using panel data and a new task-skill requirements database, they structurally estimate the model under various AI scenarios involving task augmentation, automation, and simplification. The key finding is that AI narrows wage inequality and raises average wages primarily through simplification mechanisms that enable lower-skill workers to access previously inaccessible high-productivity positions. Adoption costs moderate but do not eliminate these inequality reductions. The results challenge conventional automation-displaces-workers narratives by identifying a distributional channel through which AI can simultaneously raise aggregate wages and compress the wage distribution, depending on which tasks AI augments versus automates.
