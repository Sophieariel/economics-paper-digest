# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-07-04

## 1. Measuring the Production of Scientific Human Capital: New Data, Methods, and Evidence on the U.S. Scientific Training Ecosystem [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Dror Shvadron, Hansen Zhang, Lee Fleming, and Daniel P. Gross
**Date:** June 2025 (Revised June 2026)
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w33944
**Abstract:** The researchers analyzed comprehensive data covering nearly all U.S. STEM PhDs awarded since 1950 to investigate PhD training funding sources and workforce distribution. Their key findings indicate that the U.S. federal government is by far the largest source of financial and in-kind support for STEM PhD training. The study identifies institutions and disciplines with substantial government, industry, or philanthropic funding, particularly those training specialists in strategic technology domains including artificial intelligence, quantum computing, and biotechnology. Using temporal and agency-based variation in government funding, the authors demonstrate that increasing government-funded PhD trainees increases PhD production roughly one-for-one. The research includes publicly available datasets organized by technology area and funding source to facilitate future investigations.

*Note: Originally posted June 2025; revised June 2026 (within the 30-day window). NBER Programs: PR, SSF.*

## 2. Declines in Research Funding and Science Ecosystem Fragility [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Anbang Du, Beining Zhang, Rifat Atun, Michael G Head, and Markus Brede
**Date:** June 8, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09387
**Abstract:** The research examines how rapid declines in US Federal research funding affect global scientific collaboration. The team modeled the cancer science ecosystem as a multiplex network spanning 233 countries and territories, tracking collaborative linkages through grants, clinical trials, co-authorships, patents, and co-inventions. Their key findings indicate that sharp funding reductions degrade global information exchange in science, imposing outsized compensatory burdens on country groups such as the European Union (EU) and BRICS. However, the authors identify a potential mitigation strategy: if other nations increase support for international collaborations, there is an opportunity to remodel the cancer science system to be more resilient to future shocks. The study proposes a compensation analysis framework to quantify information flow impacts through network efficiency metrics, offering insights into how funding disruptions ripple across the global research enterprise.

*Note: Submitted June 8, 2026. Interdisciplinary network-science / science policy paper; not a traditional economics venue submission, but addresses science funding—a core Economics of Science topic.*

## 3. Human Capital, AI, and Labor Commoditization [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Auyon Siddiq and Niuniu Zhang
**Date:** June 20, 2026 (v1); June 30, 2026 (v2)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21880
**Abstract:** The researchers examined whether generative AI has altered how labor markets assess worker capabilities. Using contract-level data from Upwork, they represented worker profiles through high-dimensional text embeddings to capture detailed human capital information. They applied a difference-in-differences methodology around ChatGPT's launch to analyze changes in client demand patterns. Their findings indicate that in job categories more exposed to AI, there is a significant shift: worker expertise becomes less valuable while pricing becomes more influential. This suggests that AI has made labor markets treat workers more as interchangeable commodities. Two supporting observations were that experienced workers lost their typical demand advantage in AI-exposed fields, and hiring demand redirected toward lower-cost workers. The research carries implications for online labor platform design, worker investment decisions, and overall labor market welfare.

## 4. Innovation without Borders? The Geography of Technological Diffusion [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ursel Baumann, Zoë B. Cullen, Ester Faia, Annalisa Ferrando, Ricardo Perez-Truglia, and Judit Rariga
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35314
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a large-scale field experiment spanning 3,300 firms across twelve EU countries to examine how AI investment intentions spread across geographic boundaries. They collected firm perceptions regarding AI investment rates among domestic and foreign competitors. When researchers provided half the sample with accurate data on peer adoption, firms significantly updated their own anticipated AI investment. The study found strong strategic complementarities within national borders—a 1 percentage point increase in expected domestic AI adoption raised individual firm investment expectations by 0.57 percentage points—but the effect of expected foreign peer AI investment on a firm's own anticipated investment rate was statistically insignificant. This suggests technology diffusion operates primarily through domestic channels rather than across international borders, with implications for industrial policy and the cross-country convergence of AI adoption.

## 5. What Investment Data Implies about the AI Transition [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Jessica Wachter and Jonathan Wachter
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35290
**Abstract:** The research examines massive capital expenditures by major U.S. technology firms—$380 billion in 2025 with forecasts of roughly double that amount in 2026—using a two-sector open-economy model. The authors calibrate productivity boom scenarios to match observed investment patterns through 2027, finding that the investment data implies AI-sector productivity improves by a factor of roughly 2.7. Their analysis generates multiple scenarios consistent with industry forecasts, producing projected cumulative GDP growth ranging from 5 to 58 percentage points by 2030, with AI representing 8% to 39% of economic activity. Long-term annual growth expectations approach approximately 7%. The modeling also implies financial market effects including a risk-free rate increase of roughly half a percentage point and an equity premium rise of approximately 3 percentage points.

*Note: This paper uses observed firm investment data to calibrate a structural model; it is primarily a quantitative macroeconomic analysis rather than a reduced-form causal study.*
