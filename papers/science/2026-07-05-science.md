## 1. Knowledge Spillovers and Local Outcomes: An Existence Proof from the Establishment of the National Labs [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Susan Helper, Resem Makan, and Daniel W. Shoag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35011
**Abstract:** The paper studies how U.S. National Laboratories established in the 1940s–1950s generated local economic spillovers. Because lab sites were chosen for security and political reasons rather than existing innovative capacity, the design permits credible causal identification. Lab counties experienced large and persistent increases in patenting by non-laboratory inventors; private-sector patents increasingly cited laboratory research and shifted toward lab research fields; county retail sales and household income rose durably; Census records show wage gains for college-educated workers; and school-age children exposed to the lab openings attained higher education levels. Spillovers arose despite extensive secrecy around early nuclear research, providing unusually clean evidence that co-location with public research infrastructure generates substantial local knowledge externalities.

## 2. Who Uses AI in Research, and for What? Large-Scale Survey Evidence from Germany [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Marina Chugunova, Dietmar Harhoff, Katharina Hölzle, Verena Kaschub, Sonal Malagimani, Ulrike Morgalla, and Robert Rose
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733325002100
**Abstract:** The paper reports results from a survey of more than 6,000 researchers at the Max Planck Society and the Fraunhofer Society, covering AI tool adoption, barriers, and perceived impact across research stages. Researchers actively use AI for core and creative tasks, with adoption concentrated among younger and male researchers. Decomposition analysis shows that the gender gap in AI use reflects differences in familiarity rather than attitudes, pointing to training rather than motivation as the binding constraint. Legal uncertainty—particularly around data protection and copyright—is the most commonly cited barrier to adoption. The findings have direct implications for research organizations seeking to raise AI productivity and for science policy aiming to close within-science inequality gaps.

## 3. Innovation without Borders? The Geography of Technological Diffusion [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ursel Baumann, Zoë B. Cullen, Ester Faia, Annalisa Ferrando, Ricardo Perez-Truglia, and Judit Rariga
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35314
**Abstract:** The paper presents a large-scale field experiment with 3,300 firms across twelve EU countries to study how information about peers' AI investment affects own investment plans. Firms substantially underestimate competitors' current AI adoption, missing domestic peers by 14 percentage points and foreign peers by 7 percentage points on average. A randomly assigned information treatment that reveals accurate domestic peer adoption rates causes a statistically significant increase in firms' own expected AI investment (p < 0.001), with an elasticity of approximately 0.57. The same information about foreign competitors' investment has no significant effect, revealing sharp strategic complementarities within but not across national borders. The results identify information frictions and domestic peer effects as first-order determinants of AI diffusion, with implications for AI adoption policies and the competitive gap between national economies.

## 4. Human Capital, AI, and Labor Commoditization [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Auyon Siddiq and Niuniu Zhang
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21880
**Abstract:** The paper investigates whether generative AI has changed how online labor markets value worker capabilities. Using longitudinal data from Upwork and text embeddings to construct measures of human capital from worker profiles, the authors implement a difference-in-differences design around the November 2022 launch of ChatGPT. In occupational categories most exposed to AI substitution, the estimated wage premium for human capital declines and the importance of hourly price rises, consistent with commoditization of labor services. Workers with strong human capital see reduced demand premiums in AI-exposed categories, with hiring reallocating toward lower-priced workers. The pattern is absent in low-exposure occupations. The findings suggest generative AI reduces the returns to skill investment in exposed markets, with implications for platform design, worker training incentives, and inequality within AI-exposed occupations.

## 5. Generative AI and the Reorganization of Labor Demand [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Fangyan Wang, Zaiyan Wei, and Yang Wang
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23159
**Abstract:** The paper examines how the spread of generative AI reshapes firm hiring behavior and job structures using a large sample of U.S. job postings and machine learning–based AI exposure scores. Three findings emerge. First, occupational AI exposure is not static: the frontier of exposed tasks expands over time as model capabilities grow. Second, firms adjust labor demand through two margins—hiring reallocation across job titles (accounting for 52% of total adjustment) and within-job redesign of task bundles (39.5%)—indicating that organizational reconfiguration is as important as pure substitution. Third, career-level heterogeneity is substantial: senior positions adjust earlier and primarily through hiring changes, while entry-level roles face delayed but more varied adjustment involving both reallocation and redesign. The results imply that standard task-based exposure indices understate the breadth of generative AI's labor market effects by missing the endogenous redesign of job content.
