# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-07-06

## 1. Before the Exodus? Young Scientists and the Future of US Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Raffaella Sadun, and Daniela Scur
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35330
**Abstract:** The researchers surveyed 916 PhD students and postdoctoral researchers shortly after major US science funding policy changes began in early 2025. Key findings reveal significant shifts in career intentions among early-career biomedical scientists: barely half of respondents now say they are likely to remain in academia, down 22 percentage points from how they felt six months earlier. Intentions to remain in the United States fell by 21 percentage points, and satisfaction with pursuing a PhD in science declined by 16 percentage points. The authors emphasize these represent the stated intentions of the next generation of scientists—those who would, in ordinary times, become the principal investigators of the future—rather than protests from established researchers protecting existing resources.

## 2. AI in Science: When and Where It Makes a Difference [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Stefano Bianchini, Valentina Di Girolamo, Julien Ravet, and David Arranz
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733326000697
**Abstract:** This paper examines the diffusion of artificial intelligence across scientific research and its contribution to knowledge creation. Drawing on a dataset of over 80 million scientific publications across more than 170 scientific fields between 2005 and 2023, the authors measure AI's effect on research novelty and citation impact. They find that AI use is associated with more novel and highly cited research on average, with these effects amplified by recent technological advances—particularly the emergence of transformer architectures and large language models. Effects are heterogeneous across fields: the largest gains occur in fields with fragmented ("rough") knowledge spaces, where AI can most effectively support search and synthesis over combinatorial spaces. The study contributes to a sparse empirical literature directly measuring AI's effect on the quality and impact of scientific output.

*Published in Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 6, 2026.*

## 3. AI Users Are Not All Alike: The Characteristics of French Firms Buying and Developing AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Flavio Calvino and Luca Fontanelli
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733326000648
**Abstract:** This paper characterizes French firms using artificial intelligence in 2018 and examines the link between AI use and productivity, distinguishing between "AI buyers" (firms sourcing AI from external providers) and "AI developers" (firms building their own AI systems). AI buyers tend to be larger, but this size premium disappears once ICT infrastructure is controlled for. AI developers are larger and younger even after conditioning on ICT variables, and require more specialized digital skills. On productivity, the authors find that the raw productivity premium associated with AI use is largely attributable to positive selection—more productive and digitally intensive firms adopt AI—rather than to AI itself. However, AI developers exhibit persistent and robust productivity improvements that survive multiple approaches to addressing endogeneity concerns, consistent with a causal effect of in-house AI development on firm performance.

*Published in Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 5, 2026.*

## 4. Digital Skill Adoption and Employment Dynamics: Evidence from Matched Vacancy-Employer-Employee Data [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Mathias Fjællegaard Jensen and Cédric Schneider
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733326000673
**Abstract:** This paper studies the interplay between firms' investments in digital skills and employment dynamics in the Danish labor market, using novel digital skill demand measures derived from online job postings via natural language processing. The authors link vacancy data to matched employer-employee records to trace within-firm employment outcomes alongside changes in the composition of skill demand. They find a strong positive correlation between demand for digital skills—particularly in data science and artificial intelligence—and firm labor productivity. The relationship between digital skill investment and firm-level employment is positive and broad-based across demographic groups, with the employment effect stronger and more precisely estimated for AI and data science skills specifically. The results suggest that, in the Danish context, AI-complementary workforce investment has so far been associated with employment growth rather than displacement.

*Published in Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 5, 2026.*

## 5. Specialisation and the Career Outcomes of Inventors [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Jordan Bisset
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048733326000685
**Abstract:** Recent decades have seen innovation production increasingly organized around teams and around individual inventors who are more technologically specialized. This paper constructs a panel of highly prolific inventors observed throughout their careers and models the trade-off between knowledge depth and knowledge breadth. Empirically, after conditioning on selection into specialized careers, specialization is associated with a lower probability of contributing to top-cited inventions. This negative relationship is consistent with a knowledge obsolescence mechanism: highly specialized inventors face greater risk that their narrow expertise becomes outdated in the late stages of their career as technological fields evolve. The findings carry macroeconomic implications because a disproportionate share of social value is produced by the rare, high-impact inventions that specialized inventors are less likely to create.

*Published in Research Policy, Volume 55, Issue 5, 2026.*
