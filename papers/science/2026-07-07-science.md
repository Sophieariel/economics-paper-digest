## 1. Before the Exodus? Young Scientists and the Future of US Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Raffaella Sadun, Daniela Scur
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35330
**Abstract:** We surveyed 916 early-career biomedical scientists following major policy shifts affecting US science funding in early 2025. We document dramatic changes in career sentiment: fewer than half of respondents now expect to remain in academia, a decline of 22 percentage points from six months prior; the share likely to remain in the United States fell by 21 percentage points; and satisfaction with pursuing a science PhD dropped by 16 percentage points. These are the next generation of scientists—the researchers who would, in ordinary times, become the principal investigators of the future. The speed and breadth of the sentiment shift raises concerns about the long-run trajectory of US scientific capacity and the stability of the pipeline of early-career researchers who anchor domestic R&D.

## 2. Attention (And Money) Is All You Need: Why Universities Are Struggling to Keep AI Talent [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ufuk Akcigit, Craig A. Chikis, Emin Dinlersoz, Nathan Goldschlag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34964
**Abstract:** We construct a novel dataset linking academic publications to U.S. Census Bureau employer-employee records and track approximately 42,000 AI researchers over two decades. We document systematic changes in the allocation of AI talent across sectors: industry increasingly recruits younger and foreign-born researchers, while academic institutions show slower improvement in gender diversity. The wage gap between sectors has widened dramatically—top 1% publishing scientists in industry now earn $1.5 million more annually than comparable academics, a fivefold increase since 2001. Researchers transitioning to industry publish less frequently but generate substantially more patents, consistent with a shift from open science toward proprietary innovation. Our findings raise questions about the long-run supply of publicly accessible AI knowledge and the capacity of universities to train the next generation of AI researchers.

## 3. How Artificial Intelligence Shapes Science: Evidence from AlphaFold [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ryan R. Hill, Carolyn Stein
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35143
**Abstract:** We study how a frontier AI model affects scientific discovery by examining the release of the AlphaFold2 algorithm and its impact on structural biology and related fields. Despite the provision of hundreds of thousands of AI-predicted protein structures, experimental structure determination rates remained essentially unchanged—researchers instead used the predictions to complement and enhance traditional experimental work. We find that basic research on previously understudied proteins increased by 15–40%, redirecting scientific attention toward proteins that had received little prior investigation. However, applied drug development efforts targeting these newly studied proteins showed no significant uptake at the time of our analysis. Our results suggest that AI tools can expand the frontier of basic science inquiry without displacing experimental methods, while translation to applied research may lag substantially.

## 4. Artificial Intelligence, Productivity, and the Workforce: Evidence from Corporate Executives [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Salomé Baslandze, Zachary Edwards, John Graham, Ty McClure, Brent H. Meyer, Michael Sparks, Sonya R. Waddell, Daniel Weitz
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34984
**Abstract:** We draw on survey data from approximately 750 corporate executives to examine AI's effects on productivity and workforce composition. We document substantial heterogeneity in AI adoption rates: more than half of firms have already invested, though many smaller firms are only beginning to do so. Labor productivity gains are positive, vary across sectors, and are expected to strengthen over the next year, with the largest effects concentrated in high-skill services and finance. These gains are not primarily driven by capital deepening but instead reflect increases in revenue-based total factor productivity, closely linked to innovation and demand-oriented channels. In the near term, AI has not led—and is not expected to lead—to meaningful reductions in aggregate employment; firm-size-weighted aggregate employment is projected to decline by less than 0.4% due to AI. However, we document shifting labor demand, with declining administrative roles offset by increased demand for technical positions. We also identify a notable disconnect between executives' perceived productivity improvements and objectively measured gains.

## 5. The Microstructure of AI Diffusion: Evidence from Firms, Business Functions, and Worker Tasks [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Kathryn Bonney, Cory L. Breaux, Emin Dinlersoz, Lucia S. Foster, John C. Haltiwanger, Aditya A. Pande
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35141
**Abstract:** We characterize AI diffusion across three interconnected layers—overall firm use, deployment across business functions, and worker-task adoption—using U.S. Census Bureau data from the Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS) AI supplement covering November 2025 through January 2026. We find that 18% of firms (32% employment-weighted) had deployed AI in at least one business function, with adoption expected to reach 22% within six months. Adoption concentrates among large firms and knowledge-intensive sectors, reaching 50–60% (60–70% employment-weighted) for very large firms in information, professional services, and finance. Among adopting firms, scope remains limited: 57% use AI in three or fewer functions, most often sales and marketing, strategy, and IT. At the worker level, 23% of firms (41% employment-weighted) report task-level AI use, primarily for writing, document analysis, and information search. We document both top-down organizational and bottom-up grassroots diffusion patterns, with most companies emphasizing task augmentation rather than workforce reduction.
