# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-07-08

## 1. Before the Exodus? Young Scientists and the Future of US Science [Economics of Science]

**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Raffaella Sadun, Daniela Scur
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35330

**Abstract:** Survey of 916 young biomedical PhD students and postdocs following major policy shifts to US science funding in early 2025. Finds that barely half now say they are likely to remain in academia (down 22 percentage points from six months prior), the fraction likely to stay in the United States fell 21 percentage points, and satisfaction with having pursued a PhD in science declined 16 percentage points. The US-departure shift is concentrated among foreign-born scientists. The authors emphasize these reflect "the stated intentions of the next generation" who would normally become future research leaders.

## 2. Immigration, Innovation, and the Geography of Growth [Economics of Science]

**Authors:** Costas Arkolakis, Sun Kyoung Lee, Michael Peters
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35392

**Abstract:** Uses newly created data linking census records, immigration records, and patent information to study how 1880–1920 mass immigration (20+ million immigrants) to the US influenced innovation and economic expansion. Newcomers disproportionately concentrated in major urban innovation centers. A spatial growth model accounting for skilled workers' comparative advantage in innovation shows post-1880 international arrivals boosted US income per capita by 8.2% by 1940. Removing the restrictive immigration policies enacted in the 1920s would have generated an additional 1.7% income per capita increase through 2000.

## 3. AI Diffusion Gaps: Unequal Integration of AI Across K-12 Schools [Economics of AI]

**Authors:** Christopher Campos, John D. Singleton
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35347

**Abstract:** Using an original national survey of school principals, measures institutional AI integration through policies, teacher training, student guidance, leadership involvement, and tool availability. Finds AI has spread rapidly largely as a productivity aid; students use it for homework assistance and writing, educators for lesson planning and administration. However, schools serving higher proportions of disadvantaged students show 0.07–0.11 SD lower AI integration scores (with one SD increase in disadvantage), while private and charter schools demonstrate 0.23–0.44 SD weaker AI integration than traditional public schools. District size explains roughly one-third of the disadvantage gap.

## 4. Baby Busts and Growth Booms: Demographic Change and the Macroeconomy [Economics of Science]

**Authors:** Daron Acemoglu, David Autor, Keelan Beirne, Andrew Scott
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35401

**Abstract:** Examines how declining birth rates globally over seven decades affected economic growth. Finds that lower birth rates are associated with higher growth in GDP per working-age adult and wage increases. Countries and regions experiencing lower birth rates demonstrate increased labor-saving patents and technological advancement. Using WWII mortality data to isolate causal effects, finds declining younger populations (not population size itself) drive these outcomes through technology adapting to labor scarcity.

## 5. Generative AI and the Reorganization of Labor Demand [Economics of AI]

**Authors:** Fangyan Wang, Zaiyan Wei, Yang Wang
**Date:** May 22, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23159

> **Note:** This paper is 47 days before today's date (slightly outside the strict 30-day window) and sourced from arXiv rather than the priority sources (NBER/SSRN/Research Policy/JEP). It is included as the best available empirical Economics of AI paper meeting the other criteria; empirical Economics of AI papers from priority sources within the strict June 8 – July 8 window were not found in this search.

**Abstract:** Investigates how firms restructure hiring patterns as generative AI spreads. Using job posting data across all US economic sectors, measures AI exposure through an LLM-based pipeline identifying tasks and assessing AI capability. Finds that AI exposure fluctuates over time; firms respond through hiring shifts (52%) and task redesign (39.5%). Senior positions adjust earlier primarily through reallocation while junior roles show more varied adjustment patterns. Labor market adaptation involves organizational restructuring affecting both hiring decisions and work composition.
