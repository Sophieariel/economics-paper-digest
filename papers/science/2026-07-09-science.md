# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-07-09

## 1. Before the Exodus? Young Scientists and the Future of US Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Raffaella Sadun, and Daniela Scur
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35330
**Abstract:** Following policy changes to US science funding in early 2025, the researchers surveyed 916 young biomedical scientists—PhD students and postdoctoral researchers—to examine shifts in career intentions. Only slightly more than half of respondents indicated they would likely remain in academia, a 22-percentage-point decrease from six months prior. The proportion planning to stay in the United States dropped by 21 percentage points, and satisfaction with pursuing a PhD in science fell by 16 percentage points. The authors characterize these responses as reflecting the stated intentions of the next generation—the scientists who would, in ordinary times, become the principal investigators of the future—rather than established researchers protecting existing budgets.

## 2. Merit or Networks? What Decides Where Research Is Published [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ning Li
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03763
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether scientific publishing rewards idea quality or professional connections. The author measures paper quality directly from text using a language model evaluator before publication outcomes are known, thereby avoiding circularity. Analyzing 6,208 economics working papers, the study finds that execution quality sets a meritocratic baseline, idea quality determines intermediate prestige levels, and connections create advantages primarily at top journals. Connections operate through two pathways: connected authors produce higher-scoring work, and equal-quality papers still benefit from network advantages. However, this effect has limits—connections cannot elevate typical ideas to elite outlets. The findings provide causal evidence that both merit and social capital govern journal placement in economics, with their relative importance varying systematically by journal tier.

## 3. The Shift to Agentic AI: Evidence from Codex [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Drew Johnston, David Holtz, Alex Martin Richmond, Christopher Ong, Prasanna Tambe, and Aaron Chatterji
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26959
**Abstract:** The researchers examine OpenAI's Codex usage data to study how agentic AI technology—systems that can take autonomous actions on a user's behalf—changes how people work. Tracking three distinct user populations, they document rapid adoption with active users increasing more than fivefold in the first half of 2026. Within OpenAI, Codex has largely supplanted ChatGPT for business-related tasks. The study also documents rising task complexity over time and substantial output increases: legal staff generated thirteen times more tokens by June 2026 compared to November 2025. These patterns suggest agentic AI is not simply automating discrete tasks but reorganizing the scope and scale of knowledge work itself.

## 4. Answering Without Referring: How AI Search Rewrites the Web's Economic Bargain [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Qiaoni Shi, Kai Zhu, and Kai Gu
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07652
**Abstract:** The researchers analyze clickstream data comparing ChatGPT and Google to document fundamental differences in how AI-powered and traditional search engines engage with content. ChatGPT produces outbound clicks in only 5.2% of conversation sessions, far below Google's referral ratio. The study interprets this as AI search resolving information needs internally rather than directing users to source websites, potentially disrupting the economic model connecting search, web traffic, and content creation. The paper quantifies how this divergence threatens the advertising and referral revenue streams that fund open-web content, and discusses implications for content producers, platform markets, and the long-run viability of ad-supported information provision.

## 5. The Privilege of Exposure: Caste and Generative AI in India's Graduate Labour Market [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Kaibalyapati Mishra
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13314
**Abstract:** This paper analyzes data from India's Periodic Labour Force Survey (2025) covering approximately 83,000 employed graduates to examine how generative AI exposure varies across caste groups. Using three occupational AI-exposure indices, the author finds that Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe graduates experience significantly lower exposure to generative AI than upper-caste peers. This disparity translates into meaningful wage differentials as AI-related income advantages concentrate among workers in high-exposure roles. The study identifies two concrete mechanisms: occupational concentration, whereby lower-caste graduates sort into less AI-exposed jobs, and sectoral underrepresentation in industries where AI adoption is highest. The findings suggest that without deliberate policy intervention, the diffusion of generative AI risks amplifying preexisting labor market inequalities along caste lines.
