# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-07-10

## 1. A Double Bind: Gendered Funding, Research Topics, and Academic Performance in the Social Sciences [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Yang Ding, Ning Zhang, Helen Bao, Yu Jin, Jiang Wu, Lianlian Wu, Norman Weitemeier, Meng Huang, Alejandro Otazu Solorzano, Ana Paula Pineda Iriarte, Yunfeng Gao, Lok Man Michelle Tong, Nancy Mukalayi, Pengfei Yin, Shuyu Hu, Yuxuan Xiao, Yarong Song, Jiajing Xu, Chenxu Li, and Yi Bu
**Date:** June 15, 2026 (revised; originally submitted June 2, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03742
**Abstract:** The study examines 12,945 NSF-funded principal investigators in the social sciences from 2000 to 2019. While female researchers achieve overall funding success rates comparable to men, the paper documents a "double bind": female scholars remain underrepresented in high-impact and traditionally male-dominated research topics, and this topical segregation has measurable downstream consequences for academic performance. Using bibliometric and funding data, the authors find that NSF-funded female PIs are more likely to concentrate in topics with lower citation impact, resulting in asymmetric career trajectory effects even after controlling for funding amounts. The study calls attention to structural sorting in research agenda formation as a source of gender inequality that aggregate success-rate statistics mask.

## 2. Merit or Networks? What Decides Where Research Is Published [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ning Li
**Date:** June 2, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03763
**Abstract:** This paper investigates whether placement in academic journals is determined by idea quality or by author social networks. The author evaluates 6,208 economics working papers using a large language model to score idea quality from text alone, and constructs connection indices capturing co-authorship and advising ties to editorial boards. The central finding is that "execution sets a meritocratic floor" — papers below a quality threshold do not place in top outlets regardless of connections — while "connections set a favoritism ceiling that bites mainly near the apex," where papers of similar quality by well-connected authors are more likely to land in top journals. The paper combines LLM-based quality measurement with traditional bibliometric data to generate causal estimates of each channel, providing novel empirical evidence on the determinants of publication success in economics.

## 3. Human Capital, AI, and Labor Commoditization [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Auyon Siddiq and Niuniu Zhang
**Date:** June 20, 2026 (revised June 30, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21880
**Abstract:** This paper asks whether generative AI has changed the way online labor markets reward human capital. The authors use data from Upwork, embedding worker profile text to construct measures of human capital richness, and implement a difference-in-differences design around the launch of ChatGPT in late 2022. In more AI-exposed job categories, the estimated return to observable human capital fell significantly while the price sensitivity of hiring decisions rose — a "commoditization" pattern. Workers with strong credentials and portfolio depth saw their demand premia erode in exposed categories, while buyers shifted toward lower-priced alternatives. The paper has direct implications for how workers should invest in skills and how platform designers should respond to AI-driven shifts in labor demand.

## 4. Generative AI and the Reorganization of Labor Demand [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Fangyan Wang, Zaiyan Wei, and Yang Wang
**Date:** May 22, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23159
**Abstract:** Using a nationwide dataset of U.S. job postings and a large language model pipeline to measure occupational AI exposure at the task level, this paper studies how firms reorganize labor demand as generative AI diffuses. Total AI-exposed hiring declined, but the adjustment occurred through two distinct margins: hiring reallocation across job titles (accounting for 52% of the aggregate exposure decline on average) and within-job redesign that strips AI-exposed tasks from existing positions (39.5%). The two mechanisms differ by seniority — senior roles adjust primarily through reallocation while junior roles exhibit more varied patterns across both channels. The results suggest that AI adoption reshapes job content and hiring composition simultaneously rather than displacing whole occupations uniformly.

*Note: Submitted May 22, 2026 — 19 days before the strict 30-day window. Included as a strong recent empirical paper on generative AI and labor demand.*

## 5. Global Science Sustains U.S. Innovation [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Christopher R. Esposito
**Date:** May 28, 2026 (revised June 1, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30435
**Abstract:** This paper traces how international scientific knowledge flows sustain American technological innovation. The author links NSF-funded research to downstream patents across multiple citation generations, mapping the global supply chain of scientific ideas that ultimately reaches U.S. commercial applications. Using a counterfactual design that models barriers to cross-border knowledge movement, the paper finds that such frictions reduce network connectivity, lengthen knowledge pathways, and diminish innovation output in strategically important sectors including semiconductors, quantum science, and artificial intelligence. The findings provide novel empirical evidence on the returns to open international science: restricting foreign scientific inputs imposes measurable costs on U.S. innovation capacity in precisely the domains where maintaining technological leadership is most contested.

*Note: Revised June 1, 2026 — 9 days before the strict 30-day window. Included as the strongest available Economics of Science paper on knowledge spillovers in this period.*
