## 1. Contemporary AI Lacks the Imagination to Diverge or Negate in Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Honglin Bao, Siyang Wu, Xiao Liu, Sida Li, Shiyun Cao, James A. Evans
**Date:** June 6, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08251
**Abstract:** We conduct a large-scale evaluation involving 121,640 preprint authors across multiple scientific disciplines, who rated AI-generated scientific ideas on novelty, feasibility, and likelihood of validity. We find that non-reasoning large language models collapse into a narrow "hivemind," converging on a small set of conventional hypotheses, while reasoning models explore broader hypothesis spaces — yet neither class of models spontaneously generates null hypotheses or ideas that contradict mainstream assumptions. The performance gap is domain-specific: LLMs underperform most severely in pluralistic social science fields requiring contextual interpretation and disciplinary humility. We further show that current automated evaluation benchmarks exhibit weak alignment with expert scientist judgment, though a custom reward model we develop substantially outperforms existing approaches. Our findings imply that contemporary AI tools for science require meaningful human partnership rather than autonomous operation, particularly for abductive and negation-rich reasoning tasks.

## 2. Revisiting the ABCs of Working with AI: A Replication with Radiologists [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Daniel Martin
**Date:** June 10, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12585
**Abstract:** I replicate and extend prior findings on ability-belief calibration (ABCs) as predictors of gains from AI assistance, using data from 68 radiologists analyzing chest X-rays across more than 11,000 paired observations. Consistent with the original study, I find that lower baseline diagnostic ability and higher calibration predict larger incremental value from AI support: the two characteristics together explain meaningful heterogeneity in who benefits from AI augmentation. The replication extends these results to a high-stakes professional domain where AI tools are already in clinical deployment, suggesting the ability-calibration framework generalizes beyond the original task-work settings. Implications are discussed for optimal targeting of AI assistance and for understanding distributional labor-market effects of AI adoption within occupations.

## 3. Directional AI Advice: Experimental Evidence from Healthcare [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Yuyu Chen, Hongbin Li, Lingsheng Meng, Xinyao Qiu, Qingxu Yang
**Date:** July 9, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08706
**Abstract:** We use a preregistered randomized field experiment at a large Chinese hospital to study how a deployed generative AI chatbot shapes physician prescribing decisions. We find that the chatbot routinely cautions against medication use — especially Traditional Chinese Medicine and antibiotics — while issuing favorable recommendations for diagnostic testing. This directional bias, driven by liability-conscious design choices embedded at deployment, significantly reduces prescription rates and increases diagnostic test ordering among physicians most responsive to patient input. Survey evidence further reveals that access to the AI chatbot lowered patient compliance and satisfaction, while reshaping the authority gradient between patients and physicians. Our findings illustrate how seemingly technical AI design choices propagate into health-market outcomes and professional labor dynamics, with consequences that may diverge sharply from social welfare objectives.

## 4. The Geography of Private Sector Agricultural Innovation in the USA: Evidence from Patents [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Matt Clancy
**Date:** July 6, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04956
**Abstract:** I develop novel yearly estimates of private sector agricultural R&D investment by US state from 1976 to 2014, using geographic distributions of patent inventors across five agricultural subsectors to allocate national R&D spending across all fifty states. Three main findings emerge. First, private agricultural R&D is strongly correlated with each state's agricultural economy size, both cross-sectionally and over time. Second, state-level R&D is highly persistent: past investment predicts future investment with remarkable stability. Third, the geographic concentration of private agricultural R&D has declined since 1976, though recent patent-based indicators suggest this deconcentration trend may be reversing. These estimates fill a long-standing gap in sub-national R&D data and provide new inputs for studying the productivity returns to private agricultural research and the spatial diffusion of innovation in agrifood value chains.

## 5. Robots and the Public Finance of Disability Insurance [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Duha T. Altindag, Reem El Cheikh Taha, John M. Nunley, R. Alan Seals
**Date:** July 3, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02892
**Abstract:** We examine how exposure to industrial robots affects Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) application rates using commuting-zone data for the United States and a shift-share instrumental variable design. We find that one additional robot per 1,000 workers reduces SSDI applications by approximately 8 per 100,000 residents, with particularly pronounced effects among older workers near retirement age. Importantly, total employment levels remain stable in high-robot-exposure areas, suggesting the effect operates through labor-market tightening rather than displacement. Averted applications translate into roughly $3.4 billion in expected avoided future disability and Medicare obligations annually, implying a meaningful fiscal dividend from automation-driven labor-market attachment. Our results contribute to the broader debate over how automation reshapes both worker outcomes and the long-run solvency of public insurance programs.
