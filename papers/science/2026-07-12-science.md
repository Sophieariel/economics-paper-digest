# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-07-12

## 1. Immigration, Innovation, and the Geography of Growth [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Costas Arkolakis, Sun Kyoung Lee, and Michael Peters
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35392
**Abstract:** Between 1880 and 1920, more than 20 million immigrants settled in the United States. The authors study how this migration wave affected innovation and growth using a newly developed dataset linking individual census records to historical immigration records and the universe of U.S. patents. They develop a spatial growth model and find that immigrants disproportionately concentrated in urban innovation hubs, with their skill composition and geographic clustering serving as key drivers of economic effects. Immigration arrivals after 1880 raised U.S. income per capita by 8.2% by 1940. Counterfactual analysis indicates that removing the 1920s immigration restrictions would have further increased income per capita by 1.7% by 2000. The findings highlight multi-channel effects of immigration on innovation networks and long-run economic performance.

## 2. A Double Bind: Gendered Funding, Research Topics, and Academic Performance in the Social Sciences [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Yang Ding, Ning Zhang, Helen Bao, Yu Jin, Jiang Wu, Lianlian Wu, Norman Weitemeier, Meng Huang, Alejandro Otazu Solorzano, Ana Paula Pineda Iriarte, Yunfeng Gao, Lok Man Michelle Tong, Nancy Mukalayi, Pengfei Yin, Shuyu Hu, Yuxuan Xiao, Yarong Song, Jiajing Xu, Chenxu Li, and Yi Bu
**Date:** June 2026 (arXiv:2606.03742, revised June 15, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03742
**Abstract:** This study examines 12,945 National Science Foundation (NSF)-funded principal investigators in the social sciences from 2000 to 2019 to assess gender disparities in funding allocation, research topic selection, and academic performance. Despite comparable funding rates across genders, women remain concentrated in fewer research domains while men dominate technology- and methodology-focused fields. The study finds that postdoctoral experience is associated with lower publication and citation performance for women working in male-dominated topic areas, but yields positive outcomes for women in female-dominated areas. These patterns constitute a "double bind": women face structural constraints on which topics they can credibly enter and receive disproportionately lower returns to career investments in male-dominated domains. The findings have implications for the design of NSF funding mechanisms and career-development programs targeting underrepresented groups in science.

## 3. Innovation without Borders? The Geography of Technological Diffusion [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ursel Baumann, Zoë B. Cullen, Ester Faia, Annalisa Ferrando, Ricardo Perez-Truglia, and Judit Rariga
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35314
**Abstract:** This paper uses a large-scale field experiment with 3,300 firms across twelve EU countries to study how innovation—specifically AI investment—spreads across geographic boundaries. Surveyed firms substantially underestimated competitor AI adoption rates, both domestically and internationally. Receiving accurate information about peer adoption significantly increased firms' expected AI investment rates (p < 0.001). Strong domestic strategic complementarities in AI adoption are documented: a 1 percentage-point increase in the expected share of domestic peers investing in AI raises a firm's own expected AI investment rate by 0.570 percentage points. By contrast, no such cross-border complementarities are found, indicating that domestic innovation diffusion substantially exceeds cross-border diffusion. The results highlight information frictions and peer effects as key drivers of AI adoption patterns within countries, and suggest that coordinated policy to correct beliefs about peer behavior could meaningfully accelerate domestic AI diffusion.

## 4. AI Diffusion Gaps: Unequal Integration of AI Across K-12 Schools [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Christopher Campos and John D. Singleton
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35347
**Abstract:** This paper documents how generative AI tools have been adopted in K-12 schools using a national survey of school principals. AI adoption has expanded rapidly as a productivity tool: students primarily use it for homework and writing assistance while educators leverage it for lesson planning and administrative tasks. Two significant adoption disparities emerge. First, schools serving higher concentrations of disadvantaged students show notably lower AI integration—a one standard deviation increase in disadvantage is associated with a 0.07–0.11 standard deviation lower score on the authors' AI integration index. Second, private and charter schools substantially underperform traditional public schools on integration measures. Teacher training, institutional guidance, and district-level policy have lagged behind adoption rates. District size explains approximately one-third of disparities among public schools. The factors predicting greater AI integration differ markedly from those needed to address equity concerns, suggesting that the current trajectory of diffusion risks widening educational inequality.

## 5. Empowering Inclusive Work [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Yanyou Chen, Mitchell Hoffman, Huilan Xu, and Zhe Yuan
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35372
**Abstract:** Can AI improve workplace outcomes for workers with disabilities? This paper examines the relative performance of deaf or hard of hearing (DHH) workers on one of China's largest food-delivery platforms using a difference-in-differences design around the midpoint introduction of an AI-powered intelligent outbound calling system. Prior to AI implementation, DHH workers demonstrated lower speed and customer ratings compared to non-disabled colleagues, yet displayed greater platform engagement and lower turnover. Following the AI tool's introduction, researchers find significant improvements: enhanced productivity on customer-facing tasks, reduced negative customer feedback, increased labor supply, improved retention, and elimination of approximately one-third of the disability-related hourly pay gap. The findings suggest that AI can meaningfully enhance workplace inclusion and reduce earnings disparities for workers with disabilities, with potential implications for labor market design and disability policy.
