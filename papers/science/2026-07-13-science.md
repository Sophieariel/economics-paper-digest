## 1. Merit or Networks? What Decides Where Research Is Published [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ning Li
**Date:** June 2, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03763
**Abstract:** The study investigates whether scientific publishing prioritizes research quality or author connections, using economics journals as a test case. The researcher develops a novel approach employing a discipline-trained large language model evaluator that scores paper ideas without seeing author names or outcomes. The analysis covers 6,208 economics working papers across five dimensions: execution quality, idea quality, professional connections, author ability, and LLM scores. Key findings show that execution quality establishes a baseline standard for publication, idea quality differentiates placements at mid-tier journals, while professional connections primarily influence acceptance at the most selective journals. The research concludes that merit and networking both matter, with their effects operating at different levels of the journal prestige hierarchy.

## 2. How Artificial Intelligence Shapes Science: Evidence from AlphaFold [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ryan R. Hill and Carolyn Stein
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35143
**Abstract:** The researchers investigate how AlphaFold2's release in July 2021 affected structural biology research. Despite scientists gaining access to hundreds of thousands of AI-predicted protein structures virtually overnight, experimental structure determination rates remained largely unchanged. Scientists instead used the AI predictions to facilitate and complement experimental work rather than replace it. Basic research on previously unstudied proteins increased by 15–40% in the aftermath of AlphaFold2's release. However, the authors find no evidence of increased early-stage drug development targeting these newly accessible proteins, though they note this effect may emerge with a longer lag. The findings speak to how AI tools augment scientific inquiry rather than simply automating it, and raise questions about the translation of basic science advances into applied innovation.

## 3. Human Capital, AI, and Labor Commoditization [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Auyon Siddiq and Niuniu Zhang
**Date:** June 20, 2026 (revised June 30, 2026)
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21880
**Abstract:** The paper examines whether generative AI has changed how labor markets value human capital, using contract-level data from Upwork, a large online labor market. The researchers employ text embeddings to represent worker profiles and measure how AI exposure affects the valuation of worker skills and credentials across job categories. They find that the importance of human capital declines and the importance of price rises in AI-exposed job categories following the widespread availability of generative AI tools. The demand premium for workers with strong human capital falls in AI-exposed categories, with demand reallocating toward lower-priced workers. The results suggest a "commoditization" of labor in AI-exposed markets, with implications for wage inequality and returns to education.

## 4. AI Adoption in S&P 500 Firms [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Yang Yu, Martin Fleming, Lucy Hampton, Christophe Combemale, and Neil Thompson
**Date:** July 9, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08920
**Abstract:** The research examines how large U.S. enterprises integrated AI into their operations between 2016 and 2025, using a novel measurement approach based on SEC 10-K annual filings to distinguish genuine operational AI adoption from promotional references. Key findings show that by 2025, 11% of S&P 500 companies had AI deeply integrated into their business processes, with an additional 10% using AI in production and service delivery. Adoption quadrupled since 2022, driven primarily by technology firms, which represent two-thirds of deep integration cases. The study documents a "J-curve" pattern in profitability as firms transition from no adoption to deep adoption of AI, though the authors observe no significant differences in capital expenditure or measured productivity across adoption stages, suggesting that productivity gains from AI integration may take longer to materialize in aggregate financial data.

## 5. Generative AI and the Reorganization of Labor Demand [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Fangyan Wang, Zaiyan Wei, and Yang Wang
**Date:** May 22, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23159
**Abstract:** The paper examines how firms reorganize labor demand as generative AI technology diffuses across the economy, rather than focusing solely on which occupations are exposed to AI or whether exposed jobs decline in number. The study uses a nationwide dataset of U.S. job postings across all sectors, constructing a dynamic, posting-level measure of generative AI exposure through a two-stage large language model pipeline that identifies tasks described in each posting and classifies the extent to which generative AI can perform or assist them. The authors then decompose changes in aggregate exposure into two margins: reallocation of demand across jobs and redesign of tasks within jobs. Key findings include: generative AI exposure is dynamic rather than fixed, changing substantially over time; hiring reallocation explains the largest share of aggregate exposure decline at 52% on average, while within-job task redesign accounts for 39.5%; and senior positions adjust earlier through reallocation, whereas junior roles adjust through a combination of reallocation and redesign. The findings suggest that organizational reconfiguration—reshaping both hiring priorities and job task structures—is the primary channel through which labor markets adjust to generative AI adoption.
