# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-07-14

## 1. AI Premium [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Nicola Borri, Yukun Liu, and Aleh Tsyvinski
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35451
**Abstract:** This research examines how artificial intelligence affects financial markets, firms, and workers using 380 trillion tokens from over 400 large language models. The authors construct an AI Factor and calculate firm-level AI Betas to measure market exposure to AI developments. Key findings include that firms with higher positive covariance to the AI factor generate substantial returns, with a value-weighted long-short strategy earning 64.1 basis points per week. The premium is strongest among intensive, frontier-focused AI consumption patterns but weaker for casual users. AI exposure extends beyond tech sectors into consumer and capital-intensive industries, though is notably absent in emerging markets including China. Occupational analysis reveals positive AI exposure correlates with interaction-and-communication task content, while negative exposure associates with analytical and scientific work. The research also provides early evidence of the rise of the agentic economy.

## 2. Before the Exodus? Young Scientists and the Future of US Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Pierre Azoulay, Raffaella Sadun, and Daniela Scur
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35330
**Abstract:** Following policy changes to US science funding in early 2025, the researchers surveyed 916 early-career biomedical scientists—PhD students and postdoctoral researchers—about their career plans and expectations. Key findings reveal significant shifts in sentiment: barely half of respondents now say they are likely to remain in academia, down 22 percentage points from six months earlier. Intentions to stay in the United States dropped 21 percentage points, and satisfaction with pursuing a PhD in science fell 16 percentage points. The authors validate survey responses against contemporaneous administrative records. They emphasize these concerns come from the next generation of potential principal investigators rather than established scientists protecting existing funding.

## 3. Human Capital, AI, and Labor Commoditization [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Auyon Siddiq and Niuniu Zhang
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21880
**Abstract:** The researchers investigate whether generative AI has altered how online labor markets compensate workers for their skills and expertise. Using Upwork contract data, they employ text embeddings to analyze worker profiles and measure how human capital information influences client demand relative to pricing. Their analysis examines changes following ChatGPT's release through a difference-in-differences methodology. Key findings indicate that in job categories more susceptible to AI disruption, the importance of human capital declines and the importance of price rises, suggesting labor commoditization. Workers with strong qualifications experienced diminished demand premiums in AI-exposed sectors, with demand shifting toward lower-cost workers. The authors conclude these patterns have significant implications for labor market platform design, worker incentives to invest in human capital, and overall worker welfare.

## 4. How Artificial Intelligence Shapes Science: Evidence from AlphaFold [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ryan R. Hill and Carolyn Stein
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35143
**Abstract:** This research examines how AlphaFold2's release in July 2021 influenced structural biology and adjacent scientific fields. The study reveals that despite gaining access to hundreds of thousands of AI-predicted protein structures, researchers maintained their experimental structure-solving rates rather than abandoning laboratory work. Instead, scientists leveraged these predictions to enhance their existing methodologies. The findings indicate a noteworthy shift in research priorities: basic research on proteins that had no structure information prior to AlphaFold increases by 15 to 40% relative to proteins that already had a structure, redirecting scientific focus toward previously understudied proteins. However, the investigation found minimal evidence that pharmaceutical development activities have begun targeting these newly accessible proteins, though such developments may occur subsequently.

## 5. Artificial Intelligence, Productivity, and the Workforce: Evidence from Corporate Executives [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Salomé Baslandze, Zachary Edwards, John Graham, Ty McClure, Brent H. Meyer, Michael Sparks, Sonya R. Waddell, and Daniel Weitz
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34984
**Abstract:** The researchers surveyed approximately 750 corporate executives to examine AI's impact on business productivity and employment. Key findings include significant variation in AI adoption across companies, with over 50% already investing. Labor productivity gains are positive, vary across sectors, and are expected to strengthen in 2026, with the largest effects concentrated in high-skill services and finance. These gains reflect increases in revenue-based total factor productivity associated with innovation and demand-oriented channels rather than capital deepening. The authors identify a productivity paradox in which perceived productivity gains exceed measured gains, likely reflecting delayed revenue realization. The study documents little evidence of near-term aggregate employment declines, though larger firms anticipate workforce reductions while smaller firms expect modest hiring. An index ranks job functions most vulnerable to AI displacement.
