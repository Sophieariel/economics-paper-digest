# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-07-15

## 1. Randomized Experiments in the Science of Science: The Case of Peer Review and the Evaluation of New Knowledge [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Kevin J. Boudreau
**Date:** January 5, 2026
**Source:** https://ssrn.com/abstract=6025114
**Abstract:** This chapter synthesizes empirical evidence from randomized controlled trials and other field experiments bearing on peer review and the evaluation of new scientific knowledge. Boudreau organizes findings through a unified framework and documents a sharp empirical asymmetry: most system-level interventions—changes to review process, timing, anonymity, and compliance mechanisms—reliably shift participation and behavioral margins while leaving core evaluative judgments largely intact. Core assessments respond primarily to who evaluates the work and their domain expertise. The evidence also reveals systematic differences between two distinct peer review tasks: fine-grained rank-ordering among acceptable submissions and filtering of clearly unacceptable work. The chapter draws implications for which aspects of peer review are tractable targets for institutional reform and which appear tightly constrained by the epistemic demands of evaluation at the knowledge frontier.

## 2. AI in Science [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ajay K. Agrawal, John McHale, and Alexander Oettl
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34953
**Abstract:** This paper examines how artificial intelligence affects scientific knowledge production by characterizing AI as a tool for augmentation—enhancing search over combinatorial spaces—rather than full automation. The authors apply a task-based framework to the multistage knowledge-creation process to explain the "jagged frontier" of AI in science: different scientific domains and workflow stages benefit unevenly from AI tools. AlphaFold provides strong design support in data-rich biology, while question-generation AI tools yield subtler gains in anomaly-sparse physics, where human abductive reasoning remains essential. Using a task-based model distinguishing ordinary from AI-expert scientists, the authors demonstrate that AI productivity improvements generate nonlinear gains proportional to the share of AI-expert scientists in a field, highlighting skills training and organizational design as critical complementary investments. The analysis offers a unified account of heterogeneous AI adoption across scientific disciplines and stages of research.

## 3. AI Adoption in S&P 500 Firms [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Yang Yu, Martin Fleming, Lucy Hampton, Christophe Combemale, and Neil Thompson
**Date:** July 9, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08920
**Abstract:** This paper constructs a novel measure of deep AI adoption using mandatory 10-K annual report disclosures filed with the SEC by S&P 500 firms from 2016 through 2025. Because securities laws prohibit materially false or misleading statements, the authors argue that 10-K language provides a more reliable signal of genuine operational integration than survey self-reports. In 2025, 11% of S&P 500 firms had deeply integrated AI into their business operations, with an additional 10% using AI in production and service delivery—a fourfold increase from 5% in 2022. The technology sector accounts for two-thirds of deep integration. Profitability follows a J-curve pattern across the adoption lifecycle, with early integration associated with short-term performance dips before gains materialize. Larger firms and those with higher Tobin's q are more likely to be early and deep adopters. The paper finds no measurable average differences in capital expenditure or productivity at the current stage of adoption, consistent with an implementation lag before productivity gains appear.

## 4. Firm Data on AI [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Ivan Yotzov, Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, Philip Bunn, Steven J. Davis, Kevin M. Foster, Aaron Jalca, Brent H. Meyer, Paul Mizen, Michael A. Navarrete, Pawel Smietanka, Gregory Thwaites, and Ben Zhe Wang
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836
**Abstract:** This paper surveys nearly 6,000 senior business executives at firms in the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, and Australia to develop new evidence on AI adoption and its effects on jobs, productivity, and output. Sixty-nine percent of surveyed firms actively use AI, with higher adoption rates among younger and more productive firms. While over two-thirds of executives personally use AI, their average engagement is approximately 1.5 hours weekly. Looking backward over the past three years, executives report little own-firm impact, with nine-in-ten indicating no effect on employment or productivity. Looking forward three years, however, these same executives project substantial impacts: productivity gains of 1.4%, output increases of 0.8%, and employment reductions of 0.7%. Employees hold different expectations, forecasting a 0.5% employment increase at their own firms over the coming three years—revealing a significant expectations gap between leadership and workforce. The international comparison reveals notable cross-country heterogeneity in adoption rates, motivations, and expected outcomes.

*Note: Published February 2026; outside the strict 30-day window.*

## 5. AI Premium [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Nicola Borri, Yukun Liu, and Aleh Tsyvinski
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35451
**Abstract:** This paper constructs a novel measure of firm-level AI exposure using 380 trillion tokens of large language model API consumption data from OpenRouter spanning over 400 models. The authors estimate firm-level AI Betas capturing each firm's sensitivity to AI technology shocks and document a significant AI Premium in equity markets: high AI-beta firms earn 64.1 basis points per week more than low AI-beta firms in a value-weighted long-short strategy. The premium is robust to standard risk factors and is concentrated among firms engaging with frontier-oriented AI models rather than casual or open-weight use. Benefits of AI adoption extend beyond the technology sector into consumer-facing and capital-intensive industries, but are notably absent in emerging markets including China. At the occupational level, roles emphasizing interaction-and-communication tasks show a 0.36-standard-deviation higher AI exposure. The results offer early empirical evidence of an emerging agentic economy in which AI becomes a priced factor of production.
