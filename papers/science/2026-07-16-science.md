# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-07-16

## 1. AI Premium [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Nicola Borri, Aleh Tsyvinski, and Yukun Liu
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35451
**Abstract:** The researchers analyzed 380 trillion tokens across more than 400 large language models to construct an "AI Factor" derived from growth in tokens processed, dollars of compute, and active users. They find that firms whose returns covary more positively with the AI factor—high AI beta firms—earn higher subsequent returns, with a value-weighted strategy earning 64.1 basis points weekly. The premium is concentrated in frontier AI consumption and extends across sectors well beyond technology. Differential occupational impacts emerge: positions emphasizing interaction-and-communication content show positive AI exposure, while analytical and operations-control roles display negative exposure, pointing to redistributive wage dynamics as AI diffuses.

## 2. The Shift to Agentic AI: Evidence from Codex [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Drew Johnston, David Holtz, Alex Martin Richmond, Christopher Ong, Prasanna Tambe, and Aaron Chatterji
**Date:** June 25, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26959
**Abstract:** Using granular usage data from OpenAI's Codex platform, the authors document the early-2026 transition from chat-based to agentic AI workflows. Active users increased more than fivefold during the first half of 2026, expanding well beyond software developers. Within OpenAI, Codex adoption is nearly universal and has largely displaced ChatGPT for business functions; over 10% of users manage multiple concurrent agents weekly. Task complexity surged sharply—users submitting tasks requiring eight or more hours of professional work increased nearly tenfold since the start of the year. The median OpenAI employee in a legal role generated 13 times more monthly output tokens by mid-2026 than in November 2025. The paper draws out implications for measured productivity, organizational restructuring, and shifting labor demand across skill levels.

## 3. The Broken Ladder: AI, Remote Work, and Early-Career Hiring [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Peter John Lambert and Yannick Schindler
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6787638
**Abstract:** The paper asks whether generative AI is displacing junior workers, using two data sources spanning 243 million new hires and 407 million online job postings collected across the US, UK, Canada, and Australia from 2017 to 2025. Difference-in-differences designs are estimated at the occupation, region, and firm level. When estimated separately, a two-standard-deviation increase in either GenAI or remote-work (WFH) exposure each predicts a roughly 5-percentage-point fall in the junior share of new hires by 2025. However, when estimated jointly, the WFH effect remains large and significant while the GenAI coefficient attenuates sharply and is often indistinguishable from zero. The authors identify a classic omitted-variable problem: white-collar, routine-cognitive occupations are the most GenAI-exposed precisely because they also shifted most heavily to remote work. The verdict that AI is replacing junior workers is, they argue, premature.

## 4. Tax Incentives and Venture Capital Risk-Taking: Evidence from the QSBS Program [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Murillo Campello and Guilherme Junqueira
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35418
**Abstract:** The authors examine whether capital-gains tax subsidies shift the risk profile of venture investment using the Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS) program as a quasi-experiment. Drawing on data from 158,000 investor–firm pairs over two decades, they find that VCs shift measurably toward riskier ventures when the subsidy applies—investing more in early-stage startups and those with existing debt burdens. These ventures exhibit higher failure rates but also substantially greater valuations and unicorn attainment. Patenting and innovation outcomes are also higher in QSBS-eligible portfolios. The findings demonstrate that tax policy can durably redirect entrepreneurial financing toward riskier, more innovative, and ultimately more valuable startups, with implications for the design of public instruments to spur private R&D investment.

## 5. Field Experiments in the Science of Science: Lessons from Peer Review and the Evaluation of New Knowledge [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Kevin Boudreau
**Date:** February 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34811
**Abstract:** This paper synthesizes evidence from randomized controlled trials embedded in peer-review systems at journals, academic conferences, and grant-funding agencies. Using a Q–A–R–S framework that jointly considers submission qualities, author characteristics, reviewer attributes, and evaluation-system design, Boudreau finds that system-level interventions often alter participant behavior while leaving core evaluative judgments largely unchanged. Peer review functions more reliably as a filter for poor submissions than as a precise ranker of acceptable work. The paper identifies peer review as a "functioning institution operating under binding epistemic and organizational constraints" and maps potential levers—reviewer selection, structured evaluation instruments, and phased review—to improve speed, cost-effectiveness, and reliability in scientific knowledge evaluation.
