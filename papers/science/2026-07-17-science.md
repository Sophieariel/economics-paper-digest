## 1. AI Premium [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Nicola Borri, Aleh Tsyvinski, Yukun Liu
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35451
**Abstract:** This paper examines how artificial intelligence affects financial markets, firms, and workers using an extensive dataset of AI consumption. The authors construct an AI Factor and calculate firm-level AI Betas to measure how stock returns co-vary with AI adoption patterns. A value-weighted long-short strategy on firms' AI Betas earns 64.1 basis points per week. The AI premium is particularly strong for closed-source models, paying and seasoned users, and long prompts, but weaker for casual or open-weight usage. Benefits extend across sectors beyond technology into consumer-facing and capital-intensive industries, but are notably absent in emerging markets and China. Occupation-level analysis reveals positive AI exposure in nonroutine interactive work but negative exposure in analytical and operations-control roles. The study also provides preliminary evidence on the structure of the emerging agentic economy.

## 2. Task-Specific Technical Change and Comparative Advantage [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Lukas Althoff, Hugo Reichardt
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35353
**Abstract:** This paper examines how artificial intelligence reshapes workers' comparative advantage by modifying tasks and their skill requirements. The researchers construct a dynamic task-based model in which workers with multidimensional skills select occupations and develop expertise through employment. They develop an efficient estimation procedure using panel data and a novel database of task-level skill requirements. Across various AI implementation scenarios — from gradual to accelerated adoption — the analysis finds that wage inequality narrows and average wages increase. The primary mechanism is task simplification: by lowering tasks' skill requirements, AI allows lower-skill workers to compete for previously inaccessible jobs. While adoption costs create obstacles, particularly for lower-skill workers, these expenses do not fully counteract the inequality reduction observed in the model's predictions.

## 3. Writing Code vs. Shipping Code: Productivity Effects Across Generations of AI Coding Tools [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Mert Demirer, Leon Musolff, Liyuan Yang
**Date:** May 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35275
**Abstract:** This paper examines how productivity improvements from AI coding tools evolve through successive generations and whether task-level gains translate into final software output. Using data from over 100,000 GitHub developers and a matched event study design, the authors analyze three generations of coding AI: autocomplete tools, interactive coding agents, and autonomous coding agents. These generated cumulative productivity increases of 40%, 140%, and 180% respectively in coding activity. However, gains diminished substantially at higher production stages — declining to 50% for project count and 30% for actual software releases — a pattern the authors attribute to human bottlenecks in the production chain. Testing across four major app marketplaces reveals moderate increases in new applications but no corresponding growth in total usage, suggesting that broader adoption constraints dampen the downstream impact of task-level AI productivity gains.

## 4. Attention (And Money) Is All You Need: Why Universities Are Struggling to Keep AI Talent [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ufuk Akcigit, Craig A. Chikis, Emin Dinlersoz, Nathan Goldschlag
**Date:** March 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w34964
**Abstract:** This paper tracks approximately 42,000 artificial intelligence professionals over twenty years using linked academic publication and employment census data. The findings reveal a significant and accelerating shift in where AI talent concentrates: industry increasingly attracts younger and foreign-born researchers, while gender representation improves more in academia. A striking compensation disparity emerges, with top-tier industrial scientists earning substantially more than their university counterparts. Researchers transitioning to industry alter their output patterns markedly — producing fewer publications but generating more patents — suggesting a shift from open science toward proprietary innovation. The paper raises concerns about the implications of talent concentration in private industry for the direction of AI research, the openness of the scientific knowledge base, and the long-run capacity of universities to train the next generation of AI researchers.

## 5. How Artificial Intelligence Shapes Science: Evidence from AlphaFold [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Ryan R. Hill, Carolyn Stein
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35143
**Abstract:** This paper examines how AlphaFold2's release in July 2021 impacted structural biology research, using the sudden availability of hundreds of thousands of AI-predicted protein structures as a natural experiment. Despite researchers gaining access to this vast repository virtually overnight, experimental protein structure determination rates remained stable: scientists leveraged predictions to support traditional experimental work rather than replace it. The findings reveal a 15–40% increase in basic research on previously unstudied proteins — expanding the scientific frontier — yet show minimal early-stage pharmaceutical development targeting these newly accessible proteins at this time. The results suggest that frontier AI tools function as complements to human scientific effort rather than substitutes, at least in the near term, and that access to AI-generated predictions redirects rather than reduces the demand for experimental scientific labor.
