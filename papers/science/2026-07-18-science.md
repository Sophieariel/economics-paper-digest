# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-07-18

## 1. AI Premium [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Nicola Borri, Aleh Tsyvinski, and Yukun Liu
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35451
**Abstract:** Using data on 380 trillion tokens consumed across over 400 large language models, the authors construct a firm-level AI Beta based on stock return exposure to an AI Factor derived from real-world AI consumption patterns. A value-weighted long-short strategy based on AI factor exposure yields 64.1 basis points per week. The AI premium is not confined to the technology sector—it extends into consumer and capital-intensive industries—and is notably absent in emerging markets, particularly China. Occupational analysis shows that workers in nonroutine interactive roles exhibit positive AI exposure, while those in analytical and operations-control occupations show negative exposure. The authors document early evidence of an emerging agentic economy in which AI systems act autonomously on behalf of firms and individuals. The study represents the first large-scale empirical analysis linking actual AI consumption data to asset prices and labor market outcomes.

## 2. Task-Specific Technical Change and Comparative Advantage [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Lukas Althoff and Hugo Reichardt
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35353
**Abstract:** This paper develops a dynamic model in which workers choose occupations and accumulate skills over time, with AI changing the task requirements within occupations. The authors estimate the model using panel data on workers matched with new task-skill requirement information, allowing them to trace how AI adoption reshapes career trajectories across different ability levels. A central finding is that AI narrows wage inequality and raises average wages by simplifying tasks, enabling workers with lower baseline skills to perform jobs that previously required higher skill levels. Although adoption costs impose greater barriers on lower-skill workers in the near term, these frictions do not reverse the inequality-reducing tendency of AI-driven task simplification. The paper contributes to the task-based model literature by allowing AI to shift comparative advantage rather than uniformly automating or augmenting all workers.

## 3. The Jagged Global Economy: Frontier AI Unevenly Exposes National Economies [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Arul Murugan, Tomás Aguirre, Abhishek Nagaraj, and Rishi Bommasani
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05404
**Abstract:** The authors construct a national AI exposure index by combining occupation-level AI exposure scores with international employment data across 141 countries. High-income economies face substantially greater AI exposure than low-income ones; Europe and Central Asia are exposed at rates roughly 50 percent higher than Sub-Saharan Africa. A striking gender pattern emerges: women are more AI-exposed than men in 91 percent of countries, driven by their concentration in white-collar and sales occupations. The paper also identifies indirect exposure channels—exemplified by Tajikistan, whose remittance-dependent economy inherits AI exposure from migrant workers abroad. Estimates are validated against published AI adoption statistics from major AI companies. The authors conclude that policy frameworks built on U.S. or European labor market assumptions will not generalize to most of the world, and that a "jagged" global exposure landscape demands country-specific adjustment policies.

## 4. Translation Readiness Index: Measuring Patent-Paper Proximity from Scientific Publication Text [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Paul X. McCarthy, Rasika Amarasiri, and Xian Gong
**Date:** June 30, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31102
**Abstract:** The authors introduce the Translation Readiness Index (TRI), a text-based metric for identifying scientific publications with commercial potential before patents or startups emerge. Analyzing 20,610 publications via semantic embeddings and machine learning—with XGBoost achieving a 0.77 ROC-AUC score—the study finds that patent-paired publications use invention-oriented framing and forward-looking language, distinct from the observational language of comparison papers. External validation across universities confirms positive correlations between high TRI scores and independent commercialization indicators such as subsequent patents and spinouts. The TRI measures semantic proximity to patented science rather than realized commercialization, making it useful as an early-stage predictor of university-to-industry knowledge transfer. The paper contributes to the economics of science by offering a scalable, ex ante measure of research translatability that does not require observing downstream commercial outcomes.

## 5. The Geography of Private Sector Agricultural Innovation in the USA: Evidence from Patents [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Matt Clancy
**Date:** July 6, 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04956
**Abstract:** The author constructs estimates of annual private sector agricultural research and development at the U.S. state level from 1976 to 2014, using the geographic distribution of inventors on US patents to allocate national private-sector R&D figures across all 50 states and five agricultural subsectors. Three empirical patterns emerge: private-sector agricultural R&D is strongly correlated with state agricultural economy size; it is highly persistent over time, with states retaining their relative ranks for decades; and while R&D concentration declined substantially between 1976 and 2014, this trend shows recent signs of reversal. The newly assembled dataset enables researchers to study the local determinants and spillovers of private agricultural innovation at a spatial resolution previously unavailable, filling a gap in the empirics of R&D geography and knowledge production in the agricultural sector.
