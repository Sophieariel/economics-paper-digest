# Economics of Science & AI Papers — 2026-07-19

## 1. AI Premium [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Nicola Borri, Aleh Tsyvinski, and Yukun Liu
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35451
**Abstract:** The researchers examine how artificial intelligence affects firms, markets, and workers using 380 trillion tokens of realized AI consumption across more than 400 large language models from the OpenRouter dataset. The study constructs an AI Factor from observed consumption patterns and estimates firm-level AI Betas to characterize a novel AI Premium. A high-frequency AI factor shows that firms with higher AI beta earn greater subsequent returns, with a value-weighted long-short strategy earning 64.1 basis points per week. The premium concentrates among frontier AI consumption — closed-source models and paid users — rather than casual use. Benefits extend beyond tech into consumer and capital-intensive sectors but remain absent in emerging markets such as China. Occupational analysis reveals a 0.36-standard-deviation higher market-implied AI exposure in roles emphasizing interaction and communication, while analytical and scientific skills show negative exposure. The paper provides preliminary evidence on the emerging agentic economy.

## 2. Task-Specific Technical Change and Comparative Advantage [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Lukas Althoff and Hugo Reichardt
**Date:** June 2026
**Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w35353
**Abstract:** Artificial intelligence reshapes workers' comparative advantage by altering the tasks they perform and the skills those tasks require. The authors develop a dynamic task-based model to quantify the general-equilibrium effects of task-specific technical change. Workers have multidimensional skills, choose occupations, and accumulate skills on the job; occupations combine tasks, and productivity depends on how workers' skills match task requirements. A computationally efficient procedure estimates the model using panel data and a new database of task-level skill requirements. Applying the model to AI — allowing it to augment, automate, and simplify tasks — the paper finds that AI narrows wage inequality and raises average wages across scenarios ranging from slow to rapid AI progress. The key equalizing force is simplification: by lowering tasks' skill requirements, AI lets lower-skill workers compete for previously inaccessible jobs. Adoption costs, highest for lower-skill workers, dampen but do not eliminate the decline in inequality.

## 3. The Jagged Global Economy: Frontier AI Unevenly Exposes National Economies [Economics of AI]
**Authors:** Arul Murugan, Tomás Aguirre, Abhishek Nagaraj, and Rishi Bommasani
**Date:** June/July 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05404
**Abstract:** This research examines how frontier AI's labor-market impacts vary globally across 141 countries. The authors create a national AI exposure metric combining occupation-level AI vulnerability scores with employment data. Key findings reveal that high-income nations face substantially greater direct exposure than low-income counterparts, with Europe and Central Asia experiencing 50% more exposure than Sub-Saharan Africa. The study identifies a pronounced gender dimension: women are more exposed than men in 91% of countries, primarily due to their concentration in white-collar and sales roles, with notable exceptions where women's employment remains centered in agriculture. Beyond direct exposure, the research introduces an indirect mechanism through cross-country income dependencies — illustrated through Tajikistan, whose economy's heavy reliance on Russian remittances (37% of GDP) elevates its indirect exposure given Russia's high vulnerability. The authors conclude that national variation in exposure is large enough that policy responses calibrated to U.S. or European labor markets will not generalize.

## 4. The Geography of Private Sector Agricultural Innovation in the USA: Evidence from Patents [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Matt Clancy
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04956
**Abstract:** This study creates new annual estimates of private sector agricultural research and development across all 50 US states from 1976 to 2014. National private sector R&D figures are allocated among states using geographic data from patent inventors working in five major agricultural subsectors. The analysis reveals three patterns: private R&D spending closely tracks each state's agricultural economy size; spending is remarkably stable over time relative to the agricultural output of each state; and while geographic concentration decreased from 1976 to 2014, recent evidence suggests this trend may be reversing. The new state-level panel data enable future research on the returns to private agricultural R&D, knowledge spillovers across regions, and the relationship between public and private research investment in agriculture.

## 5. Does Multi-Agent Debate Improve AI Feedback on Research Papers? [Economics of Science]
**Authors:** Tomas Havranek and Zuzana Irsova
**Date:** July 2026
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14713
**Abstract:** The researchers conducted a pre-registered experiment with 44 meta-analysis authors in economics who ranked three AI-generated reports on their papers by usefulness. Authors preferred a single pass by a frontier model over two multi-agent debate tools by approximately 0.66 and 0.57 rank points, respectively. Notably, when authors recalled their actual journal referee reports, they ranked these highest among all options. However, when AI judges evaluated the reports, they ranked genuine referee feedback last — suggesting that AI self-evaluation of research quality diverges sharply from the preferences of human researchers. The results counsel caution against substituting AI judgment for human author assessment and have direct implications for proposals to use AI to augment or replace peer review in economics and related disciplines.
